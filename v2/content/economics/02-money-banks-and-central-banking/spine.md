# Money, banks, and central banking

**Period:** Ancient exchange to modern central banking

## The problem money solves

Exchange begins with a difficulty. If a shepherd wants shoes and the shoemaker wants meat, a trade may happen. If the shoemaker wants beer instead, the shepherd must find a third person who wants wool and has beer, or give up. This is the double coincidence of wants: both sides must want what the other has, at the same time, in the right amounts. Barter can work in a small village where everyone knows everyone, but it becomes clumsy as trade widens and the range of goods grows.

Money solves this problem by being generally accepted in exchange. A seller takes money not because it is wanted for itself but because it can be passed on. This lets sale and purchase separate: the shepherd can sell wool today for money and buy shoes next week from someone who has no use for wool. Aristotle described money as a device that arose from barter for exactly this reason, a portable and accepted medium that makes exchange easier.

Money performs several jobs at once. As a medium of exchange it settles transactions. As a unit of account it gives prices a common measure, so a coat and a bushel of grain can be compared. As a store of value it carries purchasing power from one time to another, though imperfectly when prices rise. As a standard of deferred payment it lets debts be written and repaid across time. A single item rarely performs all four jobs equally well, and the tension among them runs through the whole subject.

## What money is

Money is not a natural object so much as a social institution. Almost anything durable, divisible, and hard to counterfeit can serve: cattle, shells, salt, silver, gold, or a printed note. Commodity money carries its own value in the material, as a silver coin does. Representative money is a claim on a stored commodity. Fiat money has value because a government declares it legal tender and because people expect others to accept it. Modern money is mostly not cash at all but bank deposits recorded in ledgers.

Because money rests on acceptance, it rests on trust. A coin carries metal content and a stamp; a banknote carries a promise; a ledger entry carries institutional memory. When trust weakens, the institution is tested. Debasement, the practice of a ruler shrinking the metal in a coin while keeping its face value, is an ancient form of inflation: more coins chase the same goods, and prices rise. Counterfeiting, bank failure, and hyperinflation are later versions of the same problem. Aristotle already worried that money-making could detach from real need and be mistaken for wealth itself, since the token is only useful while others will take it.

## Credit and the price of money

Saving and lending extend exchange across time. A person who has more grain than he needs this year may lend it to a neighbour who has less, in return for repayment with something extra next year. The extra is interest, and interest is best understood as a price: the price paid for the use of money, or more precisely for the willingness to give up present command over resources. John Locke argued that interest is shaped by the same forces as other prices — the supply of funds from lenders, the demand from borrowers, and the risk that the loan will not be repaid. He warned that a legal ceiling set below the market rate would not make borrowing cheaper; it would reduce the number of people willing to lend, leaving some borrowers without funds and pushing others into evasion.

Interest also reflects time and uncertainty. A lender who parts with money for a year takes a risk and forgoes other uses, so the rate must cover both. Different borrowers pay different rates because their prospects differ: a government with taxing power borrows cheaply, a new venture borrows dearly, and a borrower who cannot offer security may not borrow at all. The rate of interest therefore carries information, much as other prices do, and it allocates a scarce resource — loanable funds — among competing uses.

## Banks and the creation of deposits

A bank is a business that takes in deposits and makes loans. Its balance sheet has liabilities, the deposits it owes to customers, and assets, the loans and securities it holds. The apparent simplicity hides two important features. First, a bank transforms maturity: it promises depositors they may withdraw on demand while lending their money out for years. Second, in a modern system, banks create deposits when they lend. When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit; the money supply expands. When the loan is repaid, the deposit is destroyed. Lending and money creation are two sides of one act.

This is why banks are not merely intermediaries passing existing savings from one person to another. They are the main source of the money that circulates. Their lending depends on the demand for credit, their capital, their assessment of risk, and the interest rate at which they can fund themselves. When they lend freely, spending and prices tend to rise; when they pull back, credit contracts and activity slows. A change in the willingness to lend can matter as much as a change in the central bank's policy rate.

## Fragility, runs, and panics

The same structure that makes banks useful makes them fragile. Because deposits can be withdrawn on demand but loans cannot be called in at once, a bank holds only a fraction of its liabilities in ready cash. If many depositors lose confidence at the same time, they will all try to withdraw, and the bank cannot pay them all. A run can destroy an otherwise sound institution, because the fear of failure can itself cause failure. Walter Bagehot described the London money market as concrete machinery, not mystery, and saw that the concentration of cash in a few institutions created both great power and great delicacy.

Runs can spread. A panic at one bank makes depositors question others, and the failure of one institution can freeze the credit that healthy firms depend on. Contagion is not a metaphor: when lenders are unsure who is solvent, they stop lending to everyone, and a shortage of liquidity becomes a shortage of credit, which becomes recession. This is why banking is one of the few industries in which a loss of confidence can bring down firms that are fundamentally sound.

## Central banks and the lender of last resort

Central banks grew up around this problem. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, gradually became the holder of the system's reserves; the Federal Reserve was created in 1913 after repeated panics. Their original purpose was not to fine-tune the economy but to prevent the money market from seizing. Bagehot gave the classic rule for a crisis: lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral. The point is to stop a panic by supplying cash to solvent institutions, while charging enough to discourage ordinary borrowing and demanding collateral so that the public purse is protected.

Central banks today have broader mandates. They conduct monetary policy, set the interest rate at which they lend, buy and sell securities, and in recent decades have used large-scale asset purchases, sometimes called quantitative easing. They supervise banks and act as the ultimate source of liquidity. Charles Goodhart argued that central banks were not designed all at once from theory but formed by repeated pressure, acquiring public responsibilities because they already held the system's cash. The institution is therefore historical as much as analytical, and its powers are the product of crises it was asked to contain.

## The 2008 crisis

The crisis of 2008 tested these arrangements. In the years before it, banks and shadow banks — institutions that lend like banks but are funded by short-term borrowing rather than deposits — had built a vast market in mortgage-backed securities. Loans were pooled, sliced, and sold, and the resulting instruments were widely held and often poorly understood. When house prices fell and borrowers defaulted, the value of these securities became uncertain, and the short-term funding that shadow banks relied on dried up. The failure of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 turned uncertainty into panic.

The response was extraordinary by the standards of the previous half-century. Central banks lent on a scale Bagehot might have recognised, opening facilities to a wide range of institutions and buying securities to keep credit flowing. Governments recapitalised banks and guaranteed deposits and funding. Interest rates were cut toward zero, and when that was not enough, central banks bought large quantities of government and mortgage debt to push longer-term rates down. The episode showed how far modern finance had moved beyond the deposit-and-loan bank that Bagehot described, and how central the shadow banking system had become.

## The return of inflation, 2021–23

For a decade after 2008, inflation in the advanced economies stayed low, and the concern was sometimes the opposite: too little inflation, and interest rates stuck near zero. That changed in 2021. Pandemic shutdowns and reopenings disrupted supply chains; demand was supported by fiscal transfers; energy and food prices jumped, sharply after the invasion of Ukraine in 2022; and labour markets tightened. Inflation rose to levels not seen in forty years.

Central banks responded by raising policy rates quickly, after an initial period in which they judged the price pressures to be temporary. Jerome Powell, the chair of the Federal Reserve, argued in a 2022 speech that the central bank must act to keep inflation expectations anchored, because once people and firms expect rising prices, they set wages and contracts accordingly and inflation becomes self-sustaining. He warned that restoring stability would likely bring pain in the form of slower growth, and he framed the task as avoiding the worse outcome of entrenched inflation. The episode reopened old arguments about how much of inflation is demand, how much is supply, and how much is expectations.

## Inflation, expectations, and the value of money

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, and it reduces the purchasing power of money. A simple framework, the quantity equation, relates the money supply, the velocity of circulation, the price level, and real output. It is an identity, not a theory, but it directs attention to a durable point: if money grows much faster than the goods it buys, prices tend to rise. Over long periods this relationship is visible; over short periods, velocity and output move too, so the link is loose.

Inflation has costs even when it is anticipated. It makes long-term contracts riskier, pushes people to hold less money, distorts taxes and accounting, and arbitrarily redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers. When it is unanticipated, the redistribution is sharper. Deflation, a falling price level, brings its own harms: debts grow in real terms, spending is postponed, and a downward spiral is hard to escape. Because expectations matter so much, central banks spend much of their effort persuading the public that they will keep inflation near a target, which is why their credibility is treated as an asset.

## Disagreement: rules, discretion, and the limits of policy

How central banks should use their power is contested. One camp prefers rules — a fixed growth rate for the money supply, or a predictable formula for the policy rate — on the ground that discretion invites inflation and political pressure. Another prefers judgment, because no rule can anticipate every shock. The 2008 crisis and the 2021–23 inflation gave ammunition to both sides: discretionary action was credited with preventing a depression, and blamed for being too slow to tighten later.

A second argument concerns independence. Central banks are deliberately insulated from day-to-day politics, because a government tempted to print money before an election can damage the currency. Critics reply that independence removes important decisions from democratic control, and that monetary policy has distributional effects — it helps some borrowers and hurts others — that ought to be debated openly. A third argument concerns limits. When interest rates are already near zero, conventional policy loses room to cut, and the central bank must rely on unconventional tools with uncertain effects. Money and banking are therefore not a settled machine but a set of institutions whose rules, powers, and limits are argued over continuously.

## The demand for money

How much money people choose to hold is itself an economic decision. A household keeps some cash for ordinary transactions, some as a precaution against unexpected needs, and some as a temporary resting place between selling one asset and buying another. Holding money has an opportunity cost: cash earns no interest, or little, so the higher the rate available on safe alternatives, the less money people tend to hold. This trade-off links the demand for money to the interest rate, and through it to spending and prices.

The demand for money is not fixed. It shifts with income, with habits of payment, and with confidence. In a panic, people want to hold more money and fewer other assets, which can turn a scramble for cash into a fall in the prices of everything else. In calm times, new payment methods can reduce the money people hold for transactions. Because the demand for money moves, a central bank cannot control the quantity of money and the interest rate at the same time; it chooses one and lets the other adjust.

## Interest rates and how policy travels

A central bank does not set every interest rate. It sets a short-term rate it controls directly, and the rest of the structure of rates arranges itself around that anchor. The path from the policy rate to the prices people actually face is called the transmission mechanism, and it has several links. Banks fund themselves partly at rates tied to the policy rate, so their lending rates tend to follow. Mortgages, business loans, and credit-card balances adjust, sometimes quickly and sometimes slowly depending on whether the rate is fixed or floating. Longer-term rates reflect expectations of future policy as much as the current setting.

The effects then spread through spending. Higher rates make borrowing costlier and saving more rewarding, so households postpone purchases and firms delay investment. Higher rates can also strengthen the currency, making imports cheaper and exports dearer. These effects take time — months or longer — to show up in output and prices, which is why policy is often described as working with a lag. Because of the lag, a central bank must act on its forecast, and a wrong forecast can mean tightening into a slowdown or easing into a boom. Expectations are part of the mechanism: if people believe the bank will bring inflation down, they may adjust wages and prices in ways that make it easier to do so.

## Seigniorage, legal tender, and the state

Money and the state have been entangled for as long as both have existed. A government can declare what must be accepted in payment of taxes and debts, which gives its money a captive demand. It can earn seigniorage, the difference between the cost of creating money and its purchasing power, and central banks typically remit their profits to the treasury. These powers are useful and dangerous. Used with restraint, they supply a stable medium of exchange. Used to finance spending beyond what taxes and borrowing can support, they can destroy the currency.

History offers many warnings. When a government prints money to pay its bills, prices rise, and if the printing continues the rise becomes a spiral. The public eventually refuses to hold the money, spending it as fast as it arrives, which raises prices further. This is why the financing of deficits is watched so closely, and why the boundary between monetary and fiscal policy — who decides how much money is created and for what — is a recurring source of argument. The state's role in money is not incidental. It is one of the main reasons money is accepted at all, and one of the main ways it can be ruined.

## Shadow banking and the plumbing of finance

Much modern credit runs outside deposit-taking banks. Money market funds, repurchase agreements, securitisation vehicles, and non-bank lenders perform bank-like functions — borrowing short and lending long — without the deposit insurance and central bank access that make ordinary banks safer. The repurchase, or repo, market is the clearest example: institutions borrow cash overnight against securities as collateral, and the market greases the daily funding of the financial system. It works quietly until collateral values are questioned, at which point lenders demand more collateral or withdraw, and a run occurs outside the regulated banking system.

This is what made 2008 so severe, and it reappeared in March 2020, when a dash for cash forced central banks to intervene on a scale usually reserved for crises. The lesson is that fragility follows the mismatch between short-term funding and long-term assets, wherever it occurs, not only inside institutions called banks. Regulation has since been extended to parts of this shadow system, but the boundary between what is protected and what is not remains contested, because tighter rules raise costs and push activity to wherever the rules are lightest.

## Money in the digital age

The instruments of payment are changing. Cards, mobile transfers, and online ledgers have reduced the use of physical cash in many countries, and private digital tokens have been proposed as alternatives to government money. A privately issued token that is redeemable at a fixed value resembles a bank deposit; one whose value floats resembles a commodity. Governments have responded with proposals for central bank digital currencies, which would give the public direct access to a central bank liability. The arguments echo older ones: who should issue money, what backs it, how private should it be, and what happens in a run. The functions of money have not changed — a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value — but the institutions that provide them are in flux.

## Why banks are regulated

If banks are fragile and their failure can damage the whole economy, societies have reasons to supervise them more closely than ordinary businesses. Regulation takes several forms. Capital requirements force a bank to fund a minimum share of its assets with its own money rather than with borrowed money, so that losses fall first on the owners and depositors are protected. Liquidity rules require a bank to hold enough readily sellable assets to survive a period of withdrawals. Deposit insurance reassures small depositors and removes their incentive to run, and in exchange banks accept supervision and pay premiums into an insurance fund.

Each remedy creates a new problem. Deposit insurance and the expectation of rescue can encourage banks to take greater risks, because the downside is shared with the insurer and the taxpayer; this is moral hazard, and it is the reason capital and liquidity rules exist alongside insurance. Institutions whose failure would threaten the system can borrow more cheaply because lenders expect to be saved, which gives them an advantage over smaller rivals and can make the system more concentrated over time. Rules that are tightened in good times are often relaxed in a crisis, when the priority shifts from discipline to survival.

The result is a permanent argument about the right balance. Too little regulation invites the crises that central banks were created to contain; too much can choke the credit that households and firms depend on, and can push activity into the less regulated parts of the system. Macroprudential policy tries to lean against the build-up of risk across the whole system, not just at individual institutions, by adjusting rules as conditions change. There is no setting that removes both fragility and moral hazard, only a choice about which risks to hold and how to pay for them.

## Bubbles and the psychology of credit

Credit and asset prices can feed on each other. When prices rise, the assets used as collateral are worth more, so banks lend more, which pushes prices higher still. The process reverses when confidence cracks: falling prices shrink collateral, lenders demand repayment, and forced selling drives prices down further. Hyman Minsky argued that long periods of stability encourage lenders and borrowers to take on more risk, moving the system from cautious to speculative to fragile financing, so that stability itself sows the seeds of instability. The pattern has appeared in tulip mania, the railway booms, 1929, the dot-com bubble, and the housing boom of the 2000s.

Bubbles are hard to identify while they are inflating, because it is difficult to say what an asset is really worth. Some argue that central banks should try to lean against credit growth before it becomes dangerous; others reply that they cannot reliably spot bubbles and that pricking them may do more harm than good, so the better course is to strengthen the system against the crash. The debate is unsettled, but the episodes share a lesson: money, credit, and confidence are not separate subjects. They move together, and their interaction is the source of both the economy's growth and its recurring instability.
