# Externalities, public goods, and public finance

**Period:** Classical political economy to the present

## When prices leave costs and benefits outside

A market price reflects what a buyer pays and a seller receives. Sometimes the transaction affects people who are not part of it, and the price does not reflect that effect. When a factory discharges smoke, the people who breathe the air pay a cost that appears in no one's accounts. When a person is vaccinated, neighbours gain protection they did not pay for. These spillovers are called externalities. A negative externality imposes a cost on others; a positive externality confers a benefit on others. In both cases, the private calculation that governs the transaction differs from the social calculation that would count everyone affected.

The consequence is a systematic bias. Where production imposes external costs, the market tends to produce more of the good and charge less for it than is socially best, because the seller does not bear the full cost. Where production creates external benefits, the market tends to produce too little, because the seller cannot charge everyone who gains. The bias is not a failure of greed or of competition; it follows from the fact that the price, which coordinates the private exchange, is not the whole story. Economists call the gap between private and social cost a market failure, not because markets are generally broken, but because in this respect they leave something out.

## Negative externalities: pollution and climate

Pollution is the classic negative externality. A factory that releases waste into a river bears the cost of labour, materials, and machinery, but not the cost of the damaged fishery downstream, unless the law makes it do so. Because that cost is missing from its accounts, the factory produces more pollution than it would if it had to pay for the harm. The same logic applies to traffic congestion, noise, and the overuse of a shared aquifer.

Climate change is the largest and hardest case, because the externality is both global and delayed. A tonne of carbon dioxide released anywhere contributes to warming everywhere, and its effects fall on people who did not choose to bear them, often decades later. No single country can capture the benefit of its own restraint, and each has an incentive to let others act first. This is a collective-action problem on a planetary scale, and it explains why climate policy has been so difficult to agree and to enforce. It also means that the usual remedies — property rights and bargaining between neighbours — are not available, because there is no world government to assign the rights and enforce the bargain.

## Positive externalities and underprovision

Positive externalities are less discussed but equally common. Basic research produces knowledge that others can use without paying the discoverer. Education may make a person a better citizen as well as a better earner, benefiting people who never hire them. A household that maintains its building improves the neighbourhood. In each case, the private return is smaller than the social return, so less is produced than would be best. Patents, prizes, and public funding are attempts to close the gap by rewarding the producer of a spillover, though each brings its own distortions: patents raise prices and can block follow-on invention, and public funding must decide what to support.

## Public goods and collective action

Some goods are hard to provide through markets at all. A public good is non-rival, meaning one person's use does not reduce another's, and non-excludable, meaning it is hard to prevent people who do not pay from enjoying it. A lighthouse warns every ship within sight, whether or not the owner paid; national defence protects everyone in a country once it is provided. Because people can benefit without paying, each has an incentive to free ride, and voluntary contributions fall short of what everyone would prefer.

Mancur Olson argued that this logic weakens large groups in particular: as a group grows, each member's share of the benefit shrinks while the cost of organising stays, so collective action becomes harder. The free-rider problem is a prisoner's dilemma with many players, and it explains why public goods are often provided by the state or by institutions that can compel contributions. It also warns that the state is not the only answer; clubs, cooperatives, and norms can provide some public goods, but they tend to work best in smaller, repeated settings.

## The commons and its alternatives

A related problem arises with common-pool resources: goods that are rival but hard to exclude, such as a fishery, a pasture, or a groundwater basin. Garrett Hardin's famous essay described a "tragedy of the commons" in which each user, acting rationally, takes as much as possible and destroys the resource for all. For a time this was read as an argument for either privatisation or state control. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities have often avoided the tragedy without either, by devising rules they set and enforce themselves: limits on access, quotas, monitoring, and graduated penalties. Her work does not claim that self-governance always works, but it shows that the outcome depends on the rules and the community, not on an iron law.

## Instruments: taxes, markets, and regulation

If external costs are the problem, the remedy is to make them visible in the price. A Pigouvian tax, named for the economist who proposed it, sets a charge equal to the external cost, so that the polluter faces the true social cost of the activity. A cap-and-trade system fixes the total quantity of pollution and lets firms trade permits to emit, which achieves a given reduction at the lowest cost because firms that can cut cheaply do so and sell their permits. Regulation sets limits directly, which is simpler but often more costly. Subsidies can encourage activities with positive externalities, such as clean generation or vaccination.

Each instrument has drawbacks. Taxes must estimate the external cost, which is often uncertain. Tradable permits require careful allocation and monitoring, and a cap set too high does little. Regulation can be rigid and can favour incumbent technology. In theory, if property rights were clear and bargaining costless, affected parties could negotiate to the efficient outcome without government, as Ronald Coase argued; in practice, when many people are affected and the harm is diffuse, bargaining is costly and the state must act.

## Massachusetts v. EPA and the legal boundary

Whether an externality is addressed depends on law as much as economics. In Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency (2007), the Supreme Court considered whether the EPA had authority to regulate greenhouse gases as air pollutants under the Clean Air Act. A group of states and others argued that the agency had the power and the duty to act; the agency argued it lacked authority and that the issue was better handled elsewhere. The Court held that the plaintiffs had standing, because rising seas threatened coastal land, and that greenhouse gases are air pollutants within the meaning of the statute, so the agency could regulate them unless it gave a reasoned explanation for declining.

The case is a reminder that the line between private and public responsibility is drawn by law, not discovered in nature. The same physical spillover can be treated as a private nuisance, a regulated pollutant, a taxed activity, or a permitted one, and which it becomes is a political and legal choice. Economics can describe the cost of each arrangement; it cannot decide the boundary by itself.

## Public finance: what governments do and how they pay

Once a society decides to provide public goods, correct externalities, or redistribute income, it must raise money. Government spending falls into broad categories: defence and public safety, education, health, infrastructure, social insurance such as pensions and health care, and the servicing of debt. Revenue comes mainly from taxes on income, consumption, and property, from payroll contributions, and from borrowing when spending exceeds revenue. A deficit is the annual gap; the debt is the accumulation of past deficits. Borrowing shifts the cost of present spending onto future taxpayers, though it can also finance investment that benefits them.

The scale of government varies widely across countries and over time, and so does the mix of taxes. What is common is the need to weigh what is spent against how it is raised. A program can be desirable and still not worth its cost; a tax can be necessary and still badly designed. Public finance is the study of those comparisons, and its central questions — who pays, who benefits, and at what cost — are the same questions that run through the rest of economics.

## Tax design: maxims and incidence

Adam Smith set out four maxims for a good tax: it should be proportionate to ability, certain rather than arbitrary, convenient in time and manner of payment, and economical to collect, so that it does not take more from the public than it delivers to the treasury. These remain a useful checklist. Certainty matters because an unpredictable tax makes planning impossible and invites corruption. Economy matters because a tax that costs more to administer than it raises is a net loss.

Modern analysis adds two ideas. The first is incidence: the person who legally pays a tax is not always the person who bears its burden. A tax levied on firms may be passed to workers in lower wages; a tax on a product may be passed to consumers in higher prices. Who truly pays depends on how easily each side can avoid the taxed activity. The second is efficiency cost: most taxes discourage the activity being taxed — work, investment, consumption — and the value of the discouraged activity that would otherwise have occurred is a cost beyond the revenue raised. This is why tax design tries to raise needed revenue while distorting behaviour as little as possible, and why the trade-off between equity and efficiency is unavoidable.

## Disagreement: how large, and who pays

How much government should do, and how it should be paid for, is a permanent argument. One view holds that government is too large, that high taxes and debt burden future generations, and that private markets allocate better. Another holds that public provision of goods and insurance is undervalued, that markets leave large harms uncorrected, and that the measure of a society includes what it chooses to provide together. The disagreement is not about whether externalities and public goods exist; it is about how far to go in correcting them, how to weigh the gains against the costs of taxation, and who should bear the burden.

Climate policy concentrates the argument. Economists of many schools favour a price on carbon as the least costly way to reduce emissions, yet carbon taxes are politically difficult because the costs are visible and immediate while the benefits are distant and shared. The design of public finance is therefore not only a technical exercise but a negotiation over who pays now, who benefits later, and what risks a society is willing to bear.

## Public goods in practice

The pure public good is rare, but the spectrum is wide. National defence is close to pure: once a country is defended, everyone within it is protected, and no one can be excluded. Basic research is nearly as open, because a discovery can be used by anyone who learns of it, which is why governments fund it and why firms underinvest in it. A lighthouse, the textbook example, warns every ship in sight; a navigational satellite system serves countless users at once. Roads and bridges are often congested, so they are rival at peak times and closer to a private good; broadcast television is non-rival but excludable by scrambling, which makes it a club good. The labels matter less than the underlying question: can the provider charge those who benefit, and does one person's use reduce another's?

Where the answer is no, provision tends to be public or subsidised, and the design problem becomes how much to provide and who to tax. Where the answer is partly yes, a mix can work: tolls for roads, subscriptions for some services, public funding for the rest. The choice is rarely all-or-nothing, and the practical question is which arrangement delivers the good at the lowest cost while raising enough to pay for it.

## The economics of climate policy

Climate change combines several difficulties at once. The externality is global, so no country can solve it alone and each has an incentive to let others act. The harm is delayed and uncertain, so the case for paying now rests on how the future is valued, a question on which reasonable people differ. The costs of reducing emissions fall on identifiable industries and regions, while the benefits are spread across everyone and arrive later, which is the opposite of the political geometry that usually wins.

Economists across a wide range of views favour putting a price on carbon, either through a tax or a cap-and-trade system, because it lets each emitter decide whether to cut, to pay, or to adopt cleaner technology, and it finds the cheapest reductions first. A carbon tax raises revenue that can be used to cut other taxes or to compensate households; a cap-and-trade system fixes the quantity and lets the price emerge. Both require measuring emissions and monitoring compliance, and both raise the cost of energy, which is why they are politically hard. Regulation and public investment in clean technology are alternatives or complements, and the choice among them is partly about cost and partly about what is politically durable.

## Debt, deficits, and sustainability

When spending exceeds revenue, the difference is borrowed, and the accumulated borrowing is the public debt. Whether a given debt is a problem depends on its size relative to the economy, on the interest rate the government pays, and on the growth of the economy. If the economy grows faster than the interest on the debt, the ratio of debt to output can fall even while the government borrows, which makes the burden more manageable. If the interest rate exceeds growth, the ratio tends to rise unless the government runs a surplus.

Borrowing is not simply a transfer from the future to the present. If it finances investment — roads, schools, research — it can leave the future with more, not less. If it finances current consumption, it shifts the cost onto future taxpayers without leaving them the means to pay. The argument is therefore not about whether deficits are good or bad in themselves but about what the borrowing buys, at what interest rate, and how the burden is shared across generations. Debt can also be used to stabilise an economy in a downturn, when private demand collapses and the government is the spender of last resort.

## Redistribution and the welfare state

Public finance is not only about efficiency. Governments also raise and spend money to change the distribution of income, through progressive taxes, transfers, pensions, health care, and insurance against unemployment and disability. The case rests on several arguments: that markets produce inequalities that a society may judge excessive, that insurance against misfortune is worth providing collectively because private markets handle some risks poorly, and that equal opportunity requires public investment in health and education. The counter-arguments concern the costs of taxation, the risk that transfers reduce the incentive to work or save, and the difficulty of targeting help without creating cliffs and traps.

Design choices matter as much as the size of the budget. Universal programs are simple and politically durable but expensive; targeted programs reach the neediest at lower cost but can stigmatise and can leave the near-poor out. Progressive taxes raise revenue from those most able to pay but can be avoided by the mobile and the well advised. There is no arrangement that avoids trade-offs, and the honest question is which trade-offs a society prefers.

## Valuing what has no price

To decide whether a regulation is worth its cost, governments try to value the benefits that do not appear in markets, such as cleaner air, safer roads, or a lower risk of death. One common method estimates how much people pay for small reductions in risk — for safer jobs, for example — and uses that to put a value on a statistical life, not on any particular person but on the reduction of risk across a population. Other methods survey willingness to pay for environmental improvements or infer it from housing prices near a nuisance. These valuations are contested, because they reduce things people hold sacred to a number, but without some such measure the benefits of protection would be treated as zero. The exercise makes explicit a comparison that would otherwise be made silently, and it forces the argument onto the evidence rather than leaving it to whoever can shout loudest.

## The limits of correction

Recognising a market failure does not prove that government will fix it. The same information problems that prevent markets from pricing an externality also afflict the regulator, who must estimate costs and benefits that no one observes directly. Agencies can be captured by the industries they oversee, or can act on the views of whoever organises most effectively. Programs can produce unintended consequences: a price ceiling meant to protect tenants can reduce the supply of housing; a subsidy meant to help the poor can be captured by middlemen. Public choice theory studies how the incentives facing officials and voters shape policy, and it warns that the remedy can be worse than the disease.

Private arrangements sometimes solve externalities without the state. Ronald Coase showed that if the affected parties are few and bargaining is cheap, they can negotiate to the efficient outcome themselves, regardless of who holds the initial right, because there is a gain to be split. Norms, reputation, and repeated dealing can restrain behaviour where law cannot reach. Firms sometimes internalise spillovers by owning the activities that affect them. But these solutions fail when the affected parties are numerous and dispersed, when the harm is hard to trace, or when the transaction costs of agreement are high — which is exactly the situation for pollution and climate.

The honest position is neither that markets always work nor that government always can. Each has strengths and failure modes, and the choice is comparative: which arrangement handles a given problem with fewer errors and at lower cost. That is why cost-benefit analysis, transparency, and periodic review matter. They make the comparison explicit and give the public a way to judge whether a correction is earning its keep. Economics can clarify the trade-offs and measure the costs; it cannot decide them, because the decision depends on values that citizens must settle together.

## The tax mix

Governments raise revenue in different ways, and the mix shapes the economy. Taxes on income fall on what people earn, and they can be made progressive by taxing higher incomes at higher rates. Taxes on consumption, such as a value-added tax or a sales tax, fall on what people spend; they are efficient to collect and hard to evade, but they weigh more heavily on poorer households unless offset by transfers or exemptions. Taxes on property and wealth fall on what people own; they can curb speculation and reduce inequality, but they are hard to assess and easy to avoid when assets can be moved or hidden. Corporate income taxes fall on company profits, though their burden is ultimately shared by owners, workers, and consumers in proportions that depend on how mobile capital is.

Every mix reflects a judgment about efficiency and fairness. Economists often favour broad consumption taxes for raising revenue with little distortion, and progressive taxes or transfers for redistribution, because they target those most able to pay with the least damage to incentives. Others argue that income and wealth taxes are needed to check the concentration of economic and political power, even at some cost in efficiency. The argument is not resolvable by economics alone, because it turns on how much inequality a society is willing to accept and how much it is willing to pay to reduce it. What economics can do is make the costs and the incidence visible, so that the choice is made with the trade-offs in view rather than hidden behind the design.

## Insurance, risk, and pooling

Some of what governments do is not about externalities or public goods at all but about insurance. Illness, unemployment, disability, and old age are risks that individuals can bear badly on their own, because the losses can be catastrophic and because private insurers face problems of selection and moral hazard. Pooling risk across a large population spreads the cost and makes protection affordable. Public pension and health systems are, in large part, collective insurance, and the case for them rests on the same logic as any insurance, plus the judgment that some risks are better shared than left to the market.

Collective insurance has its own design problems. If the insured cannot be charged according to their risk, those at low risk may resent the subsidy to those at high risk, and those who expect to need the protection have the strongest incentive to join, which is adverse selection. If benefits do not depend on behaviour, people may take more risk or rely on the program when they could provide for themselves, which is moral hazard. The answers — mandates, risk adjustment, waiting periods, and work requirements — each carry costs. As with the other functions of government, there is no arrangement without trade-offs, only a choice about which risks to share and how.

- When does a transaction affect people who are not part of it, and why does that bias the market outcome?
- Why are public goods underprovided, and what can overcome the free-rider problem?
- Which instrument — tax, permit, regulation, or subsidy — fits a given externality, and what does each cost?
- Who really bears the burden of a tax, as distinct from who writes the cheque?
