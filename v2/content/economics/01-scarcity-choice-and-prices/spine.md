# Scarcity, choice, and the price system

**Period:** Classical foundations to modern economics

## The economic problem

Economics begins with a plain fact: means are limited and possible uses are many. A plot of land may grow wheat, carry a house, remain woodland, or be sold. A day may be spent at labour, rest, study, repair, worship, travel, or idleness. A bar of iron may become a nail, a rail, a hinge, or a tool. To choose one use is to refuse another. Scarcity is not the same thing as poverty. A wealthy merchant, a small farmer, and a treasury all face it, because none can use the same resource twice at the same moment for incompatible ends. The economic problem is therefore a problem of choice under constraint.

Because means are limited, every decision has a cost, and that cost is not always a sum of money. It is the best alternative that must be given up. If a household spends a pound on coal, that pound cannot also buy bread. If a town builds a bridge, the labour, timber, stone, and tax money cannot also build a school. This foregone alternative is the opportunity cost of the choice. It is often harder to see than the thing purchased, because the purchased thing stands before the eye while the alternative remains only imagined.

A trade-off is not always a tragic sacrifice. Many are ordinary. A farmer plants more barley and less oats; a printer buys a faster press and gives up hiring another apprentice; a household keeps more money ready for emergencies and gives up some comfort. The point is not that one use is wicked and another virtuous. The point is that comparison is unavoidable. A person who says only what a policy buys has not yet said what it costs. A person who says only what is saved has not yet said what is lost by saving it.

This way of framing the subject is relatively modern. Adam Smith and his contemporaries wrote about the wealth of nations, the growth of output, and the division of labour. A later generation, including Lionel Robbins, defined economics more narrowly as the study of behaviour in relation to scarce means that have alternative uses. That definition widened the subject: wherever there is a choice among uses, there is an economic problem, whether or not money changes hands. It also narrowed it, by setting aside questions about institutions, power, and history that earlier writers had treated as central. Both the widening and the narrowing are arguments, and the argument is still live.

## Opportunity cost and the unseen

Frédéric Bastiat gave the clearest early statement of opportunity cost in his parable of the broken window. A shopkeeper's careless son breaks a pane of glass. The bystanders console the owner: the glazier will earn six francs, and the money will circulate. Bastiat grants that the glazier's gain is real and visible. What is not seen, he insists, is what the shopkeeper would have bought instead — a new pair of shoes, or another book. The town ends with a repaired window where it once had a whole window plus the shoes or the book. Breaking glass has not created wealth; it has redirected labour toward restoring a loss.

The broken-window fallacy is the error of counting only the first visible expenditure. It appears whenever destruction, waste, or needless repair is treated as a net gain because it gives someone work. Work is not itself wealth. Work is a means of overcoming cost, producing goods, and satisfying wants. To praise work caused by destruction is to forget the work that could have produced an additional good if the destruction had not occurred. The same reasoning applies to public works, tariffs, subsidies, and standing armies. A road built by taxes is visible; the private purchases prevented by the tax are less visible. That does not prove the road is bad. It proves only that the road is not free. The question is whether the road is worth more than the alternative uses of the resources taken to build it.

The difficulty is that the unseen is, by definition, hard to measure. A visible job can be counted; a job that was never created because resources were used elsewhere cannot. This asymmetry shapes political argument, because proposals that concentrate visible benefits on a few while spreading invisible costs across many are easier to defend than the reverse. Economics does not resolve the disagreement. It supplies a discipline for stating what is being compared.

## Production, specialisation, and exchange

Adam Smith opened The Wealth of Nations not with kings, taxes, mines, or gold but with a pin factory. One worker draws the wire, another straightens it, another cuts it, another points it, another grinds the top, and so on through some eighteen operations. Ten workers, each confined to a narrow task, can make tens of thousands of pins a day; ten workers each making whole pins alone could make only a few. Smith attributes the gain to three causes: increased dexterity in each repeated task, the time saved by not passing from one kind of work to another, and the invention of machines suited to a single operation.

Specialisation is not merely an arrangement inside one shop. It spreads through a whole society. The baker does not grow all his own grain, build his own oven, weave his own clothes, and make his own tools. He bakes, sells bread, and buys what others make. The shoemaker, smith, sailor, brewer, and farmer do likewise. Each depends on the rest, and the dependence is not charity. It is exchange. Smith traces the division of labour not to any foresight of general opulence but to a human propensity to truck, barter, and exchange one thing for another. Specialisation makes sense only where output can be traded for other output, so the extent of the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market.

Specialisation raises output but also increases interdependence. A worker who makes only pin heads cannot live on pin heads. A city that produces cloth and imports grain is vulnerable to interrupted trade. The gain from specialisation therefore brings a second problem: coordination. How do separated people, each knowing only part of the whole, decide what to make, how much to make, and where to send it? One answer is command. Another is custom. A third is price.

## Demand, supply, and price

Demand is not mere desire. It is desire backed by willingness and ability to give something in exchange at a particular price. A person may want a carriage, but if he will not or cannot pay the price, his wish does not enter the market as demand. Demand also varies with price. At a lower price, more buyers enter, or existing buyers buy more. At a higher price, some leave, reduce quantity, or seek substitutes. Supply is not mere existence either. Wheat in a barn may not be supplied if the owner holds it back. Supply is the quantity sellers are willing to bring to market at a price. Higher prices often draw out more supply because they cover higher costs or promise greater profit; lower prices push marginal sellers away.

Alfred Marshall compared demand and supply to the two blades of a pair of scissors. Asking whether demand or supply determines price, he wrote, is like asking which blade cuts the paper. If demand for bread rises and supply is unchanged, price tends to rise. If supply expands and demand is unchanged, price tends to fall. If both shift, the result depends on the size and direction of both movements. The metaphor guards against one-sided explanations, and it became the organising image of the subject for a century.

Marshall also drew attention to time. A storm may raise the price of fish at dawn because fewer boats returned; over a longer period, high prices attract more boats, encourage substitutes, or reduce demand. The shorter the period under consideration, the more price is governed by demand against a fixed stock; the longer the period, the more it is governed by cost of production, as capacity, technique, and the number of sellers adjust. The temporary market price therefore differs from a more settled price shaped by costs and by the time needed for buyers and sellers to respond.

The demand-and-supply framework is powerful, but it is a framework and not a description of any particular market. Its curves are drawn on the assumption that other things are held equal. When many things move at once, the diagram must be redrawn. It also assumes that buyers and sellers respond to price, which is usually true in the aggregate and sometimes false for individuals, as the later study of behaviour would show.

## The margin, substitution, and elasticity

Many economic choices are marginal. A farmer does not usually ask whether to grow wheat at all, but whether to put one more acre into wheat rather than barley. A household does not ask whether food has value, but whether the next shilling buys bread, tea, coal, or rent. The marginal question concerns the next unit, not the whole class of goods. This distinction resolves an old puzzle. Water is necessary for life, yet an extra cup of water in a wet country is cheap; a diamond is unnecessary, yet an extra diamond is dear. The total usefulness of water is enormous, but the usefulness of one more cup, where water is already plentiful, is small.

When the price of one good rises, buyers often look for substitutes. If beef becomes dear, some buy mutton, fish, beans, or less meat altogether. If coal becomes dear, households may use less heat, repair windows, or switch fuels if another is available. Substitution is not perfect. A substitute may be inferior, inconvenient, or unavailable. Still, the possibility matters. It is one reason demand curves slope downward and one reason producers cannot set any price they wish.

The responsiveness of quantity to price is called elasticity. If a small rise in price sharply reduces purchases, demand is elastic. If quantity changes little, it is inelastic. Salt, medicine, rent, and staple foods may be less elastic in the short run than luxuries, but even necessities have limits. Elasticity explains why a tax or a shortage does not affect all goods alike, and why the same price change can be a minor inconvenience in one market and a crisis in another.

Marginal reasoning also governs the seller. A firm compares the cost of producing one more unit with the revenue that unit will bring. It expands while the additional revenue exceeds the additional cost, and stops near the point where they meet. This calculation does not require the owner to know the average cost of the whole enterprise or the total value of the product. It requires only a comparison at the edge, which is why marginal analysis proved so portable across very different kinds of business.

## Markets as a discovery process

A market need not be a hall with stalls. It is any arrangement through which buyers and sellers make offers, compare terms, and exchange. A weekly grain market, a stock exchange, a fish auction, and a posted catalogue are different forms of the same basic process. Each gathers information that no single person fully possesses: who has goods, who wants them, at what terms, and how urgently.

Friedrich Hayek argued that this informational role is the central fact about markets, and that it is easily missed. The knowledge required to allocate resources is not concentrated in any one mind or office. It is dispersed among millions of people, each of whom knows particular circumstances of time and place — a local shortage, a machine that needs repair, a buyer who will pay a little more. Prices condense that dispersed knowledge into a single number. When tin becomes scarcer, its price rises, and strangers who know nothing of the original cause economise, substitute, and search for new supply. They respond to the signal without needing to understand it.

Hayek drew a political conclusion from this argument: a central planner cannot gather and process the dispersed knowledge that prices coordinate, so attempts to replace the price system with direct allocation tend to fail or to require coercion. Critics reply that markets also conceal knowledge, that prices can be manipulated or distorted by market power, and that some information — about safety, honesty, or long-run consequences — is not well conveyed by a price at all. The debate is not about whether prices carry information but about how much, how reliably, and at what cost.

## Strategic interaction

Much of economics concerns situations in which the best choice for one person depends on what others choose. This is the domain of game theory. A game has players, the strategies available to each, and the payoffs that follow from each combination of choices. The central solution concept, named for John Nash, is an equilibrium in which no player can improve by changing strategy alone, given what the others are doing. An equilibrium need not be good for the players. In the prisoner's dilemma, two suspects each do better by confessing whatever the other does, so both confess, even though both would be better off if both stayed silent.

The prisoner's dilemma describes many real situations: an arms race, a price war, overfishing, or the overuse of a shared pasture. In each case, individually rational choices produce a collectively poor outcome. The dilemma also shows how the outcome can change. If the interaction is repeated, if the players can communicate and make credible commitments, if reputations matter, or if an outside authority enforces an agreement, cooperation becomes possible. Elinor Ostrom documented how communities have sustained shared resources for centuries through rules they set and monitored themselves, without either privatising the resource or handing it to a central state.

Other games have different shapes. In a coordination game, players benefit from choosing the same option, and the difficulty is settling on which one — the width of railway gauge, the side of the road to drive on, or the standard for a new technology. In a competition between a few large sellers, each firm's pricing depends on its rivals' expected response, which can lead to tacit collusion, price wars, or entry deterrence. Governments design auctions — for radio spectrum, for example — precisely to steer strategic bidders toward an efficient allocation. Game theory does not predict a single outcome in every case; it clarifies which outcomes are stable, which are fragile, and what information or commitment would change them.

## Competition, market power, and policy

The textbook benchmark of competition assumes many sellers, a product buyers treat as interchangeable, and free entry, so that no seller can raise price without losing customers and new firms can enter whenever profit is high. Under those conditions price is driven toward cost, and the market is said to be efficient. The benchmark is useful precisely because so few markets match it.

Many markets are served by a handful of large sellers, or by one. Where entry is difficult — because of scale, patents, control of a network, or the sheer cost of building a rival — a seller can hold price above the competitive level and earn more than the cost of staying in business. The gap between the competitive price and the price a powerful seller can charge measures its market power. Some of the loss falls on buyers who pay more; some falls on trades that never happen because the price is too high. Economists call the unrealised trades a deadweight loss, and estimating its size is a recurring problem in competition policy.

Firms with market power behave strategically, in the sense of the previous section. They may cut price to drive out a rival, hold price high in tacit agreement with competitors, spend to make entry harder, or differentiate their products so that buyers no longer treat them as interchangeable. In markets with network effects, where a product becomes more valuable as more people use it, success can be self-reinforcing: a platform that attracts more users attracts more sellers, and so more users again. Such markets can tip toward a single dominant firm even when the underlying technology is not protected by any patent.

Governments have long tried to police this boundary. The United States passed the Sherman Act in 1890 against cartels and monopolies, added the Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act in 1914, and has revised its merger guidelines several times since, most recently in 2023. The guiding idea for much of the last half-century has been consumer welfare: intervention is warranted when it protects buyers from higher prices or poorer quality. Critics of that standard argue that it misses harms that do not show up in the price of the product — to workers in concentrated labour markets, to privacy, to innovation, or to political power. Defenders reply that a broad standard is hard to apply consistently and can punish firms whose size reflects genuine efficiency.

The disagreement is not about whether competition is desirable. It is about what competition is, how to measure it, and which losses count. The same questions run through the rest of economics: markets coordinate dispersed knowledge remarkably well in some conditions, and fail in others, and much of the subject is the study of which conditions obtain.

## Information, behaviour, and market failure

Standard price theory assumes that buyers and sellers know what they are trading. George Akerlof showed what happens when they do not. In a used-car market, sellers know whether a car is sound or a lemon; buyers cannot tell. Buyers therefore offer a price that reflects the average quality on offer. Owners of good cars, unwilling to sell at that price, withdraw, and the average quality of the remaining cars falls. The market can unravel. The same logic applies to insurance, credit, and labour. Asymmetric information — where one side knows more than the other — can drive good products out of a market, and it gives sellers an incentive to signal quality and buyers an incentive to screen for it.

Behavioural economics questions a different assumption: that people calculate consistently and in their own interest. Herbert Simon argued that people are boundedly rational, satisficing rather than optimising because attention and computation are themselves scarce. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that choices depend on how options are framed, that losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains, and that people rely on quick heuristics that are often useful and sometimes systematically wrong. These findings do not make demand curves useless; they explain why real demand can deviate from the tidy model, and why defaults, framing, and presentation shape what people buy.

Modern markets have industrialised some of these effects. Regulators have documented "dark patterns" — interface designs that steer users toward choices they would not make with clear information, such as subscriptions that are easy to start and hard to cancel. Such practices exploit the gap between the informed seller and the distracted buyer that Akerlof and the behavioural economists described. They are a reminder that the efficiency of a market depends not only on the number of buyers and sellers but on how honestly and clearly information flows between them.

## Institutions, property, and the boundaries of markets

Exchange requires rules. People must know who may sell a field, who owns a tool, what counts as payment, and what happens if a promise is broken. Property and contract are not decorations on markets; they are conditions of ordinary exchange. Without them, bargaining gives way to seizure, fraud, or private force. Different legal orders produce different markets, and the same good may be traded very differently under different rules.

Money, too, rests on trust. A coin carries metal content and a stamp; a banknote carries a promise; a ledger entry carries institutional memory. Money reduces the need for a double coincidence of wants, but it introduces questions of issue, credibility, and value. Inflation, debasement, banking failure, and counterfeiting all show that money is a social instrument, not a neutral token floating above institutions.

Not all allocation happens through market price. Families allocate by obligation and affection; monasteries by rule; armies by command; villages by custom; firms by managerial order. Even inside a market society, much production occurs inside organisations where a worker does not bid for each task. Karl Polanyi argued that a market society is historically unusual: land, labour, and money were made into commodities through law and policy, not simply discovered as natural objects of exchange. The point is not that markets are unreal but that they are made, and that what they are made of determines who bears which risks.

## What prices know and what they do not

A price is a compressed message about relative willingness to buy and sell under existing rules. It tells a producer that a resource is more wanted here than there; it tells a buyer that a good is more plentiful or less than before. No single person needs to grasp the whole picture for the message to work, which is the source of its power. The same compression is the source of its limits. A price does not say whether a buyer is kind, whether a worker is fairly treated, whether a product is safe, or whether a good life is being lived. It records the terms on which exchange is possible, not the full range of what people value.

Economics therefore includes markets but is not exhausted by them. It studies choice under scarcity, the coordination of dispersed knowledge, the games people play when their interests collide, the failures that arise when information is uneven or a resource is shared, and the institutions that decide which choices are open in the first place. The classical writers asked how output grows and how income is divided. The marginal writers asked how choices at the edge form prices. The modern field adds strategic interaction, information, behaviour, and the rules of the game. A careful reader can hold these together: choice under scarcity is general, while the institutions through which choices are made vary, and the price system is one remarkable device among several for coordinating them.

## Questions to carry

- What alternative use is given up when a resource is used one way rather than another?
- Does a price change come mainly from demand, supply, or both, and over what horizon?
- When does individually rational behaviour produce a collectively poor outcome, and what changes it?
- What does a price tell you, and what does it leave out?
- Which parts of an economy are organised by price, which by command, which by custom, and which by negotiation?
