# Trade, growth, and inequality

**Period:** Classical political economy to the present

## Why trade is not a zero-sum game

A common intuition holds that if one side gains in a bargain, the other must lose, so trade between nations is a contest in which one country's gain is another's loss. This intuition is mistaken for the same reason it is mistaken between individuals. Voluntary exchange happens only when each side prefers what it receives to what it gives. The farmer who sells grain and buys tools does not lose the grain in a contest; the grain was worth less to him than the tools, and the tools were worth less to the seller than the grain. Both gain, or the exchange would not occur.

This does not mean every trade is wise, or that both sides gain equally. It means that exchange is positive-sum in the ordinary case, because it moves goods to uses where they are valued more. The same logic applies across borders. A country that can buy cloth more cheaply than it can make it does better by making something else and trading, provided the terms are not set by force or fraud. Adam Smith made the household analogy: a family does not try to make at home everything it consumes, and a kingdom should not either.

## Absolute and comparative advantage

Smith argued that a country should import what costs it more to produce at home and export what it produces more cheaply. This is absolute advantage: who can make a good with fewer resources. David Ricardo went further and showed that trade can benefit both countries even when one of them can produce every good with less labour than the other. His example was England and Portugal, wine and cloth. Suppose Portugal needs fewer workers than England to make both wine and cloth. It might still be better for Portugal to specialise in wine and buy cloth, because the labour freed from cloth can produce more wine than the cloth was worth.

The comparison turns on relative cost, not absolute cost. What matters is the opportunity cost of each good — how much of the other good must be given up to make one more unit. A country has a comparative advantage in the good for which its opportunity cost is lower, even if it has no absolute advantage at all. This is the single most counterintuitive and most important result in the economics of trade, and it survives a great deal of complication. Its practical implication is that specialisation according to comparative advantage raises the total output available, from which both sides can gain.

## Prices, consumers, and producers

Trade changes prices, and price changes create winners and losers. Consumers of imported goods gain, because they pay less or have more choice. Producers who compete with imports lose, because their prices and sales fall. Exporters gain access to larger markets. The gains are spread thinly across many consumers, while the losses are concentrated on a smaller number of producers, who have every incentive to organise against the change. This asymmetry — diffuse benefits, concentrated costs — is the political engine of protection, and it explains why arguments for trade are harder to sustain politically than arguments against it.

Frédéric Bastiat made the asymmetry absurd in his petition of the candlemakers. The makers of candles complain that the sun, a foreign rival, provides light for nothing, and they ask the state to require that windows be closed. The joke turns on treating cheap abundance as an injury to producers rather than a benefit to consumers. Bastiat's target is not competition but the habit of counting the visible losses of protected producers while ignoring the invisible gains of everyone who buys their goods. The same habit appears whenever a tariff is defended by the jobs it saves and not by the higher prices it imposes.

## Trade, growth, and the record

The decades after the Second World War saw a broad reduction in tariffs and other barriers, negotiated first under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and later through the World Trade Organization. World trade grew faster than world output, and many countries that opened to trade grew quickly. The record is one of both aggregate gains and uneven distribution. The volume of international trade expanded enormously over the long run, and the evidence points to trade as one factor raising average incomes and productivity. But the gains are distributed unequally within countries as well as between them: workers in trade-exposed labour markets have suffered job losses, lower wages, and long spells out of work, and the lower prices they pay do not always compensate them.

Two cautions are needed. First, correlation is not causation: countries that opened to trade often changed other policies at the same time, and the direction of cause is debated. Second, averages conceal distribution. A country's total income can rise while a region dependent on a protected industry declines, and the gains from trade can accrue disproportionately to owners of capital and to skilled workers. The question of who gains within a country is as important as whether the country gains in aggregate.

## Supply chains and the geography of production

Modern trade is less about finished goods crossing borders than about production split across them. A single product may be designed in one country, its components made in several others, assembled in another, and sold worldwide. Container shipping, falling communication costs, and just-in-time inventory made this fragmentation profitable, and the result was a dense web of supply chains rather than simple two-way exchange. The gains are large, because each step can occur where it is cheapest, but so are the vulnerabilities: a flood, a strike, or a pandemic at one node can halt production far away.

The disruption of 2021–22 made this visible. Ports congested, shipping costs soared, and shortages of semiconductors idled factories on other continents. Firms and governments began to speak of resilience rather than pure efficiency, and of moving production closer to home or to politically reliable partners. This is a real change in emphasis, but it does not repeal comparative advantage; it changes how much weight is placed on security and continuity relative to cost.

## Who gains and who loses

The distributional effects of trade run along several lines. Between countries, trade can promote convergence: poorer countries that integrate can grow faster and narrow the gap with richer ones, as parts of East Asia did. Within countries, trade tends to raise the return to the abundant factor and lower the return to the scarce one. In a country with many skilled workers and few unskilled ones, trade can widen the gap between skilled and unskilled wages, because the scarce factor loses protection.

Technology compounds the effect. Skill-biased technical change raises the demand for educated workers independently of trade, and the two forces are hard to separate. Studies of the shock from Chinese imports in the 2000s found concentrated and persistent losses in affected local labour markets, even as the country as a whole gained from cheaper goods. The finding does not overturn the case for trade; it shows that the gains and losses fall on different people, and that a country can gain in aggregate while particular communities are damaged.

## The backlash and the return of industrial policy

The uneven distribution helped produce a political reaction. Beginning in 2018, the United States and other countries imposed new tariffs, and the language of trade policy shifted from liberalisation toward protection and leverage. More recently, governments have adopted industrial policies aimed at bringing strategic industries — semiconductors, batteries, clean energy — onto domestic soil or into friendly ones. These policies blend economic and security goals, and they are defended as correcting market failures in supply security as much as protecting producers.

The shift revives old arguments. Free-trade advocates warn that protection raises prices, invites retaliation, and protects inefficient firms. Supporters of industrial policy reply that markets underinvest in resilience and in industries with long payoffs, and that the location of production shapes future capability. Both sides can point to history. The disagreement is not about whether trade has gains but about how to weigh them against security, distribution, and the risk of dependence.

## Disagreement: how much does trade explain?

A final argument concerns how much of what has happened should be attributed to trade at all. Some economists emphasise trade and the integration of labour markets as the main force behind rising wage inequality and the decline of manufacturing employment. Others emphasise technology, arguing that automation would have changed these labour markets even with no change in trade. Still others stress domestic policy — the decline of unions, the minimum wage, and the tax system. The evidence supports a role for each, and the disagreement is about relative weight.

What is not seriously disputed is the core of Ricardo's insight: relative opportunity cost shapes the pattern of trade, and specialisation can raise total output. What remains open is how the gains are shared, how much weight to give security and resilience, and whether the losses borne by particular places and workers should be met with compensation, retraining, or protection. Trade is a powerful source of growth, and it is also a powerful source of disruption, and honest argument requires holding both facts at once.

## A worked example

The logic of comparative advantage is easier to see with numbers. Suppose two countries, North and South, each have 100 units of labour. North can make a unit of cloth with 2 units of labour or a unit of wine with 4. South can make cloth with 4 or wine with 3. North is better at both — it has an absolute advantage in each. If each splits its labour evenly, North makes 25 cloth and 12.5 wine; South makes 12.5 cloth and 16.7 wine. Total output is 37.5 cloth and about 29 wine.

Now let each specialise where its opportunity cost is lower. For North, one cloth costs half a wine, while one wine costs two cloth. For South, one cloth costs three-quarters of a wine, and one wine costs one and a third cloth. North gives up less wine to make cloth, so it specialises in cloth; South gives up less cloth to make wine, so it specialises in wine. North makes 50 cloth, South makes about 33 wine. Total output rises to 50 cloth and 33 wine, more of both than before. By trading, each can end up with more than it had while self-sufficient.

The example shows three things. First, absolute productivity does not determine the pattern of trade; relative opportunity cost does. Second, the size of the gain depends on the terms of trade — how much wine one cloth buys — which bargaining and markets settle. Third, the total gain does not say how it is divided. North could capture most of it, or South could, depending on prices. Comparative advantage explains why there is a pie to divide, not how it is cut.

## Trade agreements and the rules of trade

Modern trade is governed by rules as much as by prices. After the Second World War, countries negotiated the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, which became the World Trade Organization in 1995. Members agree to lower barriers and to treat trading partners alike, and they submit disputes to a panel rather than retaliating at will. The system has reduced tariffs substantially, though it has always left agriculture, services, and government procurement partly outside its reach.

Regional agreements go further among their members, removing barriers and harmonising rules. They raise a question that economists have argued over since Jacob Viner: does a regional bloc create trade, by replacing expensive domestic production with cheaper imports from a partner, or divert it, by replacing cheap imports from outside with more expensive ones from a partner who enjoys preferential terms? The answer depends on the details. Rules of origin, which determine whether a product counts as coming from a member, can turn a simple tariff into a thicket of regulation.

## Capital, migration, and mobile factors

Goods are not the only thing that crosses borders. Capital moves as foreign direct investment, when a firm builds or buys operations abroad, and as portfolio investment, when savers buy foreign securities. Investment can bring technology and jobs, but it can also leave quickly, and sudden stops in capital flows have caused crises in emerging economies. Migration moves labour itself, and it is the most restricted factor of all. When workers move, the gains can be larger than those from goods trade, because a person carries skills and can produce in the new place; the political resistance is also larger, because the effects on local wages and communities are visible and immediate.

The movement of factors complicates the simple story of trade. If capital and labour were perfectly mobile, wages and returns would tend to equalise across countries, and trade in goods would matter less. Because they are not, trade in goods is a partial substitute: instead of moving workers to the jobs, a country can move the goods the workers make. Each arrangement distributes the gains differently, and each creates different losers.

## Trade and the environment

Trade affects the environment in two directions. It can raise production and the pollution that comes with it, and it can move dirty industries toward countries with weaker rules — the so-called pollution-haven effect. But trade also spreads cleaner technology and allows countries to specialise in what they do relatively cleanly. The net effect depends on scale, composition, and technique, and the evidence does not support a single verdict.

A practical difficulty is that a country that taxes carbon at home puts its own producers at a cost disadvantage against imports from places that do not. This has led to proposals for border carbon adjustments, which charge imports for the emissions embedded in them and rebate exports. Such measures could reduce the leakage of emissions to other countries, but they are complex to administer and can shade into protection. The tension between environmental policy and trade rules is one of the live frontiers of both fields.

## Money, exchange rates, and trade

Trade is conducted in currencies, and the exchange rate is itself a price. If a country's currency falls, its exports become cheaper abroad and its imports dearer at home, which tends to narrow a trade deficit. If it rises, the reverse. Exchange rates move with interest rates, expectations, and capital flows, so a country can find its trade position changing for reasons that have nothing to do with its producers. Some countries fix their currency to another to reduce this uncertainty; others let it float. A fixed rate buys stability at the cost of giving up an independent interest rate, and a floating rate buys independence at the cost of volatility. Neither arrangement removes the need to adjust when trade is persistently unbalanced.

## The terms of the argument today

The debate has moved from whether trade has gains to how those gains should be weighed against other goals. Advocates of open trade point to cheaper goods, larger markets, and the diffusion of technology, and warn that protection invites retaliation and shelters inefficiency. Advocates of a more managed approach point to supply security, the loss of strategic industries, and the communities that did not share in the gains. Industrial policy, tariffs, and the deliberate relocation of supply chains are now mainstream instruments, not fringe ideas.

What both sides share is a recognition that trade is not a force of nature. It is shaped by rules, by infrastructure, by security, and by politics, and those choices determine who gains. The economics of comparative advantage explains why there is something to distribute; the politics of trade determines how it is distributed and at what cost to other goals. A serious account holds the two together rather than treating either as the whole answer.

## Growth: why some countries get rich

Trade is one force among several that determine how rich a country becomes. The standard starting point is output per worker, which rises when workers have more capital to work with, when they are more skilled, and when technology improves. Capital accumulation can raise output, but with diminishing returns: each additional machine adds less than the one before, so capital alone cannot explain sustained growth. Sustained growth comes mainly from better technology and better ways of organising production, which is why the study of growth is largely the study of innovation and its diffusion.

Countries that start poor can grow quickly by adopting technology and methods already used elsewhere, a process called catch-up. Parts of East Asia demonstrated how fast this can go when investment, education, and openness combine. But catch-up is not automatic. It requires that investment be productive, that workers be educated, that firms face competition, and that the gains not be captured by a narrow group. Economists who study the deep causes of growth point to institutions — property rights, the rule of law, constraints on arbitrary power, and the openness of the political system — as the factors that determine whether the proximate causes of growth can operate.

Trade enters this picture as a channel. It lets a country specialise, exposes firms to competition and to new ideas, and enlarges the market that a producer can serve, which can make investment in better technology worthwhile. But trade is neither necessary nor sufficient for growth on its own. Some countries grew behind high barriers; some that opened did not grow. The evidence supports the view that openness helps when other conditions are present, and that its effects depend on how the gains are used and shared. Growth also has costs that do not appear in the totals: environmental damage, disruption to communities, and the concentration of gains. The question of how to grow is therefore inseparable from the question of what kind of growth, and for whom.

## Trade and development

How a country should relate to the trading system has been argued for as long as there has been development policy. One school, influential in the mid-twentieth century, urged poor countries to protect infant industries and substitute domestic production for imports, on the ground that new industries cannot compete with established ones until they reach scale. The policy produced some successes and many failures, as protected industries often stayed inefficient and consumers paid more. Another school pointed to the East Asian economies, which grew rapidly while orienting production toward export markets and exposing firms to foreign competition, and credited that orientation for their success.

The truth is more complicated than either camp allows. The successful East Asian economies did not simply open up; they combined export discipline with selective support, high saving, and heavy investment in education. Their governments used the requirement of competing abroad as a test of whether support was earning its keep. Later, global value chains changed the options again: a country no longer needs to build a whole industry to participate, because it can specialise in a single stage. That lowers the barrier to entry and raises the value of reliable infrastructure, logistics, and skills. The lesson is that trade is a tool whose effect depends on how it is used, and that development is not a matter of choosing between markets and the state but of getting the sequence and the incentives right.

## Reading the balance of payments

A country's trade with the world is recorded in its balance of payments. The current account covers trade in goods and services, plus income from foreign investments and transfers. When a country imports more than it exports, it runs a current-account deficit, which must be matched by an inflow of capital — foreign lending or investment — because every payment abroad has to be financed. A surplus is the mirror image: the country is a net lender to the rest of the world. The two sides always balance, which is an accounting identity and not a policy result.

A deficit is not automatically a sign of weakness. A young, fast-growing country may import machinery and borrow abroad to finance investment, and the future returns can repay the borrowing. A deficit can also reflect a spending boom, an overvalued currency, or a loss of competitiveness, and it can end abruptly when foreign lenders lose confidence. The composition matters as much as the total: borrowing to invest differs from borrowing to consume. Exchange-rate arrangements, capital controls, and domestic saving all influence the balance, which is why countries argue about it and why the same figure can support opposite stories.
