# Fallacies and counterexamples

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

**One bird against a rule.** — For as long as Europeans wrote about swans, they wrote about a white bird. The Roman poet Juvenal used "a rare bird on earth, very like a black swan" as a byword for something that did not exist. Then Dutch navigators reached the western coast of Australia, and in 1697 Willem de Vlamingh saw black swans in the estuary he named the Swan River. A single population, once observed, ended a rule that had seemed to hold everywhere. The claim "every swan is white" is a universal claim; its refutation required nothing more than one swan of another colour. That small transaction — a general claim meeting a case it cannot survive — is the subject here. The organizing question is narrow and hard: how does one well-chosen case refute a general claim, and when does it fail to?

**The general claim and the single case.** — A universal claim says that every member of some class has some property: every swan is white, every metal expands when heated, every action has a motive. Its contradictory is not "no swan is white" but "some swan is not white." To refute the universal, one case is enough. The inference is the one logicians call modus tollens: if the claim were true, this case could not occur; the case occurs; so the claim is false. Notice what the single case does and does not do. It does not establish the opposite universal — one black swan does not show that no swan is white, and a single counterexample to "all metals expand when heated" does not show that none do. It removes one claim from the field and leaves the rest of the field open. This is why the counterexample is a tool of criticism rather than of proof, and why a discipline that lives by it — philosophy, but also mathematics and the law — is better at destroying bad general claims than at erecting good ones.

**Necessary and sufficient conditions.** — When a philosopher defines something, the definition is usually a set of conditions offered as necessary, or sufficient, or both. A condition is necessary if nothing can be the thing without meeting it; it is sufficient if meeting it guarantees that the thing is present. Being a bird is necessary for being a swan but not sufficient, since sparrows are birds too. Having four sides is necessary for being a square and, with equal sides and right angles, sufficient. Two kinds of counterexample test a definition. A case that meets the conditions but is not the thing shows that the conditions are not sufficient. A case that is the thing but fails a condition shows that the condition is not necessary. Plato's early dialogues are a long record of this method. In the Euthyphro, Socrates asks what piety is, and Euthyphro answers that the pious is what the gods love. Socrates then presses: is the pious loved because it is pious, or pious because it is loved? If the gods love it for a reason, then their love is not what makes it pious, and the proposed condition is not the source of the thing it was meant to explain. The question does not refute the answer outright, but it exposes the definition as untested.

**The definition of knowledge.** — The most famous modern counterexample to a definition concerns knowledge. For centuries a common account held that knowledge is justified true belief: a person knows a proposition when it is true, when the person believes it, and when the person has good grounds for believing it. Each condition seems necessary, and together they seemed sufficient. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published two short cases that appeared to satisfy all three conditions without being knowledge. In one, a man named Smith has strong evidence that a colleague, Jones, will get a position, and also that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. From these he infers that the man who will get the position has ten coins in his pocket. Unknown to Smith, he himself will get the position, and he himself happens to have ten coins in his pocket. His belief is true and justified, but its truth is an accident of the way the case turned out, not the result of his reasoning. If Gettier is right, justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge, and the definition needs a fourth condition — though what that condition is has been argued about ever since. The case shows the counterexample at its most powerful: a small, precise, imaginary scenario that forces a revision of a claim that had stood for centuries.

**Scope and ambiguity.** — Counterexamples work only when the claim has a determinate meaning, and many claims do not. "All that glitters is not gold" is a proverb that trades on an ambiguity: it can mean that not everything that glitters is gold, or that nothing that glitters is gold. The first is true, the second false, and the sentence alone does not say which is meant. Logicians call this a question of scope: which word the "not" and the "all" govern. "Every boy loves some girl" is likewise two claims at once — for each boy there is a girl he loves, or there is a single girl whom every boy loves — and only one of them is meant. A related failure is equivocation: an argument that works only because a word shifts meaning between its premises and its conclusion. Aristotle's first example of a language-dependent fallacy is the argument that "those learn who know," which turns on "learn" meaning both to acquire knowledge and to use it. The Euthydemus of Plato is a comic museum of such shifts: the sophists argue that no one can contradict anyone, that no one can speak falsely, and that a man's father is a dog, each by sliding between two senses of a word. Before a case can refute a claim, the claim has to be read in one way rather than another, and much of the work of argument is fixing the reading.

**Aristotle's two lists.** — The first systematic treatment of these failures is Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations, probably written in the 340s or 330s BCE and placed at the end of the logical works later called the Organon. Aristotle's subject is not error in general but the sham refutation: an argument that appears to refute an opponent and does not. He divides them into two families. Six depend on language — ambiguity, amphiboly (ambiguity of construction), composition, division, accent, and form of expression. Seven do not depend on language — accident, the use of a phrase absolutely or only in a respect, ignorance of what a refutation is, the consequent, assuming the original conclusion, treating what is not a cause as a cause, and putting several questions into one. Aristotle treats the last family's master fault as ignoratio elenchi, ignorance of what refutation is: a real refutation must contradict the same thing, in the same respect, at the same time, and must not assume the point at issue. Every sham refutation fails one of those conditions. The list was designed for a practical setting — the competitive dialectic of the schools — and Aristotle says the point of studying it is both to avoid being taken in and to be able to expose the man who takes others in. It is the ancestor of every later catalogue of fallacies, and its vocabulary still survives in the Latin names.

**The modern names.** — The labels in common use are mostly later coinages. Ad hominem ("to the man") attacks the arguer rather than the argument. The straw man refutes a weakened version of an opponent's position. A false dilemma presents two options as the only ones. Begging the question assumes the conclusion among the premises. Equivocation shifts the sense of a term. An appeal to authority substitutes a name for a reason. Post hoc ergo propter hoc ("after this, therefore because of this") mistakes sequence for cause. A hasty generalization draws a broad rule from too few cases. A slippery slope claims that one step leads inevitably to a disastrous last step. Circular reasoning returns to its starting point. A loaded question smuggles an assumption into the asking. The list is a tradition rather than a closed canon: it grew over centuries, from Aristotle through medieval logic to the nineteenth-century textbooks, and different writers include different items. The names are useful because they make a pattern easy to spot and easy to name. They are not arguments. To call a move a fallacy is to make a claim about it, and the claim can itself be wrong.

**The argument and the arguer.** — The ad hominem is the clearest case of a fallacy that is not always one. If a witness's eyesight is poor, or an expert is paid by the party she testifies for, then the person is exactly what is at issue, and questioning the person is not a distraction from the argument but the argument. The fallacy occurs only when the attack on the person is offered as a substitute for an assessment of the claim. John Locke, in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), sorted the everyday ways of winning assent into four kinds. Three of them, he thought, are bad: the argumentum ad verecundiam, which appeals to the standing of a respected name; the argumentum ad ignorantiam, which demands that the opponent either accept the claim or produce a better one; and the argumentum ad hominem, which presses a person with consequences drawn from his own principles. Only the fourth, the argumentum ad judicium, which draws its proofs from the foundations of knowledge, "advances us in knowledge." Locke's ad verecundiam is the ancestor of the modern appeal to authority, and his verdict on it needs qualification too: appeal to a competent authority is a reasonable shortcut in a world where no one can check everything, and the fallacy lies in the irrelevance or the overreach — the physicist cited on questions of history, the celebrity cited on questions of medicine.

**Cause and the order of events.** — Post hoc ergo propter hoc is the error of concluding that because one event followed another, the first caused the second. Aristotle, in the Sophistical Refutations, gives the example of a man who infers from wet ground that it has rained, when the ground may have been wet for another reason. The argument from a sign to its cause is not always wrong — wet ground is some evidence of rain — but it is not conclusive, because the same sign has other causes. David Hume, in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748), made the deeper point: what experience presents is one event constantly followed by another, and never the causal tie itself. The mind forms the habit of expecting the second when it sees the first, and mistakes the habit for a necessity in the things. The result is that a causal claim is tested not by one sequence but by comparison: John Stuart Mill, in A System of Logic (1843), set out methods for doing this — looking for cases where the effect is present with the cause and absent without it, and for cases where cause and effect vary together. The counterexample to "X causes Y" is a case where X occurs without Y, or Y without X, once other differences are controlled.

**The sample and the generalization.** — A hasty generalization draws a general rule from a sample too small or too unrepresentative to support it. The remedy and the test are the same: look for the case outside the sample. When the case is found, the claim can be revised in one of two honest ways. "All swans are white" gives way to "most swans are white," or to "all swans in Europe are white." The dishonest way is the move Antony Flew named the "no true Scotsman": when a counterexample appears, redefine the category so that the counterexample no longer counts. If no Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge, and a Scotsman is found doing so, the reply that "no true Scotsman would" saves the claim at the cost of emptying it. The same logic governs the difference between a universal and a statistical claim. A single black swan refutes "all swans are white." It does not refute "most swans are white," and it does not refute "99 per cent of swans are white." What would refute those is not a case but a rate, and so the form of a claim determines what evidence can count against it.

**The chain and the missing warrant.** — A slippery slope argument runs: allow the first step, and the next will follow, and the next, until a disaster; therefore do not allow the first. Such an argument can be good. If each link is supported — if the first really does make the second likely, and the second the third — the warning is a prediction, not a fallacy. It is fallacious when the links are asserted rather than shown, or when the chain is presented as inevitable without evidence for the inevitability. The counterexample is aimed at a single link: find a case in which the first step was taken and the predicted next step did not follow. The ancient sorites paradox, the argument about when a heap of grain becomes a heap, is the ancestor of the puzzle about small differences accumulating, and it shows why the slope can be genuinely hard to locate: sometimes there is no sharp line, and the absence of a line is not the same as a proof that any step leads to the end.

**Assuming the conclusion.** — Begging the question, or circular reasoning, is an argument whose conclusion is already among its premises, or whose premises could not be accepted without first accepting the conclusion. The classic illustration is the defence of a text by its own authority: the Bible is the word of God because it says so, and what it says is true because it is the word of God. The argument is deductively valid — if the premises are true, the conclusion follows — and that is exactly the trouble, because validity is not the same as giving a reason. A valid argument can be question-begging when its premises carry no independent support. Molière mocked the same failure in The Imaginary Invalid (1673), where a candidate for the medical degree explains that opium puts people to sleep because it has a "dormitive virtue," a power of putting people to sleep. The explanation restates the fact in fancier words. Circularity is not always fatal: in a system of connected beliefs some mutual support may be unavoidable, and the question is whether the circle is wide enough to let in evidence from outside it.

**The false pair and the loaded question.** — A false dilemma, or false dichotomy, presents a choice as exhaustive when it is not: "either you are with us or against us" omits the many positions in between, and the counterexample is a third option. Some apparent dilemmas are real — a proposition and its negation do exhaust the possibilities — so the test is whether the alternatives are genuinely exhaustive. A loaded question does not present a choice but presupposes an answer: "Have you stopped beating your wife?" cannot be answered yes or no without conceding that the beating occurred. The fault lies not in the answer but in the question, and the remedy is to reject the presupposition rather than to answer. Aristotle's seventh non-language fallacy, the making of several questions into one, is the ancient form of the same move: the questioner conceals two questions under one, so that a single answer is taken to settle both.

**The straw man.** — A straw man argument refutes a position the opponent does not hold, and then treats the refutation as a victory over the opponent. The reasoning may be flawless; the fault is in the attribution. Its remedy is the principle of charity, the rule — argued for by W. V. O. Quine and Donald Davidson as a condition of interpretation itself — that one should read an opponent's words in the sense that makes the position strongest and most coherent. The modern name for the strengthened version is the steel man. The straw man is worth noticing because it shows that a fallacy need not be a mistake in logic at all. It can be a mistake in reporting, and the counterexample to it is not a case but the opponent's actual words.

**The case in philosophy.** — The counterexample is not only a device for finding errors; it is a method of doing philosophy. Gettier's cases revised the analysis of knowledge. John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), described the method as reflective equilibrium: general principles are tested against considered judgments about particular cases, and either the principle or the judgment may be revised until the two settle into a consistent whole. Robert Nozick, in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), attacked the claim that pleasure is the only good with the experience machine, an imaginary device that would give a person any experiences they wished; he argued that most would refuse to plug in, and that the refusal shows something is valued besides experience. The trolley problem, in which a runaway tram forces a choice between killing one person and killing five, has generated a literature of variants, each a small counterexample aimed at a proposed moral rule. Daniel Dennett called such devices intuition pumps: stories engineered to draw out a judgment. The method works because a general claim, however abstract, is answerable to cases, and the cases can be made up as well as found.

**The limits of the counterexample.** — Powerful as it is, the counterexample is not a machine for deciding disputes. Four limits are worth marking. First, the form of the claim matters. A universal claim falls to a single case; a statistical or ceteris paribus claim does not, because it never said the case could not happen. Second, the case must be described in a way both sides accept. Thought experiments are often loaded in the telling, and intuitions about them vary between people and, as experimental philosophers have shown, between cultures. Third, a claim can almost always be saved by giving up something else. Pierre Duhem and W. V. O. Quine argued that a theory meets experience as a whole, not claim by claim, so that a stubborn result can be absorbed by adjusting an auxiliary assumption instead of the central claim. Quine's image was a web of belief in which any strand can be kept if enough others are rearranged. Fourth, some claims are not answerable to cases at all — mathematical identities, definitions, and conceptual truths — and to seek a counterexample to them is to misunderstand what they are. The counterexample is a scalpel, not a sledgehammer, and it cuts only where the claim has exposed an edge.

**How to test a claim by seeking the case that would break it.** — The procedure that follows from all this is plain. State the claim precisely enough that it could be false. Fix its scope and its key terms, so that "all" means all, and "swan" means swan. Ask what a case against it would look like — not a case that merely feels awkward, but one that meets every condition the claim lays down and still contradicts it. Construct that case as strongly as possible, and give the opponent's position its best form before attacking it. Then decide. If the case is genuine, either withdraw the claim or revise it, and say which. If it is not, say which condition it fails to meet, or which ambiguity it exploits. Karl Popper, in The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934; English translation 1959), made the willingness to be refuted the mark of a scientific claim: a theory that forbids nothing, that is compatible with every possible observation, tells nothing. Popper's own account of how science actually proceeds has been much criticised — Imre Lakatos, Thomas Kuhn, and Paul Feyerabend each argued that falsification is neither as clean nor as central as he claimed — but the underlying discipline survives the criticism. The question to put to any general claim is the one the black swan put to the white: what would have to be the case for this to be false, and has that case been sought?

## Chronology

- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle writes the Sophistical Refutations, listing six language-dependent and seven language-independent fallacies.
- **c. 390 BCE** — Plato's Euthydemus stages sophistical arguments built on ambiguity and equivocation.
- **c. 380 BCE** — Plato's Euthyphro tests a definition of piety by asking what makes a thing pious.
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle's Prior Analytics sets out the syllogism; the Organon fixes the vocabulary of later logic.
- **1690** — Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding distinguishes four sorts of argument, three of them bad.
- **1697** — Willem de Vlamingh sights black swans in Australia; the white-swan rule is refuted.
- **1748** — Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding argues that cause is not observed, only constant conjunction.
- **1843** — Mill's A System of Logic sets out the methods for identifying causes.
- **1963** — Edmund Gettier publishes counterexamples to the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge.
- **1970** — Charles Hamblin's Fallacies attacks the inherited textbook treatment of the subject.
- **1971** — Rawls's A Theory of Justice describes reflective equilibrium.
- **1995** — Douglas Walton's A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy treats fallacies as failures in dialogue.

## Questions to carry

- What is the difference between a claim's being false and a claim's being unclear, and why does the difference matter for the counterexample?
- Gettier's cases are imaginary. Can an imaginary case refute a claim about the actual world, and if so, how?
- Locke thought the appeal to authority was a bad argument. When is citing an expert reasonable rather than fallacious?
- If a claim can always be saved by adjusting something else, as Duhem and Quine argued, what stops any claim from being immune to counterexample?
- Which of the named fallacies is really a mistake in reasoning, and which is a mistake in something else — reporting, asking, or defining?
