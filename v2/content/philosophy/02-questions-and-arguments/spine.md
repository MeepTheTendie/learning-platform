# Questions and arguments

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

In the porch of the King Archon at Athens, in 399 BCE, a man on his way to answer a charge of impiety stopped a religious expert named Euthyphro and asked him a question. Euthyphro was prosecuting his own father for the death of a labourer, and he was confident that he knew exactly what piety required. Socrates asked him what piety is. Euthyphro answered by pointing to what he was doing: piety is prosecuting a wrongdoer, whoever he may be. Socrates replied that this was an example, not an answer. He had not asked for one or two pious acts; he wanted the general idea that makes all pious things pious. The exchange that followed — Euthyphro offers a definition, Socrates tests it, the definition collapses, Euthyphro offers another — is the oldest surviving extended display in the West of a distinctively philosophical activity: pressing a question until the reasons behind an answer are exposed.

## The question that will not settle

**A definition demanded and evaded.** — Euthyphro's first answer was that piety is doing as he was doing. Socrates objected that many acts are pious besides prosecuting a murderer, and that a definition must state the one feature that all pious acts share. Euthyphro tried again: piety is what is dear to the gods, and impiety what is not. Socrates asked whether the gods ever disagree. Euthyphro, who believed the old stories in which the gods quarrel, had to admit that they do; and if the gods disagree about what is just, then the same act can be dear to one god and hateful to another, and so both pious and impious at once. Euthyphro amended the definition: what *all* the gods love is pious. Socrates then asked a question that has kept its force: is the holy loved by the gods because it is holy, or is it holy because it is loved? If the first, then holiness is one thing and being loved another; the definition has named an attribute of piety, not its essence. If the second, then piety depends on the gods' approval and nothing else. Euthyphro could not say, complained that the argument seemed to walk away like a statue of Daedalus, and left. The dialogue ends with no definition of piety. The failure is the point: the question has been pursued by reasons, tested, and sharpened, even though it has not been settled.

**The question behind the question.** — The Euthyphro is not only about piety. It is about what it is to give an answer, and what it is to give a reason. The organizing question of this unit is: when someone gives reasons, what exactly are they claiming supports what? That question is one step back from any particular dispute. It asks about arguments as such — their parts, their force, the premises they leave unsaid, the difference between supporting a claim and explaining it, and the difference between evaluating a reason and dismissing a conclusion one dislikes. The materials for answering it are old, because the problem is old: Plato's Socrates testing definitions, Aristotle's account of the syllogism and the enthymeme, Descartes's chain of "therefores" in the *Meditations*.

## What makes a question philosophical

**Empirical questions and philosophical questions.** — Some questions are settled by looking: how many ships sailed, how much the barley weighed, whether the patient's fever broke. These are empirical questions; their answers are found by observation, measurement, documents, or experiment. Other questions are not settled by looking, even in principle: whether it is ever right to kill, what makes a promise binding, whether the mind is the same thing as the brain, what a number is. These are philosophical questions. They concern concepts, norms, and the presuppositions of inquiry rather than the inventory of facts. The distinction is a working one, not a sharp line. "Is water H2O?" is empirical chemistry, but "what is a cause?" is philosophy even though physicists use causes constantly. "Does the soul survive death?" turns partly on what a soul is, which is a conceptual question, and partly on whether there is a soul, which is not.

**First-order and second-order questions.** — A first-order question is asked from inside a practice: is this action just? Is this belief true? A second-order question is asked about the practice: what does "just" mean? What makes a belief true? What makes a reason a good reason? The second-order questions are the philosophical ones. Socrates's question, "What is piety?", is second-order. He is not asking whether this or that act is pious; he is asking what makes anything pious, and he wants an answer that can serve as a standard for judging cases. When he says he wants "the general idea which makes all pious things to be pious," he is asking for a definition, and he treats the inability to give one as a serious defect in the person who claims to know.

**The boundary is contested.** — Not every philosopher has accepted a clean separation between the empirical and the philosophical. W. V. O. Quine argued in "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) that philosophy is continuous with natural science, and that the theory of knowledge is best pursued as a chapter of empirical psychology rather than as a separate discipline with its own method. On that view the difference between a philosophical and an empirical question is one of generality and emphasis, not a wall. The question "what is a species?" is answered partly by biology and partly by argument about classification, and it is not always clear which part is doing the work. What marks out a philosophical question, on most accounts, is that it turns on the standards of the inquiry itself: it asks what would count as an answer.

**What philosophy does with a question.** — The Socratic method, as Plato presents it, has a recognizable shape. A definition is proposed. Consequences are drawn from it. A contradiction or an unwelcome result appears. The definition is revised. In the *Euthyphro*, the shape is visible in miniature. M. F. Burnyeat, in "Socratic Midwifery, Platonic Inspiration" (1977), described the method as a kind of intellectual midwifery: the questioner does not hand over the answer but helps the interlocutor deliver and test what he already believes. Gregory Vlastos, in "The Socratic Elenchus" (1983) and *Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher* (1991), argued that the method can refute a position but cannot by itself establish a positive doctrine — a difficulty he called the problem of the elenchus. Burnyeat and others read the method as more constructive. That disagreement is itself an argument about what counts as an argument, and it has not been closed.

## The anatomy of an argument

**Claims, beliefs, and arguments.** — A claim, or proposition, is something that can be true or false: that the wax is one and the same thing throughout its changes; that courage is a kind of knowledge. A belief is a claim that someone holds. An argument is neither a claim nor a belief. It is a claim offered together with reasons for accepting it. The difference matters. "I believe that courage is a kind of knowledge" reports a state of mind; it can be true even if the belief is groundless. "Courage is a kind of knowledge, because it is the knowledge of what is and is not to be feared" offers support; it can be assessed. Beliefs can be held without reasons. Arguments are the giving of reasons, and they are what can be evaluated as good or bad.

**Premises and conclusion.** — Every argument has at least one premise and one conclusion. The conclusion is the claim being supported; the premises are the claims doing the supporting. In standard form the premises are listed, a line is drawn, and the conclusion follows. All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Therefore Socrates is mortal. The first two sentences are premises; the third is the conclusion. The premises are the ground, the conclusion what rests on it. An argument may have one premise or many, and the conclusion may be modest or sweeping. The word "therefore" is not part of the content; it marks the relation between the two.

**The syllogism and its theory.** — Aristotle's *Prior Analytics* (c. 350 BCE) gave the first systematic theory of the syllogism: a form of argument in which, certain things having been laid down, something else follows of necessity. Aristotle catalogued the valid forms and showed that their validity depends on their shape, not on their subject matter. A valid syllogism holds whatever it is about, whether animals or numbers or virtues. The theory was transmitted to the Latin Middle Ages by Boethius (c. 480–524) and refined by Peter Abelard (1079–1142) and William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347), who worried about the logic of conditional statements and entailment. The vocabulary of premises, conclusions, validity, and consequence that is still used is largely theirs, inherited through Aristotle.

**Locating a disagreement.** — Identifying the parts of an argument is not pedantry; it is how a dispute is located. Two people may agree on the conclusion and differ about the premises, or agree on the premises and differ about whether they support the conclusion. In the *Euthyphro*, nobody disputes that murder is wrong. The dispute is about what makes an act pious — about whether there is a standard of holiness independent of the gods' love. That is a disagreement about a premise, and until the premise is stated the argument cannot be assessed. Socrates's method is, in large part, a method for making people state the premises they had been assuming.

## Inference and the "therefore"

**The force of "therefore".** — "Therefore," "so," "hence," "consequently," and "it follows that" are conclusion indicators; "because," "since," "for," and "given that" are premise indicators. They are useful guides to the shape of a passage, but they do not make an inference. A speaker can write "therefore" where nothing follows, and a good argument can be stated without any indicator at all. The test of an inference is not the word but the relation: would the conclusion have to be true if the premises were true? If not, does it at least become more likely?

**Validity and soundness.** — An argument is deductively valid when it is impossible for its premises to be true and its conclusion false. Consider: if it rained, the ground is wet; it rained; therefore the ground is wet. That form, called *modus ponens*, is valid. So is *modus tollens*: if it rained, the ground is wet; the ground is not wet; therefore it did not rain. Two near neighbours are invalid. Affirming the consequent — if it rained, the ground is wet; the ground is wet; therefore it rained — fails, because a hose could have done it. Denying the antecedent — if it rained, the ground is wet; it did not rain; therefore the ground is not wet — fails for the same reason. Validity is a matter of form. A valid argument with true premises is *sound*. A valid argument may still have a false conclusion if one of its premises is false, which is why soundness is the stronger standard.

**Induction and strength.** — Not all good reasoning is deductive. An inductive argument makes its conclusion probable without guaranteeing it. Every swan observed so far has been white; therefore the next swan will be white. The conclusion does not follow with necessity, and the discovery of black swans in Australia in 1697 refuted the corresponding universal claim. Inductive arguments are strong or weak rather than valid or invalid, and their strength depends on the number and variety of the cases and on how well the conclusion fits with what else is known. Aristotle treated the rhetorical example as a kind of induction, and the difference between conclusive and probable support runs through the whole subject. It matters for evaluating reasons because a reason can be good without settling the matter.

**Descartes's chain of therefores.** — Descartes's *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) is built out of inferences. In the First Meditation he resolves to reject as false anything that admits of the least doubt. In the Second he notices that the act of doubting cannot itself be doubted, and concludes: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind." The inference runs from "I think" (or "I am deceived") to "I exist." Its unstated premise is that whatever thinks exists. He then asks what he is and concludes that he is "a thinking thing." The "therefore" is doing heavy work, and whether it is entitled to is exactly the question. Antoine Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections (1641), pressed a related difficulty: if clear and distinct ideas need God to guarantee them, and God's existence is proved by a clear and distinct idea, the argument seems to circle — the problem later called the Cartesian circle. The dispute is not about whether Descartes's conclusion is true but about whether his reasons reach it.

## Enthymemes, or the premises left unsaid

**Aristotle's rhetorical syllogism.** — In the *Rhetoric* (c. 350 BCE), Aristotle defined the enthymeme as a rhetorical syllogism and the example as a rhetorical induction. "The enthymeme is a sort of syllogism," he wrote, and persuasion through proof uses either enthymemes or examples; there is no other way. An enthymeme often leaves one premise unstated because the audience is expected to supply it. "Socrates is a man; therefore he is mortal" omits "all men are mortal." The omission is not a defect. It is economical, and it can be more persuasive, because the hearer who fills the gap completes the argument and so experiences the conclusion as partly their own. Aristotle observed that speeches relying on enthymemes excite louder applause than those relying on examples.

**Suppressed premises in the Euthyphro.** — Euthyphro's case against his father rests on premises he never states: that the gods agree about what is impious; that whatever the gods hate is impious; and that the story of Zeus punishing Cronos is a reliable guide to what piety requires. Socrates makes him state the first, and it fails, because the gods of the old stories disagree. The unstated premise is where the argument is weakest, and finding it is where reconstruction begins.

**Reconstructing the missing premise.** — To evaluate an enthymeme, supply the premise that makes the argument valid, choosing the most plausible version the speaker could accept. This is the same operation that the principle of charity describes. John Stuart Mill, in *A System of Logic* (1843), argued that the major premise of a syllogism is not a source of new knowledge but a "memorandum" recording past particular inferences, and that all inference is really from particulars to particulars. Whatever one makes of Mill's account, it makes vivid that the general premise may be doing less work than its prominence suggests, and that the particular cases from which it was drawn are doing more.

## Explanation is not argument

**Two uses of "because".** — "The bridge collapsed because the cables had rusted" gives a cause. It takes the collapse as already known and tells us why it happened. "The cables had rusted, so the bridge collapsed" offers a reason to believe the collapse was coming. The same facts appear in both, but the direction of support is reversed. An explanation answers the question "why is it so?"; an argument answers the question "why believe it?" A useful sign that a passage is an explanation is that its "conclusion" — the thing explained — is not in dispute. When the fact is not in doubt, the reasoning is explanatory; when the conclusion is what is at issue, it is argumentative. The two can be hard to separate in practice, because an explanation can also be offered as a reason to believe.

**The symmetry thesis.** — Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim, in "Studies in the Logic of Explanation" (1948), argued that an explanation is a deductive argument whose conclusion is the event to be explained and whose premises include a general law. On this covering-law model, explanation and prediction have the same logical form: whatever explains an event could, in principle, have predicted it. The thesis is contested. Many philosophers hold that explanation has an asymmetry that prediction lacks — that causes explain their effects but not the reverse, and that some explanations are not arguments at all. Wesley Salmon, in *Scientific Explanation and the Causal Structure of the World* (1984), argued for a causal model in place of the covering-law one. The dispute is a live case of the difference between explaining and proving.

**Abduction.** — C. S. Peirce (1839–1914) distinguished deduction, induction, and abduction, or inference to the best explanation: from a surprising fact, infer the hypothesis that would best account for it. Gilbert Harman gave the modern statement in "The Inference to the Best Explanation" (1965). Abduction is at work in the diagnosis, the detective's hypothesis, and the reconstruction of a damaged text. It blurs the line between explanation and argument, because the best explanation is also offered as the reason to believe it. It also shows why evaluating reasons is not always a matter of checking a form: sometimes the question is which of several explanations is best, and the criteria — scope, simplicity, fit with background knowledge — are themselves arguable.

## The principle of charity

**The strongest version.** — The principle of charity says that an interpreter should read a speaker so as to maximize the truth and coherence of what is said, where the text allows. Do not select the weakest reading and refute it — the straw man. Construct the strongest version, sometimes called the steel man, and answer that. The principle has a theoretical as well as a practical role. W. V. O. Quine, in *Word and Object* (1960), and Donald Davidson, in "Radical Interpretation" (1973), argued that an interpreter must assume that speakers mostly believe truths and reason well, because otherwise there is no basis for identifying what their beliefs and words are at all. Richard Grandy, in "Reference, Meaning, and Belief" (1973), proposed a principle of humanity instead: interpret so as to maximize the intelligibility of the speaker's situation and choices, not merely the truth of their beliefs.

**Charity is not agreement.** — The principle does not require concluding that the speaker is right. It requires stating the case in the form in which it is worth answering. The charitable reading of Euthyphro is not "he is confused" but "he holds a divine-command theory: an act is pious because the gods command it." Stated that way, it is a serious position with able defenders. Leibniz, in the *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686) and the *Theodicy* (1710), held that God loves what is just because it is just, so that the standard is God's own nature rather than an arbitrary decree. In the twentieth century Robert Adams and Philip Quinn defended versions of divine-command ethics. The position faces the Euthyphro dilemma directly, and it is more interesting than the caricature.

**The limits of charity.** — Charity can be overdone. If the charitable reading requires the speaker to hold beliefs they plainly reject, or to use words in a private sense no one else could share, then it has stopped being interpretation. The goal is the strongest version the evidence will bear, not a version invented for the speaker's benefit. Charity and accuracy pull against each other, and the interpreter has to hold both.

## Evaluating reasons

**Against dismissing conclusions.** — A common failure is to judge an argument by whether one likes its conclusion. If the conclusion is congenial, the reasoning is waved through; if it is not, the whole thing is dismissed. Both are mistakes, and they are the same mistake in opposite directions. A bad argument can have a true conclusion: "The sky is blue because blue is the prettiest colour" asserts something true on a worthless ground. A good argument can have a false conclusion if one of its premises is false. The two questions — is the conclusion true, and do these premises support it — have to be kept apart.

**Fallacies of the person and the source.** — The *ad hominem* fallacy attacks the arguer rather than the argument; the genetic fallacy rejects a claim because of where it came from. Descartes's conclusion is not refuted by the fact that he was a seventeenth-century Catholic, nor Euthyphro's position by his self-importance. The reverse also fails: a claim is not established by the good character of the person who makes it. An appeal to authority is legitimate only when the authority is relevant and the claim lies within their competence, and even then it gives a reason to take the claim seriously rather than a proof of it.

**Evaluating rather than winning.** — To evaluate a set of reasons, a reader can work through a short checklist. Identify the conclusion. List the premises. Supply the unstated ones. Test the inference, asking whether the premises make the conclusion necessary or probable. Distinguish explanation from argument. Look for counterexamples. Ask what else would have to be true for the premises to hold. The aim is not to win a point but to see what the reasons actually support. The *Euthyphro* ends without a definition, yet it has produced a sharper question than it began with, and a clearer view of what an answer would have to do. When the subject is concepts rather than facts, that is what progress looks like.

**The Euthyphro dilemma as a working case.** — Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved? If the first, piety has a standard independent of the gods, and the gods are not the source of morality. If the second, piety is whatever the gods happen to love, and the content of morality depends on their will. Leibniz answered that God loves what is just because it is just, locating the standard in God's nature rather than in a decree. Kant, in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), located morality in reason and autonomy rather than in command. Neither answer closes the argument. Each shows what is at stake in a premise, and why evaluating the premise matters more than announcing the conclusion.

## Chronology

- **c. 470–399 BCE** — Socrates of Athens questions definitions of virtue, piety, and justice; he writes nothing.
- **399 BCE** — Socrates is tried and executed; the *Euthyphro* is set on the day of his preliminary hearing.
- **c. 390 BCE** — Plato writes the *Euthyphro* and the *Meno*, dialogues built around the demand for definitions.
- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle writes the *Prior Analytics*, the *Topics*, and the *Rhetoric*, which define the syllogism and the enthymeme.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Theophrastus and the later Peripatetics develop and comment on Aristotle's logic.
- **c. 480–524 CE** — Boethius translates and transmits Aristotelian logic to the Latin West.
- **1079–1142** — Peter Abelard works out the logic of conditionals and entailment.
- **c. 1287–1347** — William of Ockham writes on consequences and the logic of propositions.
- **1641** — Descartes publishes the *Meditations on First Philosophy*; Arnauld's Fourth Objections raise the Cartesian circle.
- **1686, 1710** — Leibniz states the view that God loves the just because it is just.
- **1785** — Kant grounds morality in reason and autonomy in the *Groundwork*.
- **1843** — J. S. Mill's *A System of Logic* treats the syllogism's major premise as a memorandum.
- **1914** — C. S. Peirce dies, leaving the account of abduction that later writers take up.
- **1948** — Hempel and Oppenheim publish the covering-law model of explanation.
- **1958** — Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* challenges the syllogistic model of real arguments.
- **1960** — Quine's *Word and Object* states the principle of charity.
- **1965** — Gilbert Harman names inference to the best explanation.
- **1969** — Quine's "Epistemology Naturalized" treats philosophy as continuous with science.
- **1973** — Davidson's "Radical Interpretation" and Grandy's "Reference, Meaning, and Belief" appear.
- **1977** — M. F. Burnyeat's "Socratic Midwifery, Platonic Inspiration" appears.
- **1983, 1991** — Gregory Vlastos publishes "The Socratic Elenchus" and *Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher*.

## Questions to carry

- What turns a question into a philosophical one, and who gets to decide where the empirical ends?
- When a speaker says "therefore," what would have to be true for the conclusion to follow?
- Which premises in the *Euthyphro* does Euthyphro never state, and does supplying them strengthen or weaken him?
- Is the difference between explaining an event and arguing for a claim a difference of form or only of purpose?
- Does the principle of charity make an interpreter more accurate, or only more generous?
