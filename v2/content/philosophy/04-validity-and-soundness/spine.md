# Validity and soundness

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

## Holding together and being true

**Two questions, not one.** — An argument is a set of statements in which some, the premises, are offered as support for another, the conclusion. Logicians ask two separate questions of it. The first is whether the conclusion follows from the premises — whether the premises, if they were true, would force the conclusion to be true. If they would, the argument is valid. The second is whether the premises are in fact true. An argument that passes both tests is sound. The two questions are independent, and that independence is the whole subject of this unit. An argument can be valid with every premise false; it can have all true premises and be invalid; it can be valid with false premises and a true conclusion, or valid with false premises and a false conclusion. Only one combination is impossible: a valid argument with true premises and a false conclusion.

**The stock example.** — The most repeated argument in the history of logic runs: All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal. Its form is: All M are P; S is M; therefore S is P. Because the form is valid, no substitution of terms can turn true premises into a false conclusion. But notice what the validity does not tell us. It does not tell us that all men are mortal. That is a separate claim, established by observation and inference, not by logic. If the first premise were false — if some men were immortal — the argument would remain valid and would prove nothing about Socrates. Validity is a conditional guarantee: it says that if the premises are true, the conclusion is. It never says that the premises are true.

**The contrast in miniature.** — (a) All cats are mammals; all dogs are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs. This is invalid, and the proof is that the premises are true and the conclusion false — the signature of invalidity. (b) All fish are mammals; all mammals breathe air; therefore all fish breathe air. This is valid, and its first premise is false. It is not sound, and a person who did not already know that fish breathe air would learn nothing true from it. A third case makes the point sharper. From the premises "two plus two equals five" and "two plus two does not equal five," anything at all follows. The inference is valid. It is not sound, and it can be used to prove any conclusion whatever, which is why a contradiction among premises is a disaster rather than a nuisance.

## Aristotle and the discovery of form

**The Prior Analytics.** — Aristotle (384–322 BCE) wrote the first systematic study of valid inference. The Prior Analytics, composed in the 350s or 340s BCE, opens by defining its subject: "A syllogism is discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so." Two features matter. First, the conclusion is something other than the premises, not one of them restated. Second, the consequence is necessary: given the premises, the conclusion cannot be otherwise. Aristotle distinguishes the perfect syllogism, in which the conclusion is plain from the premises as stated, from the imperfect, which needs further propositions to make the consequence evident.

**Terms and figures.** — Aristotle analyses a syllogism into three terms: the major term, the predicate of the conclusion; the minor term, its subject; and the middle term, which appears in both premises and vanishes from the conclusion. The arrangement of the middle term fixes the figure. In the first figure the middle is subject of one premise and predicate of the other; in the second it is predicate of both; in the third it is subject of both. The first figure yields the four perfect moods that medieval logicians named with the vowels of their mnemonic verses: Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), Celarent (No M is P; All S are M; therefore No S is P), Darii (All M are P; Some S is M; therefore Some S is P), and Ferio (No M is P; Some S is M; therefore Some S is not P). The vowels A, E, I, O stand for the four kinds of categorical statement: universal affirmative, universal negative, particular affirmative, particular negative. Every other valid categorical syllogism can be reduced to these four by conversion and by proof from an impossible assumption.

**Demonstration and mere syllogism.** — In the Posterior Analytics Aristotle draws the line that this unit is about. A demonstration, he says, is a syllogism that produces scientific knowledge; its premises must be "true, primary, immediate, better known than and prior to the conclusion." He adds at once: "Syllogism there may indeed be without these conditions, but such syllogism, not being productive of scientific knowledge, will not be demonstration." Here, in the fourth century BCE, is the distinction between a valid inference and a sound one, stated as the difference between a syllogism and a demonstration. A demonstration is a syllogism whose premises are true and basic; a syllogism is a form that may be filled with true premises, false premises, or a mixture. The form is the business of logic; the truth of the premises is the business of the particular science, or of observation.

**Why this was new.** — Earlier Greek thinkers had argued, and the sophists taught techniques of persuasion, but Aristotle separated the question whether an inference is good from the question what it is about. The same pattern of terms can carry a true conclusion about animals and a false one about numbers; what makes the inference good is the arrangement, the form. That separation is the founding move of formal logic.

## Form, content, and the counterexample

**What "form" means.** — To see the form of an argument, replace its substantive terms with letters. "If it is raining, the street is wet; it is raining; therefore the street is wet" becomes "If P then Q; P; therefore Q." The letters are placeholders; any sentences may be substituted for them, provided the substitution is consistent. The resulting schema, modus ponens, is valid: whatever P and Q are, true premises cannot yield a false conclusion. Other schemata are not. "If P then Q; Q; therefore P" — affirming the consequent — is invalid, as the substitution P for "it rained last night" and Q for "the street is wet" shows: the street may be wet because a pipe burst. "If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q" — denying the antecedent — is likewise invalid: the street may be wet though it did not rain. Modus tollens, by contrast, is valid: "If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P." If the street is not wet, it cannot have rained, granted the first premise.

**The counterexample method.** — To show that an argument is invalid, it is enough to exhibit another argument of the same form with true premises and a false conclusion. This is the counterexample method, and it is decisive, because validity is exactly the property that no substitution can defeat. Suppose someone argues: "Every student who works hard passes; Maria passed; therefore Maria worked hard." The form is affirming the consequent, and a counterexample makes the failure vivid: "Every human is a mammal; my cat is a mammal; therefore my cat is a human." The premises are true and the conclusion false, so the form is invalid, and the original argument, whatever its subject, inherits the defect. Aristotle himself used the method. In Prior Analytics I.4 he tests a candidate inference with the terms animal, man, horse, and again with animal, man, stone, and with science, line, medicine, to show that the same premises can be combined with opposite conclusions and that no necessary consequence follows. The terms are placeholders chosen to make the failure visible.

**The undistributed middle.** — The most common invalid categorical form has a name. "All dogs are mammals; all cats are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs" fails because the middle term, mammals, is never claimed to cover its whole extension; it is undistributed. The form looks like Barbara and is not. The fallacy is easy to see once the form is laid out and nearly invisible when it is buried in a paragraph. Whately's textbook made the point with "every horse is an animal; sheep are not horses; therefore they are not animals," set beside "all vegetables grow; an animal grows; therefore it is a vegetable."

**Validity is about the skeleton, not the flesh.** — This is why logicians can talk about validity without knowing anything about the subject of the argument. Whether Maria worked hard is a question for the registrar; whether the argument is valid is a question about its shape. The two inquiries use different evidence and can be pursued separately. That separation is powerful, and it is also the source of the unease that the next section describes.

## Why a valid argument can tell you nothing

**Logic is truth-preserving, not truth-producing.** — A valid argument is a machine for transmitting truth from premises to conclusion. It cannot manufacture truth. Feed it false premises and it will faithfully transmit falsehood. This is the answer, in one sentence, to the organizing question: an argument can be perfectly valid and tell us nothing true because validity guarantees only the connection between premises and conclusion, not the truth of either. The guarantee is real and useful — it means that a valid argument cannot lead from truth to falsehood — but it is conditional, and the condition is supplied from outside logic.

**The classical worry.** — The gap was noticed early. If the conclusion of a valid syllogism is already contained in the premises, does the syllogism prove anything new? John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) put the objection sharply in A System of Logic (1843). In every syllogism, he wrote, "considered as an argument to prove the conclusion, there is a petitio principii" — a begging of the question. When we reason that all men are mortal, and Socrates is a man, and therefore Socrates is mortal, the conclusion is presupposed in the general premise: we cannot be sure that all men are mortal unless we are already sure of every individual case, Socrates included. If so, the general premise does no evidential work. Mill's own solution was that the real inference lies not in the descent from the general premise to the particular conclusion but in the ascent from observed particulars to the general premise; the syllogism is a registering device, a way of recording what has already been inferred, not a method of discovery. Whether Mill was right is disputed, but his statement of the problem remains the clearest.

**What a proof is.** — The dispute about the syllogism has a second layer: what kind of thing a proof is. The logician Jan Łukasiewicz, in Aristotle's Syllogistic from the Standpoint of Modern Formal Logic (1951), read Aristotle's syllogistic as an axiomatic system: the perfect first-figure moods are axioms, the rest theorems derived from them. Critics, among them John Corcoran and Timothy Smiley, replied that Aristotle wrote a proof procedure closer to natural deduction, reasoning from assumptions by explicit rules. The debate concerns the form of Aristotle's logic, not whether the syllogisms are valid; both sides agree that Barbara and Celarent are valid and that a valid argument may have false premises.

**Two senses of "good argument."** — It helps to distinguish the logician's sense of a good argument from the reasoner's. In the logician's sense, an argument is good if it is valid, or valid with true premises. In the reasoner's sense, an argument is good if it is valid and its premises are known or justified, so that it can move someone from knowledge to new knowledge. The first is a formal property; the second depends on the standing of the premises, which logic does not supply. A valid argument with premises nobody has reason to accept may be a perfect specimen of reasoning and a useless piece of persuasion.

## Definitions and the demand for essence

**What a definition is for.** — A definition says what a word means, or, more ambitiously, what the thing named is. The Socratic dialogues turn on the second kind. When Socrates asks "What is piety?" or "What is courage?" or "What is justice?", he is not asking for a list of examples or a dictionary gloss. In the Euthyphro he makes the demand explicit: "Remember that I did not ask you to give me two or three examples of piety, but to explain the general idea which makes all pious things to be pious." A definition, in this sense, must state the feature that belongs to everything of the kind and to nothing else, and explain why things of that kind are what they are. Later philosophers express the first two conditions as necessary and sufficient conditions: a definition of X gives conditions such that anything with them is an X, and anything without them is not.

**Necessary and sufficient conditions.** — A condition is necessary for X if X cannot occur without it; sufficient if its occurrence guarantees X. "Being an unmarried adult male" is necessary and sufficient for being a bachelor in the conventional sense. Each condition can be tested separately, and each test is a search for a counterexample. Is being male necessary? If the word is used only of men, yes. Is being unmarried sufficient? A five-year-old boy is unmarried and male but not a bachelor; adulthood is doing further work. Definitions fail by admitting too much, when the conditions are not sufficient, or too little, when they are not necessary. The same counterexample method that tests validity tests a definition. It also shows why a definition cannot be a mere list: "a triangle is a figure with three sides" states what makes a figure a triangle, while "a triangle is a figure that pleases geometers" does not, even if true of every triangle.

**The Euthyphro's definitions.** — Euthyphro offers a series of answers, each failing in an instructive way. First he says piety is doing what he is doing — prosecuting a wrongdoer, even his own father. That is an example, not a definition. Then he says piety is what is dear to the gods; but the gods quarrel, and what one loves another hates, so the same action would be both pious and impious. Refined to "what all the gods love," the answer survives that objection but falls to a different one. Socrates asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved. The answer, Euthyphro agrees, is the former: the gods love it because it is already pious. Then "being loved by the gods" cannot be what piety is; it is a consequence of piety, not its essence. Euthyphro has given an attribute, a pathos, rather than an essence, an ousia. The argument that draws the distinction is still called the Euthyphro dilemma.

**Definitions and the priority of definition.** — The dialogues assume that a definition has priority: that one must know what piety is before one can know of any particular act that it is pious. This assumption has been attacked. The philosopher Peter Geach, in "Plato's Euthyphro: An Analysis and Commentary" (1966), called it the "Socratic fallacy." Geach's point is that people routinely and correctly apply a term without being able to define it — one can know that a particular act is cruel, or that a particular colour is red, without being able to state the essence of cruelty or of red. If so, Socrates' demand that Euthyphro produce a definition before he can be sure he is acting piously sets an impossible and unnecessary condition. The counter-argument, made by defenders of Socrates, is that the priority claim is about expert knowledge, not about ordinary recognition: only someone claiming the kind of mastery that licenses teaching and correcting others needs to be able to give an account of the essence.

## The elenchus, and how to test a definition

**The method.** — The Socratic elenchus, the cross-examination practised in Plato's early dialogues, is the historical ancestor of the counterexample method applied to beliefs. The interlocutor asserts a thesis — "piety is what the gods love." Socrates secures agreement to further premises the interlocutor accepts — that the gods quarrel, that what one god loves another hates, that the pious is loved because it is pious and not the reverse. From these premises there follows the negation of the original thesis. The interlocutor is refuted out of his own mouth, without Socrates asserting a doctrine of his own. The dialogue ends not in a proof but in aporia, a puzzlement in which the interlocutor no longer knows what to say. The Euthyphro ends this way: Euthyphro pleads a hurry and leaves, and Socrates complains that his arguments, like the statues of his ancestor Daedalus, "walk away and will not remain fixed."

**What the elenchus proves.** — The elenchus is an argument from consistency. It shows that a set of beliefs cannot all be true together; it does not show which member is false, and it establishes no positive thesis. This is the crux of a long dispute. Gregory Vlastos, in "The Socratic Elenchus" (1983), argued that Socrates treats a successful elenchus as more than a consistency test — as establishing that the thesis refuted is false and its contradictory true, and so as a route to positive moral knowledge. Critics, including Hugh Benson and Michael Frede, replied that the method can at most convict an interlocutor of confusion; it cannot certify a positive doctrine, because the premises have no authority beyond the interlocutor's agreement. The dispute is unresolved, and it turns on how much weight one gives to the fact that a belief has survived examination.

**Why it matters here.** — The elenchus is a machine for finding counterexamples in definitions, and it exposes the gap this unit has been tracing. A refutation is a valid argument whose conclusion is the negation of a thesis, and it is only as good as its premises. If the premises are the interlocutor's own beliefs, the refutation shows that he is inconsistent; it does not show that the world is as the refutation says. The elenchus, like the syllogism, preserves truth and cannot manufacture it. Socrates seems to have accepted this, and to have kept examining himself and others because the method delivers the exposure of error rather than a supply of truth.

## Reading an argument out of prose

**Reconstruction.** — Real arguments rarely arrive in the tidy three lines of a syllogism. They arrive in paragraphs, with premises out of order, some suppressed, and the conclusion stated first or not at all. The first task is reconstruction. Find the conclusion — ask what the passage is trying to establish. Find the stated premises, then supply the suppressed ones the author takes for granted; an argument with a missing premise is called an enthymeme. Then set the argument out in numbered form so the inference can be inspected. A charitable reconstruction supplies the strongest premise the author could reasonably have meant, not the weakest. Whately called the syllogism "not a peculiar kind of argument, but only a peculiar form of expression, in which every argument may be stated."

**A worked case.** — Take a short passage of the kind found in a legal opinion: "The defendant was in the house at the time of the theft. No one else had a key. The safe was opened without force. Therefore the defendant is guilty." Reconstructed: (1) the defendant was in the house; (2) no one else had a key; (3) the safe was opened without force; (4) whoever was in the house with a key and opened the safe without force took the contents; therefore the defendant took the contents. The inference is valid as reconstructed. Soundness is another matter. Premise 4 is an empirical generalisation with exceptions — a key may have been copied, a window left open, the safe opened earlier — and the second premise may be false. The argument may be valid and unsound. The reconstruction is where most of the work happens: the validity of the finished schema can be checked mechanically, but which premises to attribute, and which to challenge, is judgement.

**Where prose arguments go wrong.** — Four failures recur. Equivocation: a term shifts meaning between premises, so the argument has four terms where it needs three — "The end of a thing is its perfection; death is the end of life; therefore death is the perfection of life," trading on two senses of "end." Begging the question: the conclusion, or something equivalent, is smuggled into a premise. Non sequitur by form: affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent. And the false dilemma: the premises exclude options never excluded. Each can be diagnosed with the tools above — the counterexample for formal failures, the necessary-and-sufficient test for the definitions on which material failures turn. Whately brought forward a parallel argument whose conclusion is obviously absurd; the modern logician lays the form bare and reads off the failure.

## From the syllogism to modern logic

**What Aristotle's logic could not do.** — The syllogistic is a theory of arguments built from categorical statements about classes: all, no, some. It is complete for that domain, and it dominated logic for more than two thousand years, transmitted to the Latin Middle Ages through Boethius (c. 480–524 CE) and elaborated by William of Sherwood and Peter of Spain in the thirteenth century. But it cannot easily represent arguments that turn on relations — "every boy loves some girl," "x is taller than y" — or on multiple generality, and it handles the propositional connectives (if, and, or, not) less freely than the Stoics had begun to. Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) supplied a notation that could, and the predicate calculus of Frege, Bertrand Russell, and Alfred North Whitehead's Principia Mathematica (1910–1913) became the standard logic of the twentieth century. Modern logic tests validity by truth tables for the connectives and by countermodels for the quantifiers; the method is more powerful but the same, a search for a substitution that makes the premises true and the conclusion false.

**The form/content distinction survives the change.** — What survives every change of notation is the distinction on which the subject rests. An argument is valid in virtue of its form; a counterexample is another argument of the same form with true premises and a false conclusion; soundness adds the truth of the premises; no amount of formal perfection can supply what only the world can supply. Frege's logic is vastly more powerful than Aristotle's, but it is truth-preserving in the same sense and leaves the same gap between a valid argument and a true conclusion. The gap is not a defect of any system. It is what a logic is.

## Chronology

- **c. 399 BCE** — Socrates is tried and executed at Athens; the elenchus is portrayed in Plato's early dialogues.
- **c. 390 BCE** — Plato writes the Euthyphro, set on the eve of Socrates' trial.
- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle composes the Prior Analytics and the Posterior Analytics, defining the syllogism and the demonstration.
- **c. 335–322 BCE** — Aristotle's logical treatises are collected as the Organon.
- **c. 260 BCE** — Chrysippus and the Stoics develop a logic of whole propositions (if, and, or, not).
- **c. 500 CE** — Boethius translates and transmits Aristotle's logic to the Latin West.
- **c. 1250** — William of Sherwood and Peter of Spain write the medieval logic textbooks.
- **1662** — The Port-Royal Logic of Arnauld and Nicole appears.
- **1843** — John Stuart Mill publishes A System of Logic and states the petitio principii objection; Whately's Elements of Logic is in its ninth edition.
- **1879** — Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift introduces modern predicate logic.
- **1910–1913** — Russell and Whitehead publish Principia Mathematica.
- **1951** — Jan Łukasiewicz reads Aristotle's syllogistic as an axiomatic system.
- **1966** — Peter Geach names the "Socratic fallacy."
- **1983** — Gregory Vlastos publishes "The Socratic Elenchus."

## Questions to carry

- If a valid argument can have false premises, what work is left for validity to do?
- Mill says the syllogism begs the question. Does the objection show that deduction cannot discover anything, or only that discovery happens somewhere else?
- A definition must exclude what is not of the kind. Where does the Euthyphro's definition of piety fail, and could any definition of a virtue pass the same test?
- The elenchus refutes a thesis by showing it inconsistent with other beliefs. What does that prove about the thesis, and what does it leave untouched?
- Frege's logic is far stronger than Aristotle's. Does the greater power change what counts as a good argument, or only how many arguments can be checked?
