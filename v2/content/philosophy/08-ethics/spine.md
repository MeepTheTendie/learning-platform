# Ethics: virtue, duty, and consequences

**Period:** Antiquity to the nineteenth century

## The question

**How should one live?** — In the *Republic*, written about 380 BCE, Plato stages the question as a wager. Glaucon tells the story of Gyges, a shepherd in Lydia who finds a bronze ring, discovers that turning it makes him invisible, and uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king, and take the throne. Suppose, Glaucon says, that two such rings were given, one to a just man and one to an unjust man: who would keep his hands off what he could take unseen? The story is a challenge, not a proof. It asks whether justice is worth having for its own sake or only for its reputation and rewards, and it forces the reader to say what makes an act right at all. Plato's answer, in the rest of the *Republic*, is that justice is a condition of the soul, a harmony of its parts, and that the just man is better off even if he is thought unjust. That answer is one of the first great theories in ethics. It is not the last.

**Three families of answer.** — Across the twenty-three centuries between Plato and Mill, thinkers around the Mediterranean and in Europe proposed many answers to how one should live, but most fall into three families. The first locates rightness in the character of the person who acts: an act is right when it is what a virtuous person would do. The second locates rightness in the rule the act obeys: an act is right when it conforms to duty, whatever the consequences. The third locates rightness in the results the act produces: an act is right when it brings about the best outcome, usually measured by happiness. The names are modern — virtue ethics, deontology, consequentialism — but the positions are ancient. The organizing question is which of the three has the best claim: is an act right because of its character, its rule, or its results? The three families agree in most ordinary cases and come apart in the hard ones, and the hard ones are where a theory earns its keep.

**Before the philosophers.** — Greek ethics did not begin in the schools. It began in a world of competing codes: the warrior's honour of the Homeric poems, the civic law of the city-state, the household duties of the *oikos*, and the purification rites of Greek religion. The Homeric hero is judged by his excellence (*aretē*) — strength, courage, skill in speech and war — and by the shame he incurs before his peers. The word *aretē* means excellence of any kind, of a knife as much as of a man, and carries no suggestion of a rule. When the sophists of the fifth century BCE travelled from city to city teaching rhetoric for a fee, they made the question sharper. Protagoras is reported to have said that "man is the measure of all things," which can be read as a claim that there is no standard above custom, and Callicles in Plato's *Gorgias* argues that the strong should rule by nature. The philosophers who followed — Socrates, Plato, Aristotle — were partly answering that challenge.

## Virtue ethics: Aristotle

**The function of a human being.** — Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was born at Stagira in northern Greece, the son of a physician to the Macedonian court, and came to Athens at seventeen to study with Plato. He stayed twenty years, then returned in 335 BCE to found his own school, the Lyceum. The *Nicomachean Ethics*, named after his son Nicomachus, is the first sustained treatise on ethics in the West. It begins from a simple observation: every art and every inquiry aims at some good. Medicine aims at health, shipbuilding at a vessel, strategy at victory. If there is an end wanted for its own sake and not for anything further, that will be the chief good. Aristotle calls it *eudaimonia* — from *eu*, "well," and *daimon*, a spirit or lot, usually translated "happiness," though "flourishing" comes closer, because it names a life, not a feeling.

**The function argument.** — What is eudaimonia? To answer, Aristotle asks what the *ergon*, the function or characteristic work, of a human being is. A flute-player has a function, and a good flute-player performs it well; an eye has a function, and a good eye sees well. What is the human function? Not nutrition, which plants share; not perception, which animals share. It is activity of the soul in accordance with reason. If that is the function, then the human good is the good performance of it: "activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete." He adds a condition that is easy to miss: "in a complete life." One swallow does not make a summer. A single brave act, or one good year, does not make a happy life; eudaimonia is the shape of a whole life, and it needs external goods — friends, health, a measure of prosperity — because it is hard to act nobly with no equipment at all.

**Virtue as a mean.** — The virtues of character (*ēthikai aretai*) are the excellences that make this activity good. They are not inborn. We are adapted by nature to receive them, but we acquire them by practice: "men become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." Habit (*ethos*) is the root of the word *ēthikē*, ethics. Aristotle defines virtue as a state of character concerned with choice, "lying in a mean ... determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." Courage is the mean between rashness and cowardice; temperance between self-indulgence and insensibility; liberality in giving between prodigality and meanness. The mean is not a fixed average: what is too much food for a beginner is too little for Milo, the famous wrestler of Croton who won six Olympic victories, and the same quantity is not right for every body. It is relative to the person and the situation. Nor does every act admit of a mean. "Spite, shamelessness, envy, and in the case of actions adultery, theft, murder" are named as already bad; there is no right way to commit murder. The faculty that finds the mean in particular cases is *phronesis*, practical wisdom, which cannot be reduced to a rule, because the particulars must be perceived.

**The dispute over the mean.** — Whether the doctrine of the mean gives real guidance is contested. Bernard Williams complained that it is not a usable standard: if you do not already know which acts are just, being told to hit the mean between two extremes tells you nothing. Julia Annas replies that the doctrine is not meant as a decision procedure but as a description of what a good character is like, and that the work of guidance is done by the virtues themselves and by *phronesis*. The dispute is not about Aristotle's text alone; it is about whether ethics should supply rules at all.

**The Stoics and the Epicureans.** — Two later schools carried eudaimonism in different directions. Epicurus (341–270 BCE) called pleasure the goal but meant chiefly the absence of pain (*aponia*) and disturbance (*ataraxia*). The Stoics, founded by Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE), held that virtue alone is good and everything else "indifferent." Epictetus (c. 50–135 CE), a former slave, taught that what is up to us is our judgment and will; the *Discourses*, recorded by his pupil Arrian, insist that grief comes from judging events, not from events themselves. For both schools the question remains what makes a life go well.

## The rule in the world: divine command and natural law

**The Euthyphro dilemma.** — Long before Kant, Greek religion raised the question whether morality depends on the gods or on something independent of them. In Plato's *Euthyphro*, set at the trial of Socrates in 399 BCE, Socrates asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods. The distinction is fatal to the simplest divine-command theory. If actions are good only because God commands them, then God could have commanded cruelty, and cruelty would have been good; the theory seems to make morality arbitrary. If God commands actions because they are already good, then goodness is independent of God, and God is not its source. The dilemma does not by itself refute every divine-command view — a defender may say that God's nature is necessarily good and so cannot command cruelty, as Robert Adams has argued in a modified version of the theory — but it sets the terms of the debate, and it recurs wherever a law is said to rest on a will.

**Natural law.** — The most influential attempt to answer it in the Middle Ages was natural law. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), a Dominican friar who taught at Paris and Naples, set it out in the *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274). Law, he says, is a rule and measure of action. The eternal law is God's reason governing the universe; the natural law is "the participation of the rational creature in the eternal law," the share of it that human beings can grasp by reason. Its first precept is that good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided; from it follow the basic goods of self-preservation, procreation, knowledge, and social life. Human law is valid only so far as it derives from natural law. Conscience, for Aquinas, is the application of this knowledge to particular acts. The natural-law tradition also gave later thinkers the principle of double effect, by which an act with one intended good effect and a foreseen bad one may sometimes be permitted — a framework still used in just-war theory and medical ethics. Whether natural law needs God at all was already disputed: Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), in *On the Law of War and Peace* (1625), held that natural law would retain its force even on the supposition that there is no God (*etiamsi daremus non esse Deum*), a remark that scandalised some readers and founded the modern secular reading.

## Deontology: Kant

**Duty and the good will.** — Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) spent his whole life in Königsberg, in East Prussia, and published the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* in 1785, three years before the *Critique of Practical Reason*. He begins with a claim that had no precedent in the ancient tradition: "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will." Talents, courage, health, wealth, even happiness, can be used badly; only the good will is good in itself. And the good will is good not because of what it achieves — "not because of what it performs or effects" — but because of the principle on which it acts. Even if it accomplishes nothing, it "would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself."

**Duty versus inclination.** — To isolate the good will, Kant analyses duty. Many acts conform to duty but are done from self-interest: the prudent shopkeeper who does not overcharge a child does so because his business depends on a fixed price, not because honesty is required. Such an act is useful but has no moral worth. Kant's test is the motive: an action has moral worth only when it is done from duty, "the necessity of acting from respect for the law." Kant does not admire coldness; he says a person who helps others from sympathy does something amiable. But sympathy is a contingent feeling, and morality cannot rest on a feeling that may fail. Critics from Schiller onward have found something chilling in this. Kant's reply is that the law, not the feeling, is the ground of obligation.

**The categorical imperative.** — What law can bind without condition? Not a hypothetical imperative, which tells you what to do if you want some end — if you want health, exercise. A categorical imperative commands unconditionally. Kant states it: "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law." The test is whether the principle on which you propose to act (the *maxim*) can be willed as a law for everyone. His example is a lying promise. To get out of a difficulty, a man considers promising falsely. Ask whether the maxim could be a universal law. It could not, because if everyone made false promises when convenient, no one would believe promises, and the practice would collapse; the maxim destroys the very institution it relies on. The contradiction is not that lying makes the world worse, Kant insists, but that the maxim cannot be universalised without cancelling itself.

**Persons as ends.** — Kant gives the same imperative a second formulation, which many readers find more intuitive: "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." Rational beings are not mere things with a price; they are persons with dignity, whose nature marks them out as ends in themselves. To deceive, coerce, or use another purely as an instrument is to treat a person as a thing. From the same principle Kant draws the idea of a "kingdom of ends," a community of rational beings legislating the moral law together, and the notion of autonomy — the will giving itself the law. Duty is not imposed from outside; it is self-legislation by reason.

**The hard case: the murderer at the door.** — In 1797 the French writer Benjamin Constant published a challenge: if a murderer asks whether your friend is hiding in your house, must you tell the truth? Constant said no; the duty to truth binds only where it does not harm others. Kant replied in "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" that the duty of truthfulness is unconditional, and that even to a would-be murderer one must not lie. He adds a subtle point: one may refuse to answer, or say something that does not deceive, but one must not speak falsely, because a lie corrupts the very ground of right. Many interpreters, among them Onora O'Neill, argue that Kant's position is defensible once the options are described carefully — silence and evasion are not lies — while others, including Constant, take it as proof that a rule without exceptions is inhuman. The case is the clearest test of whether rightness lies in the rule or in the result.

## Consequences: Bentham and Mill

**The greatest happiness.** — The third family judges acts by their results, and its most famous form is utilitarianism. Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), an English legal reformer, put the principle in the first sentence of his *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780, published 1789): "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure." Right action tends to produce the greatest happiness of the greatest number; wrong action the reverse. Bentham proposed a "felicific calculus" to weigh pleasures by intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent, and applied the principle to law, prisons, and animals — his famous remark being that the question is not whether animals can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer.

**Mill's refinement.** — John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) was educated by his father James Mill, a follower of Bentham, so severely that he suffered a breakdown at twenty. His *Utilitarianism* appeared in *Fraser's Magazine* in 1861 and as a book in 1863. Mill restates the principle — "actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness" — and adds a distinction that Bentham lacked: pleasures differ in kind as well as in quantity. Some pleasures are higher, and the test of quality is the preference of those who have experienced both. "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Mill also insists that the standard is not the agent's own happiness but "the greatest amount of happiness altogether," and that the agent must be "as strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator." In *On Liberty* (1859) he applied the principle to the limits of state power through the harm principle: interference with a person's liberty is justified only to prevent harm to others.

**The proof and its critics.** — Mill's attempt to prove the principle has drawn fire. He argues that the only evidence a thing is visible is that people see it, and the only evidence a thing is desirable is that people desire it; since each person desires his own happiness, the general happiness is desirable for the aggregate. Critics, from G. E. Moore in 1903 to many since, say this commits a fallacy: the fact that each desires his own happiness does not show that each desires the general happiness. Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900), in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), gave the school its most rigorous statement, and found in it a "dualism of practical reason": egoism and universal benevolence are both reasonable, and reason alone seems unable to decide between them. Whether utilitarianism should judge acts one by one (act utilitarianism) or by rules (rule utilitarianism, defended later by R. M. Hare and Richard Brandt) remains open.

## Objectivism and relativism

**Custom is king.** — The three families all assume that some acts are right and others wrong. A fourth position denies it. Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, reports that the Persian king Darius asked Greeks and Callatians what they did with their dead. The Greeks burned their fathers; the Callatians ate them. Each was horrified by the other's custom. Herodotus draws the lesson that "custom is king of all," and that each people thinks its own ways best. Montaigne, in the essay "Of Cannibals" (1580), made the same point about the Tupinambá of Brazil: "every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country." If morality is only custom, there is nothing to say to a slaveholder or a torturer except that our customs differ, and moral criticism loses its footing.

**The objectivist reply.** — Objectivists hold that some moral claims are true or false independently of what anyone thinks. Plato replied to Protagoras that if "man is the measure," then the doctrine is true only for those who believe it, and refutes itself when applied to itself. The seventeenth- and eighteenth-century British rationalists — Samuel Clarke, Richard Price, Thomas Reid — held that moral truths are self-evident to reason, like mathematical ones. David Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) then observed that writers move without explanation from statements of what is to statements of what ought to be, and asked how that transition is possible — the "is–ought" gap. In the twentieth century G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) named the "naturalistic fallacy," the attempt to define good in terms of a natural property such as pleasure, and J. L. Mackie's *Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) argued that moral values are not part of the fabric of the world. Defenders of objectivity include Philippa Foot, whose *Natural Goodness* (2001) grounds moral evaluation in the characteristic life of a species, and Derek Parfit, whose *On What Matters* (2011) defends objective reasons. The dispute matters for the other three families: if there are no objective moral facts, then virtue, duty, and consequences are ways of organising a preference rather than discovering a truth.

**The relativist case.** — The relativist's strongest evidence is the sheer variety of moral codes. The record includes societies that practised infanticide, widow-burning, human sacrifice, and slavery, and others that condemned all of these. But variety of belief does not by itself prove that there is no truth: people once disagreed about the shape of the earth, and one side was right. The argument from disagreement shows that moral knowledge is hard and that custom shapes perception; it does not show that there is nothing to know.

## The three approaches on one case

**The case.** — Set the hard case plainly. A friend is hiding in your house. A man who intends to kill him knocks and asks whether he is inside. You may tell the truth, lie, or say nothing. The three families give three different kinds of answer.

**The virtue answer.** — The virtue ethicist asks what a person of good character would do, here and now. Loyalty, courage, and practical wisdom all point toward protecting the friend; honesty pulls the other way. The virtuous person does not consult a rule but perceives the situation, as Aristotle says, with the eye of *phronesis*, and finds the fitting act. She might say nothing, or misdirect, or stand in the doorway, choosing the response that a brave and loyal person would choose. The answer is particular, and it depends on the character of the agent; two virtuous people might act differently, and that is not obviously a failure.

**The rule answer.** — The Kantian asks what duty requires. Kant's own answer is that truthfulness is an unconditional duty, and that lying, even to the murderer, wrongs humanity by undermining the institution of truthful speech; one may refuse to answer, but not lie. A different deontologist might hold that the duty to protect the innocent outweighs the duty not to deceive, and so permit the lie; the dispute is about which duties are absolute and how they rank. What unites the family is that the act is judged by its conformity to a principle, not by how the story ends.

**The results answer.** — The utilitarian asks which act produces the greatest happiness. Here the calculation is stark: if you tell the truth, the friend is likely killed, with terrible consequences for him and for those who love him; if you lie, the murderer is thwarted. The lie seems plainly required, and a rule-utilitarian would add that a general practice of lying to murderers does not corrode trust in the way a general practice of lying does. Bentham would run the calculus; Mill would add that the higher pleasures of friendship and security weigh heavily on the side of protection.

**Why it matters.** — The case shows that the three families are not three names for the same advice. They agree that one should rescue the friend; they disagree about why, and the disagreement becomes visible only when the reasons conflict. If the utilitarian is right, the Kantian is not merely mistaken but cruel; if the Kantian is right, the utilitarian sacrifices a principle for a result and cannot say why anyone should keep a promise when breaking it pays. The history of ethics is, in large part, the record of that argument.

## Chronology

- **c. 470–399 BCE** — Socrates teaches in Athens; the question of how to live is pursued by cross-examination.
- **c. 390 BCE** — Plato writes the *Euthyphro* (the piety dilemma) and, soon after, the *Republic* (the ring of Gyges).
- **384–322 BCE** — The life of Aristotle; he lectures at the Lyceum from 335 BCE.
- **c. 350 BCE** — The *Nicomachean Ethics* defines eudaimonia and virtue as a mean.
- **c. 334–262 BCE** — Zeno of Citium founds Stoicism.
- **341–270 BCE** — Epicurus teaches in Athens; pleasure is redefined as tranquillity.
- **c. 50–135 CE** — Epictetus teaches Stoic ethics; Arrian records the *Discourses*.
- **c. 160–210 CE** — Sextus Empiricus records the sceptical case against any criterion of truth.
- **1225–1274** — Thomas Aquinas; the *Summa Theologiae* sets out natural law.
- **c. 1287–1347** — William of Ockham; voluntarism sharpens the divine-command reading.
- **1580** — Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" questions the judging of other customs.
- **1625** — Grotius, *On the Law of War and Peace*: natural law binds even without God.
- **1739–40** — Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* states the is–ought gap.
- **1785** — Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*.
- **1789** — Bentham's *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*.
- **1797** — Constant challenges Kant over lying to a murderer; Kant replies.
- **1859** — Mill's *On Liberty*.
- **1861/1863** — Mill's *Utilitarianism* serialised, then published as a book.
- **1874** — Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*.

## Questions to carry

- Does Aristotle's doctrine of the mean tell you what to do, or only what kind of person to be?
- Can the categorical imperative decide a case without smuggling in a judgment about consequences?
- If utilitarianism is right, is there anything you may never do, whatever the results?
- Does the Euthyphro dilemma refute divine command theory, or only its crudest form?
- When the three approaches disagree, what would show that one of them is correct?
