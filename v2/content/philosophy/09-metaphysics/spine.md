# Metaphysics: what exists?

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

## The question

**What there is.** — The organizing question is what has to exist for the world to be the way it appears. A table looks solid and uniformly brown. Physics says it is mostly empty space, its atoms in constant motion, and the brown a response of the eye and brain to reflected light. So which is real — the table of ordinary experience, or the particles and fields of the physicist? Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy that asks what exists, what kinds of things there are, and what it is for something to exist at all. Its older name for the first of these inquiries is ontology, from the Greek *on*, "being," and *logos*, "account." The questions are stubborn. Are there minds as well as bodies? Are there properties — redness, justice — over and above the red or just things? Is a person the same person tomorrow as today? Does every event have a cause, and if so, can any choice be free? Is time real, or a feature of how things are experienced? The subject is usually dated from Aristotle, whose lectures on first principles were placed after his *Physics* in an ancient edition and so acquired the name *ta meta ta physika*, "the things after the physics."

**A concrete case.** — Take two tomatoes on a table, both the same shade of red. Call each of them a particular: this tomato, that tomato. Call redness a universal: the one property both are said to share. The question whether the universal exists is not idle. If redness is real and one, then two different things can literally be the same in a respect; if redness is only a word, then what the two tomatoes share is a resemblance and a name, nothing more. Medieval writers called the dispute the problem of universals, and the three standard answers are realism (universals exist), conceptualism (they exist only as concepts in minds), and nominalism (only particulars exist, and general words are conveniences). The argument has run for twenty-four centuries and is not over. It is the cleanest illustration of the method: take something the world seems to contain, and ask what has to be true for the appearance to be possible.

**Appearance and reality.** — The distinction between how things seem and how they are is older than Aristotle. Parmenides of Elea, active around 500 BCE, argued that what is cannot come into being or pass away, that it is one and unchanging, and that the senses, which report change and plurality, are therefore unreliable. His pupil Zeno produced paradoxes to defend the position: the swift Achilles can never overtake the tortoise, because while he runs to where the tortoise was, it has moved on; an arrow in flight is at rest at every instant, and so never moves. The paradoxes turn on how a continuous magnitude is divided, and they were not fully answered until the mathematics of the nineteenth century. But the move they embody is the founding move of metaphysics: if the senses and reason conflict, reason may be the better guide to what there is.

## Universals and particulars

**Plato's Forms.** — Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) taught that the objects of the senses are not fully real. What is fully real is the Forms: the Beautiful itself, the Equal itself, the Good itself, existing apart from the many beautiful, equal, or good things that merely participate in them. In the *Republic*, written around 375 BCE, he sets out the contrast as a difference between knowledge and opinion. Those who see only the many beautiful things, and cannot follow a guide to absolute beauty, have opinion; those who see the absolute, eternal, and immutable have knowledge. The Forms are the objects of knowledge, the sensibles are the objects of opinion, and the two are kept apart. The doctrine answers the problem of universals with full-blooded realism: universals are more real than the particulars that share them.

**The cave.** — Plato's most famous image is the allegory of the cave in Book VII of the *Republic*. Prisoners are chained from childhood in an underground den, facing a wall, able to see only the shadows cast by objects carried past a fire behind them. They take the shadows for reality. One is released, is pained and dazzled, and must be dragged up the steep ascent into the upper world; last of all he sees the sun and reasons about it. The cave is "the world of sight," the ascent is "the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world," and the sun stands for the Form of the Good, which is "the immediate source of reason and truth in the intellectual." The image explains why ordinary people mistake appearance for reality, why education is difficult and unwelcome, and it makes a claim about what really exists. Plato's answer to the organizing question is that the world of change depends on a world of unchanging Forms the senses cannot reach.

**Aristotle's categories.** — Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Plato's pupil, accepted that there are universals but denied that they exist apart from things. His *Categories* sorts everything that can be said of a thing into ten kinds: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, and passion. The first, substance (*ousia*), is primary: a particular man or horse exists in its own right, while its paleness, its height, and its being in the market exist only as features of it. He distinguishes what is "said of a subject" from what is "present in a subject." A species, man, is said of the individual Socrates; a colour is present in a body and cannot exist without one. On this account the universal man exists, but only in the individual men who are said to be men. This is immanent realism, and it answers Plato by putting the form back inside the thing.

**Form, matter, and cause.** — Aristotle explains a particular substance as a compound of matter (*hyle*) and form (*eidos*). A bronze sphere is bronze — the matter — organised into sphericity, the form. Change is the coming-to-be of a form in matter, and to explain a change fully one must give four causes: the material (what it is made of), the efficient (what produced it), the formal (what it is), and the final (what it is for). A house has bricks as its matter, a builder as its efficient cause, a shelter as its form, and protection as its end. He also turned on his teacher the argument later called the "third man": if the many men are men by sharing in the Form of Man, then a second Form is needed to explain what they share with the first, and a third to explain that, and so on. Aristotle's metaphysics keeps Plato's insight — that there is genuine knowledge of universals — without multiplying worlds.

**After Aristotle.** — The debate was transmitted to the Latin Middle Ages through Porphyry's *Isagoge* (c. 270 CE), which asks whether genera and species exist in reality or only in thought. Boethius (c. 477–524) translated it and posed the question sharply. Peter Abelard (1079–1142) defended a conceptualism: universals are real as concepts and as the common natures of things, but not as separate entities. William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) held that only individuals exist and that universals are signs; his principle that entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity is Ockham's razor. In the twentieth century the argument was renewed in the language of logic. Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell analysed generality through concepts and quantification rather than through a separate realm of Forms. W. V. O. Quine, in "On What There Is" (1948), argued that the question of what exists is the question of what a theory's quantifiers range over — "to be is to be the value of a variable" — and that the apparent names of non-existent things can be paraphrased away, so that Plato's heaven is an unnecessary postulate unless the best theory requires it. David Armstrong (1989) defended a modern immanent realism: universals exist, but only as properties of particulars. Nominalists and trope theorists continue to argue that particulars and their particular properties are enough.

## Substance and accident

**Substance as bearer.** — If a thing has properties, what has them? The grammar of "Socrates is pale" suggests a subject, Socrates, and a predicate, pale. Substance is the subject: the thing that bears properties and persists through their change. The properties, in the older vocabulary, are accidents — what a thing happens to be, as opposed to what it is. But a puzzle appears. Strip away every property from Socrates and ask what remains. The bare substratum seems to be nothing at all. Locke, in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), called it "something, I know not what," a support for the qualities we observe but never observe itself.

**The bundle theory.** — David Hume (1711–1776), in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), denied that there is any such support. All we ever encounter are particular perceptions, and when we look inward for the self we find only a bundle of them. "I may venture to affirm," he wrote, "that we are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement." There is no impression of a substance or of a self; the idea of a persisting thing is a fiction produced by the imagination when it confuses a succession of resembling perceptions for an identity. Hume applies the same analysis to bodies and to minds alike. The bundle theory remains a live option, and it connects to the Buddhist teaching of *anattā*, "no-self," and to the reductionism of Derek Parfit, discussed below.

## Mind and body

**Descartes's dualism.** — René Descartes (1596–1650), in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), argued that mind and body are two distinct substances. He can clearly and distinctly conceive himself as a thinking, unextended thing, and can conceive body as an extended, unthinking thing; since God can make whatever is clearly and distinctly conceived exist separately, the two are really distinct. The mind is indivisible; the body is divisible into parts. In Meditation VI he adds that he is not lodged in his body "as a pilot in a vessel" but is so "intimately conjoined, and as it were intermixed with it" that the two "compose a certain unity." This is substance dualism: two kinds of thing, thinking substance (*res cogitans*) and extended substance (*res extensa*).

**The interaction problem.** — If mind and body are different kinds of substance, how do they affect each other? In 1643 Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia put the objection to Descartes directly: an unextended, immaterial thing cannot set a material body in motion, because to move something requires contact and extension. Descartes's answer, that the mind acts on the body through the pineal gland by a union that is known by experience rather than explained, persuaded few. The seventeenth century produced alternatives. Occasionalists such as Nicolas Malebranche and Arnold Geulincx held that God is the real cause on every occasion of apparent interaction; Leibniz proposed a pre-established harmony, in which mind and body run in parallel like two clocks set to agree; epiphenomenalism, later defended by T. H. Huxley, holds that the body causes mental events but mental events cause nothing. Each is an attempt to keep the dualism while removing the mystery.

**Materialism and its rivals.** — The simplest alternative is materialism: only matter exists, and mental states are states of the body. Hobbes (1588–1679) had already suggested it, and La Mettrie's *L'Homme machine* (1747) argued that a human being is a machine. In the twentieth century the identity theory of U. T. Place (1956) and J. J. C. Smart (1959) held that mental states just are brain states. Functionalism, set out by Hilary Putnam (1967), replaced the particular brain state with a functional role: a mental state is whatever plays the right causal role between inputs, outputs, and other states, and could in principle be realised in silicon. Against all these, Thomas Nagel (1974) argued that subjective experience has a first-person character that objective description leaves out; Frank Jackson's Mary (1982) knows every physical fact about colour but learns something new on seeing red; David Chalmers (1996) called this residue the "hard problem." John Searle's Chinese room (1980) argues that symbol manipulation is not understanding, and eliminative materialists hold that the ordinary vocabulary of belief and desire will be replaced by neuroscience. Each position has able defenders; none has won.

## Personal identity

**Locke's memory theory.** — What makes a person the same person over time? Locke's answer, in Book II, chapter 27 of the *Essay*, is consciousness. A person is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places." Personal identity consists not in the sameness of substance, whether material or immaterial, but in "the sameness of a rational being" — in the continuity of consciousness, which reaches as far back as memory reaches. To make the point vivid Locke asks us to suppose the soul of a prince, carrying the prince's past consciousness, enters the body of a cobbler: everyone would see the same person as the prince, accountable for the prince's actions, though not the same man. Identity matters because reward and punishment attach to the person, not to the substance.

**Objections to Locke.** — Two objections are standard. The first is the problem of forgetting. If identity depends on memory, a person who commits a crime, forgets it, and later remembers it again would be, absurdly, two persons and then one. Locke accepts the consequence for accountability but says that God's goodness prevents the transfer of consciousness that would make it unjust. Thomas Reid, in the *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), pressed the brave-officer case: a general remembers commanding a raid and remembers being flogged as a boy, but no longer remembers the flogging; by the memory criterion he is the same person as the officer and as the boy, yet the officer is not the same person as the boy, which violates transitivity. The second is whether consciousness is a thing that can be transferred at all. Locke treats it as a present representation that might in principle be detached from any particular substance, which some critics regard as incoherent.

**Hume and the no-self view.** — Hume's bundle theory denies that there is any underlying owner of experience. He reports that when he enters most intimately into what he calls himself, he stumbles on some particular perception — of heat or cold, love or hatred, pain or pleasure — and "can never catch myself at any time without a perception." Personal identity is therefore a fiction, though a natural one, produced when the mind mistakes a connected succession for a single thing. Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), reached a similar conclusion by a different route: what matters is not identity but psychological connectedness and continuity, which come in degrees and can branch. If a person's two hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies, the original does not survive as one person; and since there is no further fact that would settle who is who, Parfit argues that identity is not what matters. David Lewis, in "Survival and Identity" (1976), replies that identity is what matters, and that in the fission case the original is identical with both offshoots because all three are stages of overlapping four-dimensional continuants.

**The Ship of Theseus.** — The oldest identity puzzle is not about persons. Plutarch, in the *Life of Theseus*, reports that the Athenians kept the ship in which Theseus had sailed, replacing each plank as it decayed, and that philosophers disputed whether it was the same ship. Thomas Hobbes sharpened it: if the old planks are reassembled into a second ship, which is the original — the repaired ship or the reconstructed one? The case forces a choice among theories. One can say identity consists in spatiotemporal continuity, or in material constitution, or in form, and the answers conflict. Modern four-dimensionalism, defended by Lewis and Theodore Sider (2001), treats objects as spread through time as well as space, with temporal parts; three-dimensionalists, who hold that objects are wholly present at each moment, owe an account of what survives replacement. The puzzle is not a trick: it shows that the ordinary notion of a thing has more than one component, and that no single criterion satisfies all of them at once.

## Free will and determinism

**The problem stated.** — Determinism is the thesis that every event, including every human choice, is fixed by prior causes. If that is true, it seems that no one could have done otherwise, and if no one could have done otherwise, it is hard to see how anyone is responsible for what they do. The three classic positions are libertarianism, which holds that determinism is false and that free choices are genuinely undetermined; hard determinism, which holds that determinism is true and free will is therefore an illusion; and compatibilism, which holds that both can be true. The disagreement is not only about causation but about what the word "free" means.

**Compatibilism and its rivals.** — Hobbes, Hume, and John Stuart Mill all defended compatibilism: an action is free when it is caused by the agent's own desires rather than by external constraint, and the absence of constraint, not the absence of causation, is what freedom requires. A man who is chained is unfree; a man who walks because he wants to is free, even though his want has causes. Libertarians reply that this is not the freedom responsibility needs, since a want that is itself caused is no more under the agent's control than a chain. Roderick Chisholm and Peter van Inwagen defended libertarian freedom; van Inwagen's *An Essay on Free Will* (1983) sets out the "consequence argument," that if determinism is true our acts are consequences of laws and events before our birth, and nothing we do now can change the past or the laws. Against both, Harry Frankfurt (1969) described cases in which a person acts freely and is responsible even though he could not have done otherwise, undercutting the principle that responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. Empirical work complicates the picture: Benjamin Libet's experiments (1983) measured brain activity before subjects reported a decision to move, which some read as evidence that conscious will is not the cause of action, though the interpretation is disputed.

## Causation

**Hume's constant conjunction.** — What is it for one event to cause another? Hume's answer, in the *Treatise* and again in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), is that we never perceive any necessary connection between cause and effect. We perceive that one event is followed by another, and that events of the first kind have always been followed by events of the second; from repeated experience the mind acquires a habit of expecting the second when the first appears. The idea of necessary connection is a feeling of the imagination, not a feature observed in the world. Hume gives two definitions of cause: an object followed by another where all objects resembling the first are followed by objects resembling the second, and an object followed by another whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other. The first is the regularity, the second the mental transition. Kant, in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), accepted that causation is not read off experience but argued that it is a condition of experience, a category the mind imposes; the causal principle is therefore synthetic a priori.

**Regularity and its rivals.** — If causation is constant conjunction, then the striking of a match is a cause of the flame only because such strikings have been followed by flames. Critics find this too thin. John Mackie (1965) analysed a cause as an INUS condition — an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition. David Lewis (1973) proposed that causes are events counterfactually dependent on their effects: if the cause had not happened, the effect would not have happened. Others appeal to powers, dispositions, or probabilistic raising of chance. Each theory has counterexamples, and the debate remains open.

## Time and space

**Time.** — Is time real, and does it pass? J. M. E. McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (1908) distinguished the A-series, which orders events as past, present, and future, from the B-series, which orders them as earlier and later. He argued that the A-series is contradictory, because every event must be past, present, and future, and that without the A-series there is no real change or time; he concluded that time is unreal. Few accept the conclusion, but the argument set the terms of the debate. Presentism holds that only the present exists; eternalism, or the block universe, holds that past, present, and future are equally real and that the passage of time is a feature of perspective, like the word "here." The special theory of relativity, which makes simultaneity depend on the observer's frame, is often taken to favour eternalism, and the growing-block theory tries to keep the past real and the future open. Zeno's paradoxes remain the sharpest test of any account of the continuum.

**Space.** — Is space a thing in its own right, or only the order of relations among things? Newton held that space is absolute, a container that exists whether or not anything is in it, and offered the rotating bucket as evidence: the water climbs the sides even when it is not rotating relative to any visible body, which suggests rotation relative to space itself. Leibniz denied this, arguing in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1715–16) that space is nothing over and above the relations among bodies, and that an absolute space with no observable difference between its parts violates the principle of sufficient reason. General relativity made the question concrete again: spacetime curves in the presence of mass, so the relation between geometry and matter is not one of container and contents. Whether this vindicates substantivalism (space is real) or relationalism (only relations are real) is still argued. The organizing question returns with full force: what has to exist for motion and change to be possible?

## Chronology

- **c. 500 BCE** — Parmenides argues that what is is one and unchanging; Zeno states his paradoxes of motion.
- **c. 375 BCE** — Plato writes the *Republic*, including the allegory of the cave and the theory of Forms.
- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle writes the *Categories* and the *Metaphysics*; form is in the thing, and substance is primary.
- **c. 270 CE** — Porphyry's *Isagoge* poses the problem of universals for later readers.
- **c. 520 CE** — Boethius translates Porphyry and Aristotle into Latin; Abelard and Ockham later divide over universals.
- **1641** — Descartes publishes the *Meditations* and states the real distinction between mind and body.
- **1643** — Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia raises the interaction problem in letters to Descartes.
- **1690** — Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* ties personal identity to consciousness and memory.
- **1715–16** — The Leibniz–Clarke correspondence debates absolute and relational space.
- **1739–40** — Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* gives the bundle theory of the self and the analysis of causation.
- **1747** — La Mettrie publishes *L'Homme machine*.
- **1781** — Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* makes causation a category of the understanding.
- **1785** — Reid's *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* states the brave-officer objection to Locke.
- **1908** — McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" distinguishes the A-series and the B-series.
- **1948** — Quine's "On What There Is" defines ontological commitment.
- **1973** — David Lewis's "Causation" offers a counterfactual theory.
- **1976** — David Lewis's "Survival and Identity" answers Parfit on fission.
- **1980** — Searle states the Chinese room argument.
- **1982** — Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" gives the knowledge argument.
- **1984** — Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* argues that identity is not what matters.
- **1989** — Armstrong's *Universals: An Opinionated Introduction* defends immanent realism.
- **1996** — Chalmers's *The Conscious Mind* names the hard problem of consciousness.

## Questions to carry

- When two things are both red, what exactly do they share — a real universal, a resemblance, or only a name?
- If mind and body are different kinds of thing, how can either act on the other; if they are the same kind, what explains the first-person character of experience?
- What has to persist for a person to be the same person — a substance, a body, a continuous consciousness, or nothing at all?
- If every choice is caused, can anyone be responsible; and does responsibility require the ability to have done otherwise?
- Does the world contain time and space as things, or only events and bodies related in time and space?
