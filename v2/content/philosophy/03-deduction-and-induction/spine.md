# Deduction and induction

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

**Two arguments, two promises.** — Consider two arguments. The first: all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal. The second: the sun has risen on every day yet recorded; therefore the sun will rise tomorrow. The first cannot fail if its premisses are true. The conclusion is already contained in the premisses, and to accept the premisses while denying the conclusion is to contradict oneself. The second can fail. Its premisses have held for all of recorded history, and still the conclusion is not guaranteed, because the future need not resemble the past. The first argument is deductive; the second is inductive. The difference between them is the difference between an argument that must work and one that usually works, and it is the question this unit pursues. A deductive argument is judged by whether its form guarantees the conclusion; logicians call such an argument **valid**, and call it **sound** when it is valid and its premisses are also true. An inductive argument is judged instead by **strength**: how much support the premisses give the conclusion, and how the conclusion's probability rises or falls with new evidence. Validity is all-or-nothing; strength comes in degrees.

**Aristotle invents the syllogism.** — The first systematic theory of deductive inference is in Aristotle's *Prior Analytics*, written around 350 BCE. Aristotle begins by defining his terms. A **premiss** is a sentence affirming or denying one thing of another, and it is universal ("belongs to all"), particular ("belongs to some"), or indefinite. A **term** is what a premiss resolves into, the predicate and that of which it is predicated. A **syllogism**, he writes, is "discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so." The phrase "of necessity" carries the whole idea: the conclusion is not merely suggested or made likely by the premisses; it is forced by them. Aristotle distinguishes a **perfect** syllogism, whose necessity is evident from the premisses as stated, from an **imperfect** one, which needs one or more further propositions to make the necessity plain. This is the ancestor of the modern distinction between an argument whose validity is immediate and one that must be proved valid by steps.

**The middle term and the three figures.** — Every syllogism has three terms, and one of them, the **middle term**, appears in both premisses and vanishes from the conclusion. Aristotle classifies syllogisms by where the middle term stands. In the **first figure** the middle is the subject of one premiss and the predicate of the other: "If A is predicated of all B, and B of all C, A must be predicated of all C." In the **second figure** the middle is predicated of both the others: "Let M be predicated of no N, but of all O ... consequently N will belong to no O." In the **third figure** both of the other terms are predicated of the middle: "whenever both P and R belong to S, it follows that P will necessarily belong to some R." A stock example makes the machinery visible. Let the middle term be "man": all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal. The middle term carries the subject of the conclusion into its predicate: because every man is mortal, and Socrates is one of the men, the conclusion follows. Aristotle treated the rule at work — later called the *dictum de omni et nullo*, that whatever is said of all of a subject is said of whatever falls under it — as the principle that makes first-figure syllogisms perfect. He argues that all first-figure syllogisms are perfect, while those of the second and third figures are imperfect and must be reduced to the first by converting a premiss or by a *reductio ad impossibile* — a proof that assumes the opposite of the conclusion and derives a contradiction. The first figure is thus the standard against which the others are measured, and Aristotle can claim that every valid syllogism is either a first-figure syllogism or reducible to one.

**The moods and their names.** — Within each figure, the **mood** of a syllogism is fixed by the quantity and quality of its two premisses: universal or particular, affirmative or negative. Medieval logicians gave the valid moods mnemonic names whose vowels record the premisses. In the first figure the four are **Barbara** (universal affirmatives), **Celarent** (universal negative, universal affirmative, universal negative), **Darii**, and **Ferio**. The second figure supplies **Cesare**, **Camestres**, **Festino**, and **Baroco**; the third, **Darapti**, **Disamis**, **Datisi**, **Felapton**, **Bocardo**, and **Ferison**. That is fourteen moods in all. The schools of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries — among them William of Sherwood (c. 1190–1249) and Peter of Spain (c. 1205–1277) — added five "subaltern" moods that infer a particular conclusion from a universal one, bringing the traditional count to nineteen. The names were not decorative. Their first letters marked the first-figure mood to which a mood reduces, and the consonants *s*, *c*, and *p* marked how the reduction went: *s* for simple conversion of a premiss, *c* for conversion *per impossibile*, *p* for conversion of the premiss in the reduced mood. The mnemonic verses were a compressed algorithm, and for two thousand years the syllogism was the core of what it meant to reason correctly.

**Modus ponens and its relatives.** — Most deductive reasoning outside the syllogism uses a handful of simple forms. **Modus ponens** runs: if P then Q; P; therefore Q. **Modus tollens** runs: if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. A **disjunctive syllogism** runs: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. Each is valid, and each is valid in virtue of its form, not its subject matter: replace P and Q with any sentences whatever and the inference still holds. Two near neighbours are invalid, and the difference is easy to miss. **Affirming the consequent** argues: if P then Q; Q; therefore P. **Denying the antecedent** argues: if P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. Both are fallacies. From "if it rained, the street is wet" and "the street is wet," it does not follow that it rained; a burst main would do as well. From "if it rained, the street is wet" and "it did not rain," it does not follow that the street is dry. The medieval names *modus ponens* and *modus tollens* come from the same twelfth- and thirteenth-century schools that named the moods, and the two fallacies are the commonest errors in ordinary argument, which is why logic texts return to them so often. In the 1960s the psychologist Peter Wason showed with a simple card-sorting puzzle — the **Wason selection task** — that most people, asked to test a conditional rule, choose the cases that could only confirm it and overlook the case that could refute it. The puzzle is a psychological finding, not a logical one, but it explains why the fallacies are so persistent.

**Validity is not truth.** — A valid argument can have false premisses, and then its conclusion may be false even though nothing is wrong with the reasoning. "No fish are mammals; whales are fish; therefore whales are not mammals" is valid — if the premisses were true the conclusion would be — but the second premiss is false, so the argument is not sound. Deduction is therefore a truth-transmitting device, not a truth-producing one. It carries truth from premisses to conclusion; it cannot manufacture it. This raises the oldest difficulty in the theory of knowledge. If every conclusion is to be justified by premisses, and those premisses by further premisses, the chain either runs backwards forever, or turns in a circle, or stops somewhere at something accepted without proof. The ancient sceptics set out this trilemma; Aristotle's answer was that the first principles of a science are grasped not by deduction but by *nous*, an intuitive reason trained by experience, and that demonstration begins from them. Euclid's *Elements*, composed around 300 BCE, showed what the programme looked like in practice: a few definitions, postulates, and common notions, and hundreds of propositions derived from them in strict order. For more than two thousand years it was the model of certain knowledge, and its authority rested on assumptions it never proved.

**Induction: from the seen to the unseen.** — Deduction moves from general premisses to a particular conclusion, or from premisses already containing the conclusion's content. **Induction** moves the other way, from particular observations to a general conclusion, or from the observed to the unobserved. The simplest form is **enumerative induction**: this swan is white, that swan is white, and so on; therefore all swans are white. The classic cautionary case is the black swan. For centuries Europeans had seen only white swans, and "all swans are white" was a stock example of a secure generalization — so secure that logicians used it to illustrate the difference between a necessary truth and a merely well-supported one. In 1697 the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh, sailing along the coast of Western Australia, sighted black swans, and named the Swan River for them. The species, *Cygnus atratus*, is native to Australia, and the old generalization was simply false. The case does not show that induction is useless; it shows that enumerative induction can never exclude the instance that would refute it. Mill later put the schema plainly: "John, Peter, etc., are mortal, therefore all mankind are mortal," which he said may be thrown into a syllogism by supplying the suppressed major premiss that what is true of John and Peter is true of all mankind. The question is where that major premiss comes from. When the instances surveyed are all the instances there are, the inference has deductive force: every student in the room voted; these are all the students; therefore every student voted. The interest of induction lies in **incomplete enumeration**, where the conclusion ranges over cases not yet seen. Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) complained that the schools relied on simple enumeration and proposed instead a disciplined collection of instances — tables of presence, absence, and degree — so that nature might be read off rather than guessed at. Mill's methods are the mature form of that programme.

**Analogy.** — A second inductive form reasons from resemblance. If two things are alike in known respects, an **analogy** argues, they are probably alike in a further respect. William Harvey's *De Motu Cordis* (1628) argued that the heart is a pump, and used the analogy with mechanical pumps together with quantitative estimates of the blood's volume to show that the blood must circulate rather than be continually produced by the liver. The analogy was fruitful because the relevant similarity — a chamber that moves fluid by pressure — was real. Not every analogy is. William Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802) argued that a watch found on a heath implies a watchmaker, and that the eye and other organs imply a designer; David Hume, in the *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion*, written in the 1750s and published in 1779, had already pressed the objection that the universe is not sufficiently like a watch, and that the argument from a single case is weak. Charles Darwin used a different analogy, between the breeder's artificial selection and the struggle in nature, to build the case of *On the Origin of Species* (1859). An analogical argument is only as strong as the similarities it names are relevant to the conclusion, and there is no mechanical rule for judging relevance.

**Causal inference and Mill's methods.** — The most powerful inductive arguments aim at causes, and the standard account of how to find them is John Stuart Mill's *A System of Logic* (1843). Mill set out the "methods of experimental inquiry," commonly counted as five, since the Joint Method combines the first two. The **Method of Agreement** holds that if two or more instances of a phenomenon share only one circumstance, that circumstance is its cause or effect. The **Method of Difference** holds that if an instance where the phenomenon occurs and one where it does not differ in only one circumstance, that circumstance is the cause or an indispensable part of it. Mill added the **Joint Method** of agreement and difference, the **Method of Residues** — subtract from a phenomenon what is already known to be the effect of certain antecedents, and the residue is the effect of the rest — and the **Method of Concomitant Variations**, which tracks cause and effect through the way one quantity changes with another. The methods were not a formal logic but a codification of good experimental practice, and they assume that nature is regular. Mill himself said that the principle of the uniformity of the course of nature is "the ultimate major premise of all inductions," and admitted that this principle is not a starting point but a late and hard-won generalization, itself founded on prior inductions. The classic application came in 1854, when the London physician John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in Soho to the water of the Broad Street pump. Deaths clustered around the pump, and workers at a nearby brewery who drank beer rather than water were largely spared — a near-perfect Method of Difference. Snow's conclusion was drawn years before the germ theory of disease was accepted, and when the pump handle was removed the outbreak subsided. Mill's methods still structure controlled trials and epidemiology, though no method can guarantee that an unobserved common cause is not doing the work.

**Hume's problem.** — In 1748 David Hume stated the difficulty that has defined the subject since. He divided the objects of reason into **relations of ideas** — geometry, algebra, arithmetic, and whatever is intuitively or demonstratively certain — and **matters of fact**, whose contraries are always conceivable without contradiction. "That the sun will not rise to-morrow," he wrote, "is no less intelligible a proposition, and implies no more contradiction than the affirmation, that it will rise." All reasoning about matters of fact beyond present sense and memory, Hume argued, rests on the relation of cause and effect, and knowledge of that relation comes only from experience, never from reason *a priori*. But then how does experience justify the step to the future? Hume considers the two available answers and rejects both. It cannot be **demonstrative** reasoning, because no contradiction follows from the course of nature changing. It cannot be **probable** reasoning either, because all probable reasoning about matters of fact already assumes that the future will resemble the past, so the argument would be circular. "It is impossible, therefore, that any arguments from experience can prove this resemblance of the past to the future; since all these arguments are founded on the supposition of that resemblance." Hume's conclusion is not a counsel to abandon induction; it is that reason does not produce the inference. Custom or habit does. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life," he writes in the following section, and nature ensures that we act on it even though philosophy cannot justify it: "As an agent, I am quite satisfied in the point; but as a philosopher ... I want to learn the foundation of this inference."

**The state of the debate.** — No single reply to Hume has won general assent, and the disagreement is worth knowing. Karl Popper, in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934; English translation 1959), denied that science uses induction at all: a theory can never be confirmed by instances, only falsified, and the method of science is conjecture and refutation. Critics, including Wesley Salmon, reply that falsification itself relies on accepting observation statements, which is an inductive step in disguise. Hans Reichenbach, in *Experience and Prediction* (1938), offered a pragmatic defence: if any method can find limits of frequency in nature, induction can, so it is rational to use it even though it cannot be proved. Rudolf Carnap, in *Logical Foundations of Probability* (1950), tried to construct a formal inductive logic giving degrees of confirmation. Nelson Goodman, in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955), argued that even granting a principle of uniformity a second riddle remains, about which predicates are fit to be projected. W. V. Quine, in "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969), proposed to stop trying to justify induction from outside and instead study how creatures like us actually form expectations. Each reply is a position in a live dispute, and the dispute has outlasted every proposed solution.

**Inference to the best explanation.** — A third form of non-deductive reasoning was isolated by Charles Sanders Peirce in the 1870s, at first under the name "hypothesis" and later **abduction**: **inference to the best explanation**. Its schema is not "all observed Fs are G," but "the surprising fact C is observed; if hypothesis A were true, C would follow as a matter of course; therefore there is reason to suspect A is true." Peirce treated this as the mode of reasoning by which new hypotheses are generated, distinct both from deduction and from enumerative induction. The physician Ignaz Semmelweis used something like it in Vienna in 1847, when he noticed that mothers delivered by medical students died of childbed fever far more often than those delivered by midwives, and hypothesised that the students were carrying "cadaverous particles" from the dissecting room; washing hands in chlorinated lime cut the deaths dramatically, years before germ theory explained why. Darwin's argument for natural selection, and the geologist's inference to a past ice age from scattered boulders, fit the same pattern. Peter Lipton, in *Inference to the Best Explanation* (1991), called it inference to the "loveliest" explanation, and analysed it as a matter of comparative explanation. The form is not truth-preserving: the best available explanation may still be false, and what makes one explanation better than another — simplicity, scope, fit with background knowledge — is itself disputed.

**How the three fit together.** — Deduction, induction, and abduction are not rivals but stages of one practice. Abduction proposes a hypothesis; deduction draws out what the hypothesis implies, including predictions that can be tested; induction and Mill's methods check whether the predictions hold. This is the **hypothetico-deductive** pattern, stated most influentially by Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim in 1948, and it is why the two kinds of inference need each other. Deduction without induction gives conclusions only as good as its premisses, and nothing tells us which premisses to trust. Induction without deduction cannot say what a generalization predicts in a particular case. The two promises with which this unit began are therefore complementary: deduction transmits truth with certainty but cannot extend it beyond the premisses, while induction extends knowledge to new cases but never with certainty. The organizing question — what is the difference between an argument that must work and one that usually works — has a short answer, that the first is valid in virtue of its form and the second is strong only relative to evidence, and a long answer that philosophers have been writing since Aristotle, and have not finished.

## Chronology

- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle's *Prior Analytics* defines the syllogism, the perfect and imperfect forms, and the three figures.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Euclid's *Elements* sets out geometry as a chain of deductions from definitions, postulates, and common notions.
- **c. 300–200 BCE** — The Stoics, above all Chrysippus, develop a propositional logic of conditionals and disjunctions, the ancestor of *modus ponens*.
- **c. 1150–1250** — William of Sherwood and Peter of Spain codify the valid moods and their mnemonic names; the subaltern moods bring the traditional count to nineteen.
- **1620** — Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* calls for an inductive method of interpreting nature.
- **1628** — William Harvey's *De Motu Cordis* argues from analogy and quantity that the blood circulates.
- **1697** — Willem de Vlamingh sights black swans in Western Australia, refuting the generalization that all swans are white.
- **1748** — Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section IV, states the problem of induction.
- **1779** — Hume's *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* is published, attacking the argument from analogy to a designer.
- **1802** — William Paley's *Natural Theology* restates the watchmaker argument.
- **1843** — Mill's *A System of Logic* sets out the five methods of experimental inquiry.
- **1854** — John Snow traces a cholera outbreak to the Broad Street pump.
- **1877–1878** — Peirce publishes "The Fixation of Belief" and "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis," naming abduction.
- **1934/1959** — Popper's *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* argues for falsification instead of induction.
- **1938** — Reichenbach's *Experience and Prediction* offers a pragmatic justification of induction.
- **1950** — Carnap's *Logical Foundations of Probability* attempts a formal inductive logic.
- **1955** — Goodman's *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* states the new riddle of induction.
- **1966** — Wason publishes the selection task, showing how poorly people test conditional rules.
- **1969** — Quine's "Epistemology Naturalized" proposes a naturalistic study of how we reason.
- **1991** — Lipton's *Inference to the Best Explanation* analyses abduction as inference to the loveliest explanation.

## Questions to carry

- What exactly does a valid argument guarantee, and what does it leave open?
- Why can a valid argument still reach a false conclusion, and what does that show about the limits of deduction?
- Black swans and grue both threaten induction. Do they threaten the same thing, or two different things?
- Mill said the uniformity of nature is the ultimate major premise of every induction. Does that justify induction, or merely restate it?
- If the best explanation is not guaranteed to be true, why should anyone prefer it to the second-best?
