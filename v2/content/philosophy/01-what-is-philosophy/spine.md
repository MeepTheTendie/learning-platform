# What is philosophy?

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

## The trial

**The examined life.** — In the spring of 399 BCE a jury of 501 Athenian citizens heard a case against Socrates, a man of seventy who had spent his adult life questioning his fellow citizens in the marketplace. The charges were that he did not believe in the gods the city believed in, that he introduced new divinities, and that he corrupted the young. He was convicted, and after a second vote he was sentenced to death and executed by drinking hemlock. The speech Plato gives him at the trial — the Apology, written some decades later — is not a transcript but a reconstruction. Even so, it is the founding document of a way of life: the life of giving reasons. Socrates does not claim to know the answers. He claims to know only that he does not know, and that this is better than the false confidence of those who think they know. "The unexamined life," he says, "is not worth living."

**The oracle and the gadfly.** — The story Socrates tells begins at Delphi. His friend Chaerephon asked the oracle of Apollo whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the priestess answered that no one was. Socrates, puzzled, went to the men reputed to be wise — politicians, poets, craftsmen — and questioned them. Each, he found, believed he knew something he did not know. The oracle's meaning, Socrates concluded, was that human wisdom is worth little, and that the wisest person is the one who, like him, recognises his own ignorance. This questioning made him unpopular, and he compared himself to a gadfly fastened on a large, sluggish horse: a nuisance whose bites keep the animal awake. The examined life, on this account, is not a private hobby. It is a public disturbance, and the city that cannot tolerate it is the poorer for it.

**What examination is.** — To examine a life is to examine the beliefs that guide it. Socrates asked questions of the form: What is courage? What is justice? What is piety? The answer given would be tested by counterexample. If a general defines courage as standing one's ground, Socrates asks about the soldier who wins by retreating and then turning; if the definition is revised, he asks again. The pattern — proposal, objection, revised proposal, and often aporia, the state of being stuck — is the elenchus, the cross-examination. It assumes that a person's beliefs form a set, that some of them conflict, and that the conflict can be exposed by questions. It also assumes that the person questioned can be brought to see the conflict and to care about it.

## The beginning of the question

**Thales and the first answer.** — The Greek tradition names Thales of Miletus, who lived in the sixth century BCE on the coast of what is now western Turkey, as the first philosopher. He is said to have predicted a solar eclipse in 585 BCE, and to have held that everything is made of water. The second claim is false, and the first is doubtful, but the form of the claims matters more than their truth. Thales did not explain the world by telling a story about the gods; he proposed a single substance out of which everything is made, and gave reasons. That move — explaining the many by the one, and offering an account rather than a genealogy — is what the later Greeks called philosophia, the love of wisdom.

**The word and its neighbours.** — The word philosophia is attributed by later writers to Pythagoras, who is said to have preferred the title "lover of wisdom" to "wise man," on the ground that only a god is wise. The word names an activity, not a possession. It sits beside three neighbours that it is often confused with. Mythos is a story that explains by narrative; logos is an account that explains by reason. Sophos is the one who knows; philosophos is the one who seeks. Theology explains the world by the will of a god; philosophy explains it by causes that can be inspected. The line between them is not always sharp, and many philosophers have been religious, but the difference in method is real.

**Philosophy outside Greece.** — The questions are not Greek property. Confucius, who lived from 551 to 479 BCE, asked how a person should live and how a ruler should govern, and answered with a programme of ritual, sincerity, and the rectification of names. Nāgārjuna, writing in India around 150 to 250 CE, argued that everything depends on everything else and that nothing has a fixed nature of its own. Al-Ghazali, in eleventh-century Baghdad, wrote The Incoherence of the Philosophers against Avicenna, and Ibn Rushd answered him a century later in Cordoba with The Incoherence of the Incoherence. Zhuangzi, in fourth-century BCE China, asked whether he was a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man. The problems of reason, reality, and the good life are not the property of one language.

## Reasons

**Opinion, belief, and supported claim.** — Three things are easily confused. An opinion is a view someone holds, often without having thought much about it. A belief is a mental state: to believe that it will rain is to hold a proposition to be true. A supported claim is a belief for which reasons have been given, and those reasons can be inspected. The difference is not certainty. A supported claim can be false, and an unconsidered opinion can be true. The difference is the relation to reasons. The Greek word doxa covers the first sort of thing — the received view, what seems so — and it was set against episteme, knowledge. Plato's dialogues turn on the gap between them: a person may say the true thing for the wrong reason, and that, for Socrates, is not yet knowledge.

**What a reason is.** — A reason is a consideration that counts in favour of a claim. Reasons come in kinds. There is evidence: the butler's fingerprints on the knife. There is testimony: a witness says she saw him. There is authority: a physician says the drug is safe, and her training and track record make her word count. There is argument: a set of premises from which the claim follows. These are not equally good in every setting. Testimony from a stranger about a matter you can check yourself is weak; testimony from a trained observer about a matter you cannot check is strong. The question "what would count as a good reason?" is therefore not one question but a family of them, and much of philosophy consists in sorting them out.

**Deduction, induction, abduction.** — A deductive argument aims to make the conclusion follow necessarily from the premises. If all men are mortal and Socrates is a man, then Socrates is mortal; if the premises are true and the form is valid, the conclusion cannot be false. An inductive argument makes the conclusion probable: the sun has risen every day recorded, so it will probably rise tomorrow. An abductive argument infers the best explanation: the room is cold, the window is open, and the wind is from the north, so the window was opened recently. The three are often used together. Logic is the branch of philosophy that studies the forms of argument, and its central distinction is between validity, which concerns the form, and truth, which concerns the premises. A valid argument with a false premise can yield a false conclusion; an invalid argument can, by luck, yield a true one.

**Fallacies.** — A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that looks persuasive but fails. The ad hominem attacks the arguer instead of the argument. The straw man replaces a position with a weaker version and refutes that. The false dilemma offers two options when there are more. Circular reasoning assumes what it sets out to prove. Post hoc reasoning mistakes sequence for cause. The appeal to authority treats an expert's word as decisive outside her field. Naming fallacies is not the point; the point is to see why a piece of reasoning fails, and to say so without pretending that the failure settles the conclusion. A bad argument for a true claim leaves the claim exactly where it was.

## Understanding without endorsing

**The principle of charity.** — To understand a position is not the same as to accept it. The principle of charity says that an interpreter should, other things equal, read a speaker's words so as to make them as reasonable and as true as the evidence allows. The phrase was given currency by the philosopher Neil L. Wilson in 1959 and developed by W. V. O. Quine and Donald Davidson. The reason is practical. If you assume your opponent is a fool, you will spend your time on the weakest version of her view, and you will learn nothing. If you state her view at its strongest, you may still reject it — but now your rejection is worth something. Gregory Vlastos made a version of this point about Socrates: the questions are not traps but an attempt to find the best case for a position before testing it.

**Charity and its limits.** — Charity has limits, and philosophers disagree about where they lie. Read too generously, it can dissolve the difference between your view and another's, or quietly substitute your own concerns for the other's. The alternative is to take a view seriously enough to let it remain strange, and then to ask what reasons its holder would give. Mill's argument in On Liberty (1859) is a useful discipline here: a person who knows only her own side of a case, he wrote, knows little of that. Understanding a position and endorsing it are two different achievements, and the first is a condition of doing the second honestly. One may spend a whole life understanding an argument one rejects.

## The branches

**Logic.** — Logic studies the form of valid inference. Aristotle's Organon, written in the fourth century BCE, set out the syllogism and the rules of definition; Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) created modern symbolic logic, and Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica (1910–13) tried to derive arithmetic from it. Logic is the most settled part of philosophy: a valid form remains valid, and the discovery of a new one is a permanent addition. It is also the tool the other branches use, which is why it is taught first and why its results are the least contested.

**Metaphysics.** — Metaphysics asks what there is and what it is like. Does the world contain only matter, or also minds? Are there properties — redness, justice — or only particular things? Is time real, or a feature of how people experience things? Is every event caused, and if so, can a person act freely? The name comes from the Greek ta meta ta physika, "the things after the physics," from the place of Aristotle's lectures in an ancient edition, but the subject is older than the label. A concrete case is the problem of universals: when two apples are both red, is there one thing, redness, that both share, or only two apples and a word? Plato held that there are Forms, existing apart from the things that share them; Aristotle held that the form is in the thing; the medieval nominalists, and William of Ockham in the fourteenth century, held that only particulars exist and general words are conveniences. The argument has run for two thousand years and is not over.

**Epistemology.** — Epistemology is the study of knowledge: what it is, how it is acquired, and how far it reaches. The traditional analysis defined knowledge as justified true belief. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper giving cases in which a person has a justified true belief that is not knowledge — the justification rests on a false assumption that happens to be cancelled out by luck. The paper reopened a question that had seemed closed, and the debate it began is still running. Epistemology also asks the sceptic's question: what, if anything, can be known? Descartes's answer, in the Discourse on Method (1637), was to doubt everything that could be doubted until he reached something that could not: the fact that he was thinking. Sextus Empiricus, in the second century CE, collected the sceptical arguments of the ancient world and concluded that the honest response was suspension of judgment.

**Ethics.** — Ethics asks what to do and how to live. It divides into three. Metaethics asks what moral claims mean and whether they can be true. Normative ethics asks what makes an action right. Applied ethics asks about particular cases. The three main families in normative ethics are virtue ethics, which begins with Aristotle and asks what kind of person to be; deontology, which begins with Kant's Groundwork (1785) and asks what rules or duties bind regardless of consequences; and consequentialism, which begins with Bentham and Mill and asks which action produces the best outcome. The trolley problem, introduced by Philippa Foot in 1967 and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson, is a concrete test case: a runaway trolley will kill five people unless a switch is thrown to a side track, where it will kill one. Most people say throw the switch; most hesitate when the one must be pushed off a bridge. Whether the two cases differ, and why, is still argued.

**Aesthetics.** — Aesthetics is the study of beauty, art, and taste. Is beauty a property of objects or a projection of the viewer? Can there be a standard of taste, or only preferences? Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) and Kant's Critique of Judgment (1790) gave the classic answers. In the twentieth century the question shifted from beauty to art: what makes something a work of art? Marcel Duchamp submitted a factory-made urinal, signed "R. Mutt," to an exhibition in 1917, and the question of why it might count as art has occupied philosophers since. Arthur Danto's essay "The Artworld" (1964) argued that what makes an object art is a theory and a history that the art world supplies, not a visible property of the object.

**Political philosophy.** — Political philosophy asks how people should live together: what makes authority legitimate, what justice requires, what liberty is, and when obedience is owed. Plato's Republic asks why anyone should be just. Hobbes's Leviathan (1651) grounds authority in a social contract made to escape the state of nature; Locke's Two Treatises (1689) grounds it in natural rights; Rousseau's Social Contract (1762) in the general will. John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) revived the contract in a new form: principles of justice are those that people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance," not knowing their own place in society. Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) answered with a defence of liberty and a minimal state. The two books set the terms of a debate that continues.

## Where the branches meet

**Free will.** — The free-will problem shows why the branches cannot be kept apart. Metaphysics asks whether determinism is true — whether every event, including every choice, is fixed by prior causes. Ethics asks whether anyone can be responsible for an action if it was fixed. Law asks when a person may be punished. One argument, incompatibilism, says freedom and determinism cannot both be true; another, compatibilism, says freedom means acting without external constraint, which is compatible with causation. The same evidence and the same logic bear on all three questions, and a position in one branch constrains the others.

**Beauty and taste.** — The question whether beauty is objective joins aesthetics to metaphysics and epistemology at once. If beauty is a real property, then judgments of taste can be true or false, and there is something for the critic to discover. If it is only a projection of pleasure, then the critic's authority is harder to explain, and the apparent agreement of experienced judges needs another account. Kant tried to have both: a judgment of taste is subjective, resting on feeling, yet it claims universal agreement. Whether that is coherent is still debated.

**Personal identity.** — The question of what makes a person the same person over time joins metaphysics to ethics. If the self is a continuous stream of memory, what happens when memory fails? If it is the body, what of the person who loses a limb or receives a transplant? The problem is old: the Ship of Theseus, whose planks were replaced one by one, was already discussed in antiquity. It matters practically, because punishment, promises, and contracts assume that the person who acted is the person who is answerable. Locke tied personal identity to consciousness and memory; later philosophers have asked whether that makes a person responsible for what she cannot remember.

## What philosophy is not

**Not mere opinion.** — Everyone has opinions, and a conversation in which each person states a view and no one asks for reasons is not philosophy. The demand for reasons is what distinguishes the subject. This does not mean that only experts may speak. It means that what a person says is answerable to argument, and that the argument, not the speaker's standing, decides. The Greek practice of parrhesia, frank speech, assumed that the speaker put herself at risk by saying what she believed; the modern seminar assumes instead that the speaker puts her reasons at risk, and expects them to be tested.

**Not self-help.** — The ancient schools did aim at a good life. Epicurus promised tranquility; the Stoics promised freedom from disturbance; Epictetus's Enchiridion, a short manual compiled by his pupil Arrian in the second century CE, opens by sorting what is in a person's power from what is not, and treats the distinction as the key to peace of mind. The resemblance to a book of advice is real. The difference is that the advice is meant to follow from an argument about the nature of reality, and the argument can be challenged. The historian of philosophy Pierre Hadot argued in Philosophy as a Way of Life (1995) that ancient philosophy was precisely a set of exercises, not a body of theory; the counter-argument is that the exercises were inseparable from the doctrines, and that stripping the doctrine leaves the exercise without its justification.

**Not a body of settled facts.** — Philosophy does not accumulate results the way chemistry does, and the same questions recur. But the claim that it makes no progress is too strong. Logic has permanent results. The analysis of knowledge was clarified, then disrupted by Gettier and rebuilt. Bad arguments have been identified and abandoned. The philosopher David Chalmers has argued that progress in philosophy is real but of a different kind from progress in science: it consists in the articulation and elimination of positions, not in a single accepted theory. Whether that counts as progress is itself a philosophical question.

**Not a licence to doubt.** — Sextus Empiricus doubted in order to reach calm, and his doubt was disciplined by arguments. The person who doubts everything for effect, who asks questions without wanting answers and treats every position as equally baseless, is not doing philosophy; that posture is a refusal of the subject's central demand, which is to follow the argument. The difference between the sceptic and the troll is that the sceptic will accept a conclusion if the argument for it is good enough, and will say what would count as good enough.

## Philosophy as a practice

**Conversation and disagreement.** — Philosophy is done in public. Plato wrote dialogues, not treatises, and the dialogue form makes the reader a third party to the argument. The medieval university developed the disputatio, a formal debate in which a thesis was defended and objections answered. The modern forms are the seminar, the conference, and the journal, where a paper is published and then answered. The aim is not agreement but the improvement of reasons through opposition. A claim that no one has tested is weaker than one that has survived testing, and testing is a social activity.

**Intellectual independence.** — Kant's essay "What Is Enlightenment?" (1784) took as its motto Sapere aude — "Dare to know" — and defined enlightenment as the courage to use one's own understanding without the direction of another. The point is not to reject authority. Most of what anyone knows rests on testimony, and no one can verify everything. The point is to know when deference is warranted and when it has become a substitute for judgment. Independence without humility becomes contrarianism; humility without independence becomes deference. The examined life requires both, and keeping them together is the work.

**Changing your mind.** — The organizing question of this unit is what would count as a good reason to change your mind. The candidates are the ones already described: a counterexample that refutes a general claim, a valid argument whose premises you accept, a better explanation of the same evidence, new evidence, or the discovery that your reasons were circular. Bad reasons include social pressure, repetition, and the mere fact that everyone agrees, though agreement can be evidence if the agreeing parties are independent and informed. The hard part is that reasons for what one already believes are easy to find and reasons against are easy to miss. In 1960 Peter Wason showed that people given a rule-guessing task tend to test only the cases that fit their hypothesis, not the cases that would refute it. The discipline of asking what would count against one's view is the practical core of the examined life.

## Chronology

- **c. 585 BCE** — Thales of Miletus is said to have predicted a solar eclipse; later Greeks call him the first philosopher.
- **c. 470–399 BCE** — Socrates lives in Athens and questions his fellow citizens in the marketplace.
- **399 BCE** — Socrates is tried, convicted, and executed; Plato's Apology is set at the trial.
- **c. 387 BCE** — Plato founds the Academy in Athens.
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle founds the Lyceum and writes the Metaphysics and the Organon.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Zeno of Citium founds Stoicism; Epicurus founds his school, the Garden.
- **c. 150–250 CE** — Nāgārjuna writes the root verses of Madhyamaka, a major Buddhist philosophy.
- **c. 160–210 CE** — Sextus Empiricus compiles the ancient sceptical arguments.
- **415 CE** — Hypatia, a Neoplatonist mathematician, is killed in Alexandria.
- **1111 CE** — Al-Ghazali dies; his Incoherence of the Philosophers attacks Avicenna, and Ibn Rushd answers it in 1180.
- **1637** — Descartes publishes the Discourse on Method in French.
- **1643** — Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia challenges Descartes on how mind and body interact.
- **1739–40** — Hume's Treatise of Human Nature states the problem of induction.
- **1781** — Kant publishes the Critique of Pure Reason.
- **1859** — Mill publishes On Liberty; Darwin publishes the Origin of Species.
- **1879** — Frege's Begriffsschrift founds modern logic.
- **1901–03** — Russell's paradox undermines Frege's logical system.
- **1963** — Gettier's three-page paper reopens the analysis of knowledge.
- **1971** — Rawls publishes A Theory of Justice.
- **1980** — Searle states the Chinese room argument.

## Questions to carry

- What is the difference between believing something and having a reason for it?
- If no possible evidence could count against a claim, is that a strength or a sign that the claim says nothing?
- When is deference to an expert reasonable, and when does it become a failure to examine?
- Can a person state an opposing view at its strongest and still reject it? What does that require?
- What, specifically, would change your mind about something you take to be true?
