# Socrates and the examined life

**Period:** Fifth century BCE Athens

**The trial of 399 BCE.** — In the spring of 399 BCE, a man of about seventy stood before a jury of 501 Athenian citizens in the court of the King Archon. His name was Socrates, son of Sophroniscus, of the deme Alopece. Three accusers had brought the charge: Meletus, a young tragic poet; Anytus, a prominent politician and tanner; and Lycon, a public speaker. The indictment, as Plato reports it, was that Socrates did wrong by not acknowledging the gods the city acknowledged, by introducing other new divinities, and by corrupting the young. He was convicted, by a margin that a later report puts at 280 votes to 221, and condemned to death. He drank hemlock in the state prison a month later, after a religious embassy to Delos had returned and the execution could lawfully proceed. He left no writings. Everything known about him comes from other people, and those people disagree.

**The question this unit carries.** — How can a man who claimed to know nothing be the founder of a method of inquiry? The question is not a riddle. It is a historical problem, and it is why the sources have to be read against one another. Socrates' characteristic procedure — the cross-examination that Plato and Xenophon call the elenchus — ends, in almost every dialogue where it is shown at work, not in a settled answer but in a confession of ignorance. He insists he has no wisdom to sell or to teach. Yet he stands at the head of a tradition of reasoned inquiry that runs through Plato, Aristotle, and the schools founded by his companions. Both facts are in the record. The work of interpretation is to hold them together.

**Who he was.** — Socrates was born around 469 BCE, the son of a stonemason and a midwife. He served as an infantryman in the Athenian army at Potidaea (432), Delium (424), and Amphipolis (422), and the tradition that he saved the life of the young Alcibiades at Delium is repeated by several ancient writers. He was poor, and made a point of it: he took no fee, wore a thin cloak, and went barefoot in all weather. He married a woman named Xanthippe, whose name became a byword for a sharp temper, and had three sons. He held public office only once, as a member of the Council of Five Hundred, and it is in that office that two of his few datable political acts fall. In 406, when the assembly, in violation of its own law, voted as a single question to condemn the eight generals who had commanded at the naval battle of Arginusae, Socrates, who happened to be presiding that day, alone refused to put the motion. In 404, during the rule of the Thirty Tyrants, he was ordered to arrest a resident alien named Leon of Salamis for execution; he went home instead, and would probably have been killed had the Thirty not fallen soon afterwards. He was not a quiet man. He spent his days in the marketplace and the wrestling grounds, talking to anyone who would answer, and the young men of wealthy families followed him to watch the talking.

**The sign.** — Socrates said that he was accompanied by a divine something, to daimonion, which he described not as a god but as a voice or sign that came to him, always to restrain, never to urge. It warned him against courses of action; it never told him what to do. This sign is one of the two or three features of his life that appear in every source, and it is also, in the indictment's phrase about "new divinities," one of the things he was tried for. Whether it was a genuine inner experience, a rhetorical device, or something the tradition invented is not recoverable. What is clear is that he treated it as a reason, and that his friends treated it as a fact about him.

**The oracle.** — The story Socrates tells at his trial — that his friend Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the priestess answered that no one was — is central to Plato's *Apology* and to the picture of Socrates as a man on a divine mission. It is also one of the most contested details in the tradition. Xenophon, who knew Socrates and wrote a defence of him, does not mention the oracle in the *Memorabilia*, though his *Apology* gives it a different form. Some scholars take its absence from the *Memorabilia* as evidence that the story was Plato's invention, or at least an elaboration; others note that Xenophon had no reason to mention it there, and that Chaerephon appears in Aristophanes' *Clouds* as a real associate of Socrates. The detail matters because it supplies a motive for the questioning. Without the oracle, Socrates' compulsion to examine everyone looks like a habit or a provocation; with it, the questioning becomes a duty laid on him by a god.

**Why he wrote nothing.** — Socrates wrote no book and left no school notebook. Several explanations are given in the sources and by modern scholars. He belonged to a society in which philosophy was still mostly oral, and the written prose treatise was a new and suspect form. He held, or at least Plato's later Socrates holds, that writing is a dead thing: it cannot answer questions, it says the same thing to everyone, and it gives the appearance of wisdom without the reality. His method required a live partner who could be questioned and could answer back. And he had, on his own account, no doctrine to write down. Whatever the reason, the consequence is fixed: there is no primary document from Socrates himself. The evidence is entirely second-hand.

**The four sources.** — Four ancient writers are the main witnesses, and they do not describe the same man.

Aristophanes, the comic playwright, put Socrates on stage in the *Clouds* in 423 BCE, when Socrates was in his forties and Athens was at war with Sparta. In the play, Socrates runs a "thinking-shop," the phrontisterion, where students study astronomy, geometry, and the art of making the weaker argument defeat the stronger; he is suspended in a basket in the air so that he can think about the sun; he investigates how far a flea can jump and whether a gnat buzzes through its mouth or its tail; and he denies that Zeus exists, replacing him with the Clouds and with a cosmic "Vortex." This Socrates is a natural philosopher and a sophist — a teacher of persuasive speaking for pay. He is not a moral questioner, and he never asks what justice is. Aristophanes was writing comedy, and his target is a type, not a biography. But the play was remembered, and Plato's Socrates blames it, more than any individual accuser, for the prejudice against him.

Plato, born around 428 BCE into an aristocratic Athenian family, was Socrates' student and wrote some thirty-five dialogues over the half-century after Socrates' death. In the earliest of them — *Euthyphro*, *Laches*, *Charmides*, *Lysis*, *Hippias Major*, *Ion*, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias* — Socrates is the central figure, questioning others and disclaiming knowledge. In the middle and late dialogues, especially *Phaedo*, *Republic*, *Symposium*, and *Timaeus*, the character Socrates increasingly speaks for Plato's own metaphysics, psychology, and politics, including the theory of Forms and the immortality of the soul. Scholars divide the dialogues into "Socratic" and "Platonic" periods for exactly this reason, and the division is contested at every point.

Xenophon was another follower, a soldier and country gentleman who was not in Athens for much of Socrates' last decade. His *Memorabilia*, or *Recollections of Socrates*, written around 371 BCE, defends Socrates against the charges and presents him as a pious, practical, and improving adviser: a man who consults the gods, advises friends on farming and friendship, and teaches self-control by example. Xenophon's Socrates has no philosophical system and little irony. The *Memorabilia* is valuable because it is independent of Plato and quotes conversations Plato does not, but Xenophon is a moralist rather than a philosopher, and his portrait is widely thought to be flat.

Aristotle, who was born in 384 BCE, fifteen years after Socrates died, and who entered Plato's Academy in 367, never knew Socrates. In the *Metaphysics* and the *Nicomachean Ethics* he reports, at second hand, that Socrates concerned himself with ethical questions and sought universal definitions, and that he did not separate the Forms — that was Plato's innovation. Aristotle is the earliest surviving writer to treat Socrates as a turning point in the history of philosophy, and his testimony is brief, late, and coloured by his own agenda.

**The Socratic problem.** — These four portraits cannot be fitted together without remainder. The comic Socrates is a scientist and a cheat; the Platonic Socrates is a moral philosopher and a martyr; the Xenophontic Socrates is a pious household adviser; the Aristotelian Socrates is the inventor of definition. The problem of deciding which, if any, is the historical man has been argued since the nineteenth century, when the theologian Friedrich Schleiermacher framed it in an essay of 1818 by asking which dialogues were genuinely Socratic. No consensus has been reached. Some scholars build a "historical Socrates" out of the early dialogues; others argue that even the earliest dialogues are Plato's creations and that the historical Socrates is largely unrecoverable. The dispute is not a failure of scholarship. It is the shape of the evidence. Each source had a reason to write, and the reasons differ: Aristophanes wanted a laugh, Plato wanted a founder for his philosophy, Xenophon wanted to clear a teacher's name, Aristotle wanted to mark the beginning of a discipline.

**The elenchus.** — The method that the early dialogues display is the elenchus, a Greek word for cross-examination, refutation, or test. Its form is stable. Socrates asks an interlocutor for a definition of something important: what is piety, what is courage, what is temperance, what is justice, what is friendship. The interlocutor offers an answer. Socrates then asks a series of further questions and draws out further admissions, and from those admissions he derives a conclusion that contradicts the original answer. The answer is refuted, not by an external authority but by the interlocutor's own beliefs. In the *Euthyphro*, Euthyphro says that the pious is what the gods love. Socrates asks whether the gods disagree about what is just, and Euthyphro agrees that they do. Then the same action is loved by some gods and hated by others, and so is both pious and impious by the proposed definition. In the *Laches*, Laches defines courage as standing one's ground in battle; Socrates points out that a man who runs away may still be courageous, and the definition falls. In the *Charmides*, temperance is defined first as quietness, then as modesty, then as doing one's own business, and each answer breaks against a counterexample.

**The outcome: aporia.** — The elenchus almost never ends with a definition. It ends in aporia, a Greek word for a blocked passage, a state of perplexity in which the interlocutor no longer knows what to say. Euthyphro leaves the conversation and goes off to prosecute his father. Laches and Nicias admit they cannot say what courage is. Charmides is reduced to silence. Socrates does not fill the gap. He says he does not know either. This is the crux of the question this unit carries. A method that produces only ignorance looks like a failure, and Socrates' enemies treated it as one: he was accused of making people worse by unsettling them. But Plato's dialogues are designed so that the reader sees the difference between two kinds of not-knowing. The interlocutor began by thinking he knew and now knows that he does not; Socrates began by thinking he did not know and still does not. The removal of false confidence is itself an achievement, and Socrates treats it as the necessary first step of any inquiry. The oracle at Delphi, in the story Socrates tells at his trial, said that no one was wiser than he; he interpreted the answer to mean that human wisdom is worth little, and that the person who knows his own ignorance is better off than the person who does not.

**Socratic irony.** — The disavowal of knowledge may be sincere, or it may be a pose, or it may be both at once, and the ancient word for the difficulty is eironeia, irony. In fifth-century Athens the word was not complimentary: it meant dissimulation, the habit of hiding what one thinks and saying less than one means. Aristophanes uses it against Socrates, and so do the orators. Socrates' claim that he knows nothing can look like the same trick: he says he is ignorant, and then he demolishes everyone who takes him at his word. The modern debate about whether the disavowal is sincere is one of the central disputes in the field. Gregory Vlastos argued that Socrates' irony is "complex" rather than simple: when Socrates says he does not know, he means that he lacks the expert, infallible knowledge that a craftsman has of his craft, but he may still hold justified true beliefs about how one should live. On this reading the disavowal is literally true and not a lie, and Socrates is a moral philosopher with convictions, not a sceptic. Others read the irony as more corrosive, and some, following the *Theaetetus*, read Socrates as a midwife who has no children of his own — who delivers other people's ideas and contributes none.

**The trial.** — The trial had two stages. In the first, the accusers spoke and Socrates defended himself. Plato's *Apology* gives his version of the defence: Socrates divides his accusers into the "old" accusers, the vague public slander that had grown up over decades and was fed by the *Clouds*, and the present three. He answers the charge of natural philosophy by saying he has no interest in it, and the charge of teaching for pay by saying he takes none. He tells the story of the oracle, and he explains that his questioning is a divine mission. He refuses to beg or to bring his weeping children into court. The jury convicted him. In the second stage, the accusers proposed death, and Socrates was entitled to propose a counter-penalty. He proposed, at first, maintenance at public expense in the Prytaneum, the reward given to Olympic victors; then, on his friends' urging, a fine of thirty minae. The jury chose death. The margin at the second vote was larger than at the first, which suggests that his refusal to conciliate cost him votes.

**The death.** — The execution was delayed for a month because a sacred ship was away at Delos and no execution could take place until it returned. During that month Socrates' friend Crito arranged an escape; the *Crito* records Socrates' refusal. He argues that he has lived under the laws of Athens all his life, has benefited from them, and has no right to destroy them now that they condemn him; to escape would be to do injustice to the city that made him what he was. When the ship returned, he drank the hemlock. Plato's *Phaedo* describes the death, and the description became the founding image of the philosopher who dies for what he believes. How much of the *Phaedo* is Plato's invention cannot be settled, and the same caution applies to the *Apology*. What is not in doubt is that an Athenian jury condemned Socrates to death in 399 and that he was executed.

**The refusal.** — The most quoted lines in the *Apology* are the ones in which Socrates refuses to stop. Offered acquittal on condition that he give up philosophy, he answers that he will obey the god rather than the jury and will not cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy. Asked why he cannot simply keep quiet and go into exile, he says that the unexamined life is not worth living. He compares himself to a gadfly fastened on a large, sluggish horse — the city of Athens — that needs to be kept awake. The refusal is not a death wish and it is not an argument that death is better than life. Socrates says repeatedly that he does not know whether death is good or evil, and that he will not pretend to know. What he does claim to know is that to abandon the inquiry and to disobey what he takes to be the god's command would be wrong. The examined life is worth more than the life that is merely continued. The word translated "examine" is exetasis, which in Athenian usage could mean the military inspection of troops or the preliminary judicial examination of a defendant. To examine a life is to subject it to the kind of scrutiny a general gives an army or a court gives an accused, and to submit to that scrutiny is, for Socrates, the condition of living well at all.

**Contingency.** — The outcome was not fated. Socrates could have been acquitted: Xenophon and Plato both say that a more conciliatory defence would have persuaded the jury, and the narrow first vote shows how few votes separated conviction from acquittal. He could have escaped, and Crito had the money and the plan. He could have gone into exile and, as he says, simply stopped asking questions. He chose none of these. The death of Socrates is a decision, made by a man and by a jury, and reading it as the inevitable fate of philosophy is a mistake. The Athenians who voted to condemn him were not villains, either. The city had just lost a long and ruinous war, had suffered two oligarchic coups, and had seen two of Socrates' own associates, Critias and Alcibiades, become bywords for treason. A jury could reasonably have feared the questioning of the young. Whether it acted justly is a separate question from whether it acted understandably.

**The influence.** — Socrates' most important effect was on the people who wrote about him. Plato founded the Academy around 387 BCE, and the dialogues in which Socrates is the main speaker became the founding texts of Western philosophy; the theory of Forms, the divided line, and the ideal city are Plato's, but they are put in Socrates' mouth, and the boundary between the two men is one of the oldest problems in the field. Several of Socrates' companions founded schools of their own. Antisthenes, who was present at the trial, is counted the ancestor of the Cynics, who preached a life of radical self-sufficiency; Aristippus of Cyrene, who took pleasure to be the good, founded the Cyrenaic school; Euclides of Megara founded the Megarian school, which turned Socratic questioning toward logic and paradox. Xenophon wrote the *Memorabilia* and an *Apology* of his own. Aristotle, who entered the Academy in 367, took from the Socratic search for definitions the beginning of his own logic and ethics. The Stoics of the third century BCE adopted Socrates as a model of the philosopher who is master of himself. Through all of them, the man who wrote nothing became a founder.

**What the sources let us see.** — The three texts in this unit are chosen to show the range of the evidence. Aristophanes gives the hostile caricature that Socrates says did him the most harm. Plato gives the defence and the mission, written by a devoted student. Xenophon gives an independent and more prosaic defence by another follower. None of them is neutral: the comic poet wants laughter, the philosopher wants a founder, the soldier wants a reputation cleared. Read together, they make the Socratic problem visible, and they show why the question about the man who knew nothing is not a trick. The answer the sources permit is partial and contested. Socrates had no doctrine, but he had a practice — a way of testing claims by questioning the person who makes them. He had no answers, but he had a method for showing which answers fail. And he had, by his own account, one piece of knowledge: that he did not know, and that the unexamined life is not worth living.

## Chronology

- **c. 469 BCE** — Socrates is born in the deme Alopece, near Athens.
- **432 BCE** — He serves as a hoplite at the siege of Potidaea.
- **424 BCE** — He serves at the battle of Delium.
- **423 BCE** — Aristophanes' *Clouds* is performed at the City Dionysia; it caricatures Socrates as a natural philosopher and sophist.
- **422 BCE** — He serves at Amphipolis.
- **406 BCE** — As presiding officer of the assembly, he refuses to put the illegal mass condemnation of the generals of Arginusae to a vote.
- **404 BCE** — He refuses the Thirty Tyrants' order to arrest Leon of Salamis.
- **403 BCE** — The Thirty are overthrown and the democracy is restored.
- **399 BCE** — He is tried, convicted, and executed by hemlock.
- **c. 390 BCE** — Plato writes the *Apology* and other early dialogues.
- **c. 387 BCE** — Plato founds the Academy in Athens.
- **c. 371 BCE** — Xenophon writes the *Memorabilia*.
- **367 BCE** — Aristotle enters Plato's Academy.
- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and ethical works report Socrates as the first to seek universal definitions.

## Questions to carry

- If Socrates wrote nothing and claimed to know nothing, what makes the elenchus a method rather than a conversation?
- Aristophanes, Plato, and Xenophon each show a different Socrates. What does each writer's stake in the portrait tell us about the portrait?
- Is Socratic irony a sincere disavowal of knowledge or a strategy of concealment — and can the text decide the question?
- Socrates could have been acquitted, exiled, or helped to escape. What does his refusal of all three tell us about what he thought the examined life was worth?
- Plato put his own doctrines into Socrates' mouth. How much of the philosophy that calls itself Socratic belongs to the teacher and how much to the student?
