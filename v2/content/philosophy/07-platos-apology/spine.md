# Plato's Apology: knowledge and ignorance

**Period:** Fourth century BCE Athens

**A speech, not a transcript.** — The *Apology* opens with Socrates telling the jury that he will not speak like a courtroom orator, and that he will use the words he uses in the marketplace. That opening is a literary gesture as much as a report of one. The work is not a transcript of the trial of 399 BCE; it is a defence speech written by Plato, Socrates' pupil, some years after his teacher was put to death. No stenographer recorded the proceedings. The title comes from the Greek *apologia*, a speech in defence, not an apology in the modern sense of a regret. Plato's text belongs with a small group of Socratic writings — Xenophon's *Apology* and *Memorabilia*, the lost *Accusation of Socrates* by the sophist Polycrates, and the caricature of Socrates in Aristophanes' comedy *Clouds* — and these accounts disagree with one another in detail. Whether the *Apology* preserves Socrates' actual words, a faithful reconstruction of his argument, or Plato's own philosophical portrait is among the oldest disputes about the text, and it cannot be settled from the text alone.

**The trial and the charge.** — In the spring of 399 BCE, a man of about seventy stood before a jury of 501 Athenian citizens. Three men brought the prosecution: Meletus, a poet; Anytus, a politician who had helped restore the democracy after the civil war; and Lycon, a public speaker. The indictment, as Plato reports it, said that Socrates was "a doer of evil, who corrupts the youth; and who does not believe in the gods of the state, but has other new divinities of his own." The penalty proposed was death. The trial lasted a single day.

**The shape of the speech.** — The *Apology* falls into three movements, and the shape tells the reader what kind of text it is. The first and longest is the defence proper, in which Socrates answers the old slander that he studies the heavens and makes the worse appear the better cause, then cross-examines Meletus, then explains the oracle and the mission. The second is the penalty phase, after the guilty verdict, where custom required each side to propose a punishment and Socrates, to the jury's fury, suggests a reward. The third is the address to those who voted for acquittal, in which he argues that death is not an evil and asks them to reprove his sons if they care more for wealth than for virtue. A real defence speech would not be arranged this way. The three movements are a composition, built to carry an argument from accusation to vindication, and the reader who notices the seams is already reading the text as Plato's work rather than as a stenographer's record.

**Impiety under Athenian law.** — Impiety (*asebeia*) had no single statute to define it. A prosecution was a *graphē*, a written public action that any citizen could bring; there was no state prosecutor and no judge to rule on points of law. The jurors (*dikastai*) decided fact and law together by a show of votes, without deliberating among themselves, and each side was timed by a water clock. An accuser who failed to win a fifth of the votes paid a fine of a thousand drachmae, a large sum meant to discourage frivolous suits. If the verdict was guilty, the trial moved to a second phase, the *timēsis*: the accuser proposed one penalty and the defendant proposed another, and the jury chose between them. The charge had two religious limbs, not believing in the gods of the city and introducing new divinities. Neither was the same as atheism. The "new divinities" count fastened on Socrates' *daimonion*, the private sign or voice he said had come to him since childhood, which always forbade and never commanded. To the city's way of thinking, a man who claimed a private channel to the divine was setting up an authority beside the public cult. Whether that was a capital offence in law, and whether Socrates was guilty of it, are separate questions.

**Athens after the war.** — The trial makes sense only against the recent history. The Peloponnesian War against Sparta ended in 404 BCE with Athens defeated, its empire gone, its walls torn down. A Spartan-backed oligarchy, the Thirty Tyrants, then seized power, and in a year of terror killed or exiled many hundreds, perhaps as many as 1,500, and confiscated property; the figure comes from a later orator and is disputed, but the scale of the wound is not. Two leaders of the Thirty, Critias and Charmides, had belonged to Socrates' circle, and so had Alcibiades, the brilliant general whose defection had damaged Athens badly. When the democrats under Thrasybulus overthrew the Thirty in 403, they declared an amnesty for the past and restored the democracy. The memory did not vanish. The men who rebuilt the democracy, Anytus among them, had reason to distrust a teacher who had trained its enemies.

Socrates himself had a record of independence. In 406, serving on the council, he alone opposed an illegal motion to try the generals of the battle of Arginusae en masse, at the risk of his own life. In 404, the Thirty ordered him and four others to arrest Leon of Salamis for execution; he went home instead, and later said he would have died for it had the oligarchy not fallen first. He was not a partisan of the oligarchs. But he was also not a man the restored democracy found safe.

**The Oracle at Delphi.** — Socrates' defence turns on a story about the oracle of Apollo at Delphi. His friend Chaerephon, he says, asked the Pythia whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the priestess answered that no one was. Socrates was puzzled, because he knew he had no wisdom, "small or great," and yet the god could not lie. He decided to test the oracle by finding someone wiser than himself. He went first to a politician of repute, then to the poets, then to the artisans. The politicians and poets, he found, thought they knew what they did not know. The artisans really did know their crafts, but because of that they thought they knew "all sorts of high matters" as well. Each examination made him enemies. His conclusion was that the god was not speaking of Socrates at all: "God only is wise," he says, and the oracle meant that human wisdom is worth little or nothing; the wisest human being is the one who, like Socrates, knows that his wisdom is worth nothing. The mission that followed, examining anyone with a reputation for wisdom, became his life's work and left him, as he says, in "utter poverty."

The story is one of the most contested details in the tradition. Xenophon, who knew Socrates and wrote a defence of him, mentions an oracle but gives it a different form: Apollo declared that no human being was "more liberal, or more upright, or more temperate" than Socrates. Some scholars take the differences as evidence that Plato shaped the tale to supply a motive for the questioning; others note that the two accounts need not conflict, and that Chaerephon appears in Aristophanes as a real associate. The detail matters because it turns a habit into a duty laid on Socrates by a god. Without the oracle, the questioning looks like a provocation; with it, refusing to stop becomes obedience.

**The cross-examination of Meletus.** — The middle of the defence turns the *elenchus* on the prosecutor. Socrates asks Meletus who improves the young, and Meletus answers, "The laws." Asked who the person is who knows the laws, Meletus falls silent, then names the jurors, then the whole citizen body except Socrates, until Socrates observes that every Athenian but himself appears to improve the young and only he corrupts them, which is as likely as one man corrupting horses while everyone else improves them. He presses further: if he corrupts the young, he must do so either willingly or unwillingly; no one wishes to be harmed, and a corrupter would be harmed by the corruption of those around him, so he cannot corrupt willingly; and if unwillingly, the law does not summon a man to court for an involuntary offence but takes him aside and teaches him. The argument is not airtight, and Plato lets it stand as a piece of courtroom fencing rather than a proof. Then comes the charge of impiety. Socrates extracts from Meletus the claim that he teaches the young not to believe in the gods at all, and replies that the indictment contradicts itself: Meletus says he believes in *daimonia*, the doings of divine beings, and anyone who believes in the doings of divine beings must believe in divine beings; so he cannot be an atheist. The exchange shows Socrates' method in public, and it also shows why the jury found him irritating. He does not refute the charge so much as make its author look foolish, and a man who makes his accuser look foolish in front of 501 jurors is not improving his chances.

**What it means to know that you do not know.** — The famous formula is usually remembered as "I know that I know nothing." That is not what the text says, and the difference matters. Jowett's translation has: "he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know." Diogenes Laertius, writing centuries later, reports the saying that Socrates "knew nothing, except the fact of his ignorance."

The distinction is between two conditions, not one. The first is being ignorant. The second is being ignorant while believing oneself wise. Socrates claims to lack the second, and to be free of the conceit that a man knows what he does not know. He does not claim to lack all knowledge. In the same speech he says plainly that he does know something: "that injustice and disobedience to a better, whether God or man, is evil and dishonourable." So the ignorance is not total. It is a claim about the limits of human wisdom, above all about death and the gods, joined to a confidence about how one should live. The fear of death, he says, is "the pretence of wisdom," because it pretends to know that death is an evil when no one knows whether it may not be the greatest good.

The formula raises a puzzle that readers have pressed for centuries. If Socrates knows that he does not know, then he knows at least one thing, and the claim that he knows nothing is false. If he does not know even that, then he has no ground for the confidence with which he questions others. The usual reply is that the two kinds of knowledge are different in kind. Knowing that one does not know a subject is not the same as knowing the subject; it is a second-order awareness of one's own limits, and it is exactly what the oracle's "human wisdom" consists in. On this reading Socrates is not a sceptic who doubts everything, and not a dogmatist who secretly holds a system. He is a man who has located the boundary of what a human being can claim, and who refuses to cross it by pretending. The puzzle is not dissolved by this reply so much as relocated: it becomes a question about what kind of thing Socratic ignorance is.

The method behind the claim is cross-examination, what later writers call the *elenchus*. Socrates asks a person to define a virtue, draws out the implications, and shows that the answers conflict; the usual result is *aporia*, the state of being stuck. The aim is not to leave the interlocutor humiliated but to clear away the false confidence that blocks enquiry. Whether the historical Socrates held positive doctrines of his own, or only cleared the ground, is disputed. In Plato's later dialogues Socrates defends elaborate theories; in the *Apology* he disclaims knowledge and describes himself as a questioner. Gregory Vlastos argued that the Socrates of the early dialogues is a sincere moral philosopher whose profession of ignorance is genuine and whose irony is "complex" — he means what he says about virtue while denying that he knows. Other readers take the disavowal as a rhetorical pose, and still others doubt that the historical Socrates can be recovered from Plato's literary dialogues at all.

**The gadfly and the horse.** — Socrates tells the jury that he is not arguing for his own sake but for theirs, "that you may not sin against the God by condemning me, who am his gift to you." He compares himself to a gadfly and the city to a horse: "if I may use such a ludicrous figure of speech, am a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God; and the state is a great and noble steed who is tardy in his motions owing to his very size, and requires to be stirred into life." The image is not contemptuous. The horse is noble, only slow; the gadfly is a nuisance that keeps it moving. He says the god attached him to Athens, and that if the jurors kill him they will not easily find another, but will "sleep on for the remainder of your lives, unless God in his care of you sent you another gadfly." The sting is the point: the city needs to be annoyed into thought, and no one enjoys the annoyance.

**The refusal to stop.** — The most famous sentence in the speech follows from the mission. Offered acquittal on the condition that he stop philosophizing, Socrates refuses: "Men of Athens, I honour and love you; but I shall obey God rather than you, and while I have life and strength I shall never cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy." He will not, he says, alter his ways "even if I have to die many times." He compares himself to Achilles, who chose to avenge Patroclus and die rather than live in dishonour, and to a soldier who must hold his post. He had held his post at Potidaea, Amphipolis, and Delium; he will hold this one. He also refuses the ordinary theatrics of an Athenian defendant. He will not weep, bring his children forward, or beg. "I would rather die having spoken after my manner," he says, "than speak in your manner and live." When the jury has found him guilty and the penalty phase begins, he declines to propose any penalty he thinks unjust. What he deserves, he says, is maintenance in the Prytaneum, the public dining hall kept for benefactors and Olympic victors. At his friends' urging he offers a fine of thirty minae, secured by Plato and others. Whether this was calculated defiance or the only honest answer he could give is a matter of dispute; either way it helped secure the death sentence.

**The verdict and the death sentence.** — Plato gives the first vote as nearly even: "had thirty votes gone over to the other side, I should have been acquitted." With 501 jurors that implies 280 for conviction and 221 against. After the penalty phase, the majority for death grew. Diogenes Laertius, drawing on other sources, reports that Socrates was condemned by 281 votes, "being six more than were given in his favour," and then sentenced to death by eighty votes more than had found him guilty. His figures do not agree with Plato's, and the arithmetic of the trial cannot be reconstructed with confidence; what is clear is that the verdict was close at the first stage and less close at the second.

**The prison and the hemlock.** — The *Apology* ends before Socrates dies. The execution was delayed for a month because a sacred embassy had sailed to Delos and no execution could take place until it returned; the story is told in Plato's *Phaedo*, which describes the last day and the drinking of the hemlock. Socrates spent the interval in the state prison, talking with his friends, and according to Xenophon he refused an opportunity to escape, asking whether his companions knew of a place outside Attica where death was forbidden to set foot. The sources agree that he met his end calmly and, in Xenophon's words, "with gaiety welcomed death's embrace." The manner of the death became part of the argument. A man who had spent his life saying that he did not know what death was, and who then walked into it without fear, was himself the strongest evidence for the claim that the fear of death is a pretence of wisdom.

**Is death to be feared?** — Socrates' closing argument is not a proof that the soul is immortal. It is a disjunction. Death is one of two things: either a state of nothingness and utter unconsciousness, like a sleep undisturbed even by dreams, or "a change and migration of the soul from this world to another." If it is the first, death is "an unspeakable gain," since eternity is then a single night. If it is the second, he will meet the true judges of the dead and converse with Orpheus, Hesiod, Homer, and the heroes who suffered unjust judgment, and continue his search for knowledge among them. In neither case is death an evil. He adds a sign: the *daimonion* that had so often stopped him in mid-sentence gave no opposition to anything he said or did at the trial, which he takes as proof that what has happened to him is good. The conclusion is "that no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death."

The argument has been much discussed. Its form assumes that the two possibilities exhaust the field, and its second branch assumes that the afterlife, if it exists, is a place of pleasant conversation. Its confidence rests less on evidence than on a moral premise, that a good man cannot truly be harmed, and on the silence of the sign. Plato returns to the question in the *Phaedo*, where he gives a fuller argument for the immortality of the soul; the *Apology* does not.

**Whether Socrates was guilty.** — Under Athenian law the question is genuinely hard, and the sources are all, in one way or another, on Socrates' side. The case for conviction: he did claim a private divine sign, which the charge of "new divinities" fits; he did teach young men to question the authority of fathers, generals, and the assembly; several of his closest associates had been enemies of the democracy; and by his own account he made a practice of humiliating the prominent. The case for acquittal: the charge of atheism is hard to square with his public sacrifices and his insistence that the oracle could not lie; the *daimonion* he interpreted as a sign from the same god the city worshipped, not a rival; and the corruption charge was never given concrete content, only asserted. The trial may have been political in substance and religious in form, a way of settling accounts with the intellectual circle that had produced the Thirty and Alcibiades.

The modern debate reflects the difficulty. I. F. Stone, in *The Trial of Socrates* (1988), argued that Socrates was guilty as charged and that the trial was a legitimate, if harsh, act of a democracy defending itself against an anti-democratic teacher who despised its institutions. Thomas C. Brickhouse and Nicholas D. Smith, in *Socrates on Trial* (1989), argue the opposite: that Socrates was innocent of the formal charges, and that the prosecution's case was philosophically confused. The *Apology* is a defence speech, not a neutral record, and the reader has to weigh what a text written to vindicate its subject can establish.

**The text and its rivals.** — The *Apology* is not the only ancient account, and comparing the accounts is one way to see the seams. Xenophon's Socrates is more conventional and more pious; he argues at his trial that he has always sacrificed at the public altars, and he treats death as a release from the infirmities of age rather than as a metaphysical puzzle. Diogenes Laertius, writing in the third century CE, gathers anecdotes and sayings into a life and shows how little of the detail can be traced to eyewitnesses. Each writer has a purpose. Plato's is to present Socrates as the model of the philosophical life; Xenophon's is to defend a teacher against the charge of impiety; Diogenes' is to collect. None of them is a court record, and none can be read as one. The reader who wants to know what happened at the trial has to reason from texts that were written to persuade.

**The afterlife of the text.** — Whatever the historical Socrates said, the *Apology* made him a founding figure. It supplied the West with its image of the philosopher as a critic who will not trade truth for survival, and with the phrase, "the unexamined life is not worth living." Later traditions read it as the birth of philosophy out of the death of a man, and as a study in the limits of majority rule. The reader's task is not to choose the true Socrates but to see what each source can bear, and to notice where the *Apology* argues and where it merely reports.

## Chronology

- **c. 470 BCE** — Socrates born in the deme of Alopece, son of Sophroniscus, a stonemason, and Phaenarete, a midwife.
- **431–404 BCE** — The Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta.
- **432 BCE** — Socrates serves as a hoplite at Potidaea.
- **424 BCE** — At Delium he is said to have saved the life of Xenophon.
- **423 BCE** — Aristophanes' *Clouds* caricatures Socrates as a natural philosopher.
- **406 BCE** — As a councillor, Socrates alone opposes the illegal mass trial of the generals after Arginusae.
- **404 BCE** — Athens surrenders; the Thirty Tyrants rule; Socrates refuses to arrest Leon of Salamis.
- **403 BCE** — The Thirty are overthrown; the democracy is restored and an amnesty declared.
- **399 BCE** — Socrates is tried and executed in the month of Thargelion.
- **c. 390s BCE** — Plato writes the *Apology* (date disputed).
- **c. 380s BCE** — Xenophon writes his *Apology* (date disputed).
- **c. 200s CE** — Diogenes Laertius compiles the *Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers*.

## Questions to carry

- If Socrates knows that he does not know, what exactly is it that he knows?
- Why does Plato's account, written years later, differ from Xenophon's on the same trial?
- Was the charge of impiety a religious charge or a political one in disguise?
- Does the argument that death is not to be feared depend on anything the jury could check?
- What does the *Apology* gain, and lose, by being a defence speech rather than a record?
