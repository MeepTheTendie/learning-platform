# Epistemology: what can we know?

**Period:** Antiquity to the present

**The midwife's question.** — In Plato's *Theaetetus*, written about 369 BCE, Socrates meets a young mathematician named Theaetetus and asks him what knowledge is. Theaetetus answers with a list — geometry, astronomy, cobbling, carpentry — and Socrates objects that a list of examples is not a definition; asked what clay is, no one is helped by being told that there is clay of potters and clay of brick-makers. Theaetetus then offers three answers in turn: that knowledge is perception, that it is true opinion, and that it is true opinion with an account (*logos*) added. Socrates tests each, and each fails. The failure of the third is the most instructive. A jury, Socrates says, may be persuaded of the truth about a robbery by a clever speaker who was not there, and so hold a true opinion about who did it. The jurors judge rightly, but they judge without knowledge, because they cannot give an account of the matter themselves. The distinction between happening to be right and being in a position to know is the question this unit pursues: what would it take for a belief to count as knowledge rather than lucky opinion?

**The standard analysis: justified true belief.** — The traditional answer, read back into Plato and Locke and made explicit by twentieth-century analytic philosophers, is that knowledge has three parts. First, **truth**: what is known must be so; one cannot know a falsehood, though one can believe it sincerely. Second, **belief**: what is known must be believed; a fact can be true of a person without being known by that person, as a diagnosis may be true of a patient who has never heard it. Third, **justification**: the belief must be supported by evidence, reasons, or a reliable ground; it must not be a lucky guess, a prejudice, or a mere hunch. On the standard analysis these three conditions are each necessary and together sufficient. Suppose I believe it is raining because I can see the rain and hear it on the roof, and it is in fact raining: I know. Suppose instead that I believe it is raining because a fortune-teller told me so, and it happens to rain: I have a true belief, but not knowledge, because the ground of my belief has nothing to do with its truth. The label **justified true belief**, or JTB, became the standard target of discussion through A. J. Ayer's *The Problem of Knowledge* (1956) and Roderick Chisholm's *Theory of Knowledge* (1966).

**The Gettier problem.** — In 1963 the American philosopher Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper in the journal *Analysis* titled "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" It offered two cases in which a person has a justified true belief that is plainly not knowledge, and so appeared to show that the three conditions are not sufficient. In the first case, Smith and Jones apply for a single job. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get it, and also knows that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith therefore believes: the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. Unknown to Smith, he himself will get the job, and he himself also happens to have ten coins in his pocket. His belief is true, he holds it, and by ordinary standards it is justified — yet it is not knowledge. In the second case, Smith justifiably believes that Jones owns a Ford. From this he infers that either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona. As it happens Jones does not own a Ford, but Brown is in Barcelona by pure coincidence, so the disjunction is true and Smith's belief in it is justified. Again the belief is justified and true, and again it is not knowledge. The structure of both cases is the same: the justification rests on a false assumption, and the truth of the conclusion is an accident. Gettier's cases assume two things, both of which were later contested: that a justified belief may rest on a false belief, and that justification is transmitted across deduction. The paper reopened a question that had seemed closed, and the literature it started is still running.

**Repairing the analysis.** — The immediate replies tried to add a fourth condition. Michael Clark, in *Analysis* in 1963, and Keith Lehrer in 1965, proposed a **no-false-lemmas** condition: the justification must not depend on any false belief. Lehrer and Thomas Paxson in 1969 proposed a **defeasibility** condition: there must be no true proposition which, if added to the believer's evidence, would destroy the justification. Alvin Goldman in 1967 proposed a **causal theory**: for a belief to be knowledge, it must be causally connected to the fact that makes it true, as perception is connected to what is seen. Ernest Sosa's "The Raft and the Pyramid" (1980) and Linda Zagzebski's *Virtues of the Mind* (1996) founded **virtue epistemology**, on which knowledge is true belief produced by an intellectual virtue, a competence of the knower rather than a property of the belief alone. Each repair handles some cases and leaves others, and no account has won general assent.

**Radical scepticism: Descartes's demon.** — The most systematic attempt to doubt everything that can be doubted is in René Descartes's *Meditations on First Philosophy*, published in Latin in 1641 and in French in 1647. Descartes begins by noting that his senses have sometimes deceived him, and resolves to withhold assent from anything that admits of the least doubt. He observes that in dreams he has been convinced of the same sort of things he now perceives while awake, and that no certain marks distinguish the two states; so he supposes that he is dreaming. Even so, arithmetic and geometry seem to survive, since two and three make five whether one is asleep or awake. To unsettle even these, he introduces the **evil demon**: "I will suppose, then, not that Deity, who is sovereignly good and the fountain of truth, but that some malignant demon, who is at once exceedingly potent and deceitful, has employed all his artifice to deceive me." The demon might make the sky, the air, the earth, colours, figures, and sounds nothing but illusions, and might corrupt even the simplest calculation. In the *Second Meditation* Descartes looks for a point that survives. Let the demon deceive him as it may: "this proposition, *I am, I exist*, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind." To be deceived is to think, and to think is to exist. This is the **cogito** — from the Latin *cogito ergo sum*, "I think, therefore I am" — though the phrase itself appears in the *Discourse on Method* of 1637, not in the *Meditations*.

**The foundation and the circle.** — The cogito gives Descartes a foundation on which to rebuild: a belief that cannot be doubted even under the demon hypothesis. This is the paradigm of **foundationalism**, the view that knowledge rests on a layer of basic beliefs which support the rest without needing support themselves. But the cogito establishes very little. It tells Descartes only that he exists as a thinking thing; it does not tell him that his senses are trustworthy, that other minds exist, or that there is a material world. To reach those conclusions he argues, in the *Third Meditation*, that God exists and is no deceiver, and therefore that clear and distinct perceptions are true. Antoine Arnauld, in the *Fourth Objections* of 1641, made the obvious objection: the proof that God is no deceiver itself relies on the rule that whatever is clearly and distinctly perceived is true, and that rule was accepted only because God guarantees it. The argument appears to move in a circle. Descartes denied the charge, distinguishing what is clearly perceived in the present moment from what is merely remembered, but the **Cartesian circle** has remained a standing difficulty for any foundationalism that begins from a single indubitable point.

**Rationalism and empiricism.** — Where does the material of knowledge come from? Two families of early modern philosophers answered differently. The **rationalists** — Descartes, Baruch Spinoza (*Ethics*, 1677), and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (*New Essays on Human Understanding*, written 1704, published 1765) — held that reason can establish substantial truths about the world without relying on the senses, and that some ideas are **innate**, part of the mind's own furniture. The **empiricists** held that all the materials of thought come from experience. John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690) argued that the mind begins as white paper, a **tabula rasa**, and that there are no innate principles; every idea is traceable to sensation or to reflection on the mind's own operations. Leibniz answered with the scholastic tag that nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses — "except the intellect itself," since the mind contributes its own forms. The dispute is concrete. When a child learns that every event has a cause, has the child read that off experience, or has the mind supplied it? When geometers prove a truth about all triangles, do they consult the drawing on the page, or the idea in the mind? Rationalists point to mathematics and to the idea of God; empiricists point to the dependence of thought on the senses, and to the cases of a blind person who cannot form the idea of colour.

**The veil of ideas.** — For all their differences, rationalists and empiricists shared a picture of perception that would prove unstable. On this picture the mind does not perceive tables and trees directly; it perceives **ideas** or impressions caused in it by external objects. Locke called these the materials of knowledge, and distinguished **primary qualities** — solidity, extension, figure, motion — which really resemble their causes in bodies, from **secondary qualities** — colours, sounds, tastes, smells — which are only powers in bodies to produce sensations in us, with no resemblance to anything outside. Robert Boyle had made a similar distinction, and Galileo before him. The picture is called **representationalism** or indirect realism, and it raises an awkward question: if what one is directly aware of is always an idea, how can one ever compare the idea with the object to learn whether it resembles it? The idea seems to stand as a **veil** between the mind and the world. George Berkeley drew the radical conclusion in *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710): there is no material substance behind the ideas, and to be is to be perceived — *esse est percipi*. Bodies are collections of ideas, and their existence consists in their being perceived by some mind, finite or divine. David Hume, in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), took the empiricist premise further still, denying that we ever perceive a necessary connexion between cause and effect, and locating that connexion in the mind's own habit of expectation.

**The argument from illusion.** — A parallel route to the veil starts from error. A straight stick half in water looks bent. A white wall under a coloured light looks red. A square tower at a distance looks round. In each case the way things appear differs from the way they are. The argument concludes that what is directly perceived in experience cannot be the ordinary object, since the object is one way and the appearance another; what is directly perceived must be a mental item, a **sense-datum**, from which the object is inferred. A. J. Ayer set out the argument in *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940). The **argument from hallucination** presses harder: a person hallucinating a dagger may have an experience subjectively indistinguishable from seeing a real one, and a hallucination has no object at all; if the two experiences can be indistinguishable, whatever is present to the mind in the good case must be the same kind of thing as in the bad case. Direct or **naive realism**, which holds that perceivers are directly acquainted with mind-independent objects, must explain how illusion and hallucination are possible without conceding the veil. **Disjunctivism**, associated with J. M. Hinton's *Visual Experiences* (1967), John McDowell's "Criteria, Defeasibility, and Knowledge" (1982), and M. G. F. Martin's "The Transparency of Experience" (2002), denies that veridical perception and hallucination share a common experiential core: they are different kinds of mental state that the perceiver may be unable to tell apart from the inside.

**A priori and a posteriori.** — A second distinction cuts across the rationalist–empiricist dispute. **A priori** knowledge is knowledge that does not depend on experience for its justification: the truths of logic and mathematics, and such claims as that all bachelors are unmarried. **A posteriori** knowledge depends on experience: that water boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level, that there is a city called Paris. The terms are not about the order in which beliefs are acquired — a child learns arithmetic by counting objects — but about the source of their justification. Hume drew a closely related line in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), dividing "all the objects of human reason or enquiry" into **relations of ideas**, which are discoverable by thought alone and whose denial is a contradiction, and **matters of fact**, "whose contrary is still possible," since it can be conceived without contradiction. That the sun will rise tomorrow, and that it will not, are equally intelligible.

**Analytic and synthetic.** — Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; second edition 1787) crossed the two distinctions to produce a fourfold map. A judgment is **analytic** when its predicate is already contained in its subject — "all bodies are extended" — and **synthetic** when the predicate adds something not contained in the subject — "all bodies are heavy." Analytic judgments are a priori, and it had been assumed that synthetic judgments are a posteriori. Kant argued that some judgments are **synthetic a priori**: "7 + 5 = 12" is not analytic, because the concept of twelve is not contained in the concepts of seven, five, and addition, yet it is necessary and universal, and so cannot rest on experience. The same, he held, is true of the principle that every event has a cause. Whether there really are synthetic a priori judgments is one of the great dividing questions of philosophy. The empiricist tradition says no. Kant's distinction itself was attacked by W. V. O. Quine in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), who argued that no principled line separates analytic from synthetic truths, that the notion of meaning on which the line depends is obscure, and that beliefs face the test of experience not one by one but as a corporate body. H. P. Grice and P. F. Strawson replied in "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956) that the distinction is indispensable and Quine's objections miss it.

**The problem of induction.** — The most famous difficulty in the theory of knowledge is stated in Section IV of Hume's *Enquiry*. All reasoning about matters of fact beyond present sense and memory, Hume argues, rests on the relation of cause and effect, and knowledge of that relation comes only from experience, never from reason alone: a person presented with a wholly new object could not, by inspecting it, discover its causes or effects. But how does experience justify the step to the future? Hume considers the two possible answers and rejects both. It cannot be **demonstrative** reasoning, because no contradiction follows from the course of nature changing; a body that falls from the clouds might, for all logic shows, taste of salt or feel like fire. It cannot be **probable** reasoning either, because all probable arguments about matters of fact already assume that the future will resemble the past, and using that assumption to prove it is going in a circle. "It is impossible, therefore, that any arguments from experience can prove this resemblance of the past to the future; since all these arguments are founded on the supposition of that resemblance." Hume's conclusion is not that induction should be abandoned but that reason does not produce the inference; custom or habit does. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life," he writes, and as an agent he is satisfied, while as a philosopher he wants to learn the foundation of the inference. Later responses include Hans Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication in *Experience and Prediction* (1938), Karl Popper's proposal in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934; English 1959) that science proceeds by falsification rather than induction, and Nelson Goodman's new riddle of induction in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955).

**Foundationalism and coherentism.** — Any chain of justification faces a trilemma. If every belief needs a further belief to support it, the chain runs backwards forever; if it returns to where it began, the argument is circular; if it stops at a belief accepted without support, that stopping point is arbitrary. Sextus Empiricus set out the modes in *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* around 200 CE; Hans Albert later called the difficulty the **Münchhausen trilemma** (1968). Foundationalism accepts the third horn and argues that some beliefs are justified without inference — self-evident, incorrigible, or given directly in experience. Coherentism rejects the third horn: there are no basic beliefs, and a belief is justified by its coherence with the rest of one's beliefs, as a hypothesis is supported by fitting a theory. Laurence BonJour attacked foundationalism in "Can Empirical Knowledge Have a Foundation?" (1978) and then defended a coherence theory in *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (1985). He built on Wilfrid Sellars's "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956), which argued that the idea of a pure "given" in experience is a myth, and on Donald Davidson's "A Coherence Theory of Truth and Knowledge" (1983). The standing objection to coherentism is the **isolation objection**: a perfectly coherent system of beliefs might have no connection to the world at all, like a well-told fairy tale, and coherence alone seems unable to rule that out.

**Reliabilism and externalism.** — The Gettier problem and the regress both press the question of what makes a belief justified, and a family of answers locates justification outside the believer's awareness. **Internalism** holds that whatever justifies a belief must be accessible to the believer on reflection. **Externalism** denies this. Alvin Goldman's "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) proposed **reliabilism**: a belief is justified when it is produced by a reliable cognitive process, one that tends to yield true beliefs — perception, memory, and sound reasoning qualify; wishful thinking and guessing do not. On this view a person may be justified without being able to say why, and may fail to be justified even when the belief feels certain. Goldman's earlier "A Causal Theory of Knowing" (1967) had made knowledge depend on the right causal connection between belief and fact. BonJour's clairvoyance case (1980) is the standard objection: a man who unknowingly has a reliable clairvoyant power, and believes on that basis that the president is in New York, seems to have no justification, because he has no reason to trust the power. The **generality problem** is a second objection: a belief is produced by many processes at many levels of description, and reliabilism must say which one is relevant, since some are reliable and some are not. Richard Feldman (1985) and Earl Conee and Feldman (1998) developed this criticism.

**What the question comes to.** — Knowledge, on the standard analysis, is true belief plus something that rules out luck. The Gettier cases show how hard it is to say what that something is; the sceptic shows that the demand for certainty may leave almost nothing standing; the regress shows that any chain of reasons must end somewhere; and externalism suggests the ending need not be visible to the believer. Theaetetus's third answer — true opinion with an account — remains the ancestor of the whole debate, and the jury, persuaded of the truth without being able to give an account, remains its clearest illustration. Whether knowledge is justified true belief, or true belief from a reliable source, or true belief that manifests a competence, or something else again, is not settled. What is settled is that the difference between knowledge and lucky opinion is not merely the difference between truth and falsehood, and that saying exactly what more is required has occupied philosophers from the *Theaetetus* to the present.

## Chronology

- **c. 369 BCE** — Plato's *Theaetetus* examines perception, true opinion, and true opinion with an account, and ends without a definition.
- **c. 200 CE** — Sextus Empiricus sets out the sceptical modes, including the regress that later becomes the trilemma.
- **1641** — Descartes's *Meditations on First Philosophy* states the method of doubt, the evil demon, and the cogito.
- **1641** — Antoine Arnauld's *Fourth Objections* raises the Cartesian circle.
- **1690** — Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* defends the tabula rasa and the primary–secondary quality distinction.
- **1710** — Berkeley's *Principles of Human Knowledge* argues that to be is to be perceived.
- **1748** — Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* states the problem of induction in Section IV.
- **1781/1787** — Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* distinguishes analytic from synthetic and a priori from a posteriori, and claims synthetic a priori knowledge.
- **1951** — Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" attacks the analytic–synthetic distinction.
- **1956** — Sellars's "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" attacks the Myth of the Given; Grice and Strawson defend the analytic–synthetic distinction.
- **1963** — Gettier's "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" presents cases of justified true belief that are not knowledge.
- **1967** — Goldman's "A Causal Theory of Knowing" makes knowledge depend on a causal connection.
- **1976/1985** — BonJour defends a coherence theory of empirical knowledge.
- **1979** — Goldman's "What Is Justified Belief?" states reliabilism.
- **1983** — Davidson's "A Coherence Theory of Truth and Knowledge."
- **2002** — Martin's "The Transparency of Experience" states a version of disjunctivism.

## Questions to carry

- Why is each of the three conditions on knowledge necessary, and what does the Gettier case show about whether they are sufficient?
- Descartes doubts the senses, then dreams, then arithmetic. At what point does the doubt become unreasonable, and by what standard?
- If the empiricists are right that all ideas come from experience, where does the idea of a necessary connexion come from?
- Does coherentism's isolation objection show that coherence can never be enough, or only that a coherent system must also fit experience?
- If a reliable process can justify a belief without the believer knowing why, what is left of the demand that one give an account of what one knows?
