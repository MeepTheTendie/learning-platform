# Revolution and the Constitution

**Period:** c. 1763–1789 · **Region:** British North America and the United States

## From empire to crisis

**The peace of 1763 and its bill.** — The Treaty of Paris of February 1763 gave Britain Canada and the land east of the Mississippi, took Florida from Spain, and ended France's claim to North America. The war had doubled Britain's empire and nearly doubled its debt, to about £133 million — more than the annual output of the whole British economy, and roughly half of it borrowed to defend the colonies. British ministers concluded that the colonies, long used to loose enforcement called "salutary neglect," should now help pay for their own defence. George Grenville, chancellor of the Exchequer from 1763, built a system: keep 10,000 soldiers in America, tax the colonies for part of their cost, and enforce the customs laws long ignored. London also issued the Proclamation of 1763 forbidding settlement west of the Appalachians — a line the colonists largely ignored. Stationing an army in America and making Americans pay for it was the root of everything that followed.

**The Sugar Act and the Stamp Act.** — The Sugar Act of 1764 cut the duty on foreign molasses but tightened collection and tried offenders in vice-admiralty courts, where there was no jury — a tax in fact if not in name. The Stamp Act of 1765 was the first direct tax Parliament laid on the colonies: printed paper — newspapers, almanacs, legal documents, playing cards, even diplomas — had to carry a stamp bought from the crown. Grenville expected about £60,000 a year, a fraction of the army's cost, but the principle mattered more. Parliament had taxed colonial trade before; it had never taxed the colonists directly, and the colonists sent no representatives to Westminster. Their objection was not that the tax was heavy — it was light — but that it was laid by a body in which they had no seats. "No taxation without representation" raised a question the empire could not answer.

**Virtual against actual representation.** — British officials answered with virtual representation: every member of Parliament represented the whole empire, not merely the borough that elected him, so the colonists were represented without their own members. The colonists replied that a member who had never seen America and owed his seat to a few hundred English voters could not represent a farmer in Virginia. Some, like James Otis of Massachusetts, conceded that Parliament might regulate trade for the good of the whole empire; almost none conceded that it could take their property by taxation. The distinction between regulation and taxation could not hold a line. If Parliament could tax a stamp, it could tax land, and if it could tax land it could tax anything.

**The Stamp Act Congress and the mobs.** — In October 1765 delegates from nine colonies met in New York as the Stamp Act Congress and resolved that the colonists owed the same allegiance to the crown as Englishmen and were entitled to the same rights; that taxes could not be imposed on them except by their own legislatures; and that the Stamp Act had a "manifest tendency to subvert the rights and liberties of the colonists." In the streets the argument was made differently: the Sons of Liberty, organized in Boston and other towns, burned stamped paper, hanged effigies of stamp agents, and sacked officials' houses. Every stamp distributor in America was forced to resign. The act took effect on 1 November 1765 and never collected a shilling. Parliament repealed it in March 1766 — but passed the Declaratory Act the same day, asserting that it had "full power and authority to make laws and statutes… to bind the colonies and people of America… in all cases whatsoever." The repeal settled nothing; it postponed the question.

**Townshend and the occupation of Boston.** — In 1767 Charles Townshend, chancellor of the Exchequer, tried again with duties on imports the colonists could not make: glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea. The revenue would pay the salaries of governors and judges, freeing them from dependence on colonial legislatures. Two regiments arrived in Boston in 1768 to protect the customs commissioners. On 5 March 1770 a crowd threw snowballs and stones at a squad of soldiers; the soldiers fired, killing five men, among them Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native descent. Paul Revere's engraving of the "Boston Massacre" made the dead into martyrs. The same day, in London, Lord North moved to repeal most of the Townshend duties — except the one on tea, kept to preserve the principle.

**The Tea Act and the Boston Tea Party.** — For three years the crisis subsided. Then in 1773 Parliament passed the Tea Act, letting the East India Company sell tea in America through its own agents free of the English duty it paid on re-export, undercutting colonial merchants. The colonists saw a monopoly and a trap: cheap tea was bait to make them accept Parliament's right to tax. In every port, consignees were forced to resign. On the night of 16 December 1773, before a crowd of several thousand, some sixty men — some disguised as Mohawk — boarded three ships in Boston harbour and dumped 342 chests of tea, about 92,000 pounds worth some £9,000, into the water. The destruction was deliberate, public, and disciplined; it was also property destruction no government could ignore.

**The Intolerable Acts and the First Congress.** — London responded in 1774 with the Coercive Acts, known in America as the Intolerable Acts. The Boston Port Act closed the port until the tea was paid for; the Massachusetts Government Act voided the colony's charter and made its council appointed rather than elected; a new Quartering Act required colonists to house soldiers; and the Administration of Justice Act let royal officials accused of crimes be tried in Britain. Parliament also passed the Quebec Act, extending Quebec's boundary to the Ohio River and protecting the Catholic Church there — which the colonists read as a threat to their western claims and their Protestant religion. In September 1774 the First Continental Congress met in Philadelphia with delegates from twelve colonies, endorsed the Suffolk Resolves calling the Coercive Acts unconstitutional and urging the colonies to arm, and agreed to a Continental Association boycotting British goods. It did not yet declare independence; most delegates still hoped the king would intervene against his Parliament. The king did not.

**Lexington and Concord.** — On 19 April 1775 General Thomas Gage sent some 700 soldiers from Boston to seize arms at Concord. Riders, including Paul Revere and William Dawes, spread the alarm. On Lexington Green a small company of militia faced the redcoats; a shot was fired — by whom is unknown — and eight Americans were killed. At the North Bridge in Concord the militia turned back the British, and on the road back to Boston the column was harried by thousands of armed farmers firing from behind stone walls and trees. The British lost 73 killed and some 200 wounded; the Americans about 50 dead. In June, at Bunker Hill, the colonists held a hill against two frontal assaults before running out of powder; the British took it at a cost of more than 1,000 casualties. The war had begun, though independence had not been declared.

## The argument about authority

**The pamphlet war.** — The deepest work of the Revolution was done in print. Between 1763 and 1776 the colonies produced hundreds of pamphlets, sermons, and newspaper essays arguing about where authority came from and what made it legitimate. They drew on English radical Whig thought: John Locke on natural rights and the social contract, and the "country" opposition's fear of standing armies and corrupt ministers. Bernard Bailyn, in *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967), argued that these pamphlets are not decoration but the engine of the Revolution: the colonists had absorbed a coherent set of ideas about liberty and power, and they acted on them. Later historians have asked how far those ideas were shared beyond the men who wrote them, and how much they were shaped by class, region, and religion.

**Common Sense.** — In January 1776 Thomas Paine published *Common Sense*, written in plain prose for ordinary readers. He attacked not only Parliament but monarchy itself, calling hereditary kingship a fraud and the king a "royal brute." He argued that America's connection to Britain dragged it into European wars, that the colonies were too large to be governed from an island, and that the time for reconciliation had passed. The pamphlet sold, by some estimates, 120,000 to 150,000 copies in a population of about 2.5 million — one of the most widely read political works ever printed in America. Paine's argument was about more than taxes: it was about what kind of government a free people could build, and it made republican government, without a king, thinkable.

**The Declaration of Independence.** — On 2 July 1776 the Continental Congress voted for independence; on 4 July it adopted the Declaration, drafted by Thomas Jefferson with edits by John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, and the Congress. Its first half states a general theory: all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness; governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed; and when a government becomes destructive of those ends, the people may alter or abolish it. The second half is a bill of indictment listing the king's offences — dissolving legislatures, quartering troops, cutting off trade, taxing without consent, and inciting "domestic insurrection" and the "merciless Indian savages." The list is selective, blaming the king for acts of Parliament. Jefferson's draft also condemned the slave trade as a "cruel war against human nature itself"; the Congress struck the paragraph out at the insistence of Georgia and South Carolina. The claim that all men are created equal was made by men who held slaves — a contradiction the document does not resolve.

**Who was included.** — The Revolution's language of liberty circulated among people the Founders did not have in mind. Enslaved people in New England petitioned for freedom, invoking the same natural rights; in Massachusetts, Elizabeth Freeman sued for her freedom in 1781 and won, on the ground that the state constitution's declaration that all men are born free and equal made slavery unlawful there. Abigail Adams wrote to her husband in March 1776 to "remember the ladies," warning that women would not hold themselves bound by laws in which they had no voice. The New Jersey constitution of 1776 gave the vote to "all inhabitants" worth fifty pounds, and for a time propertied women and free Black people voted there, until the legislature restricted the franchise to white men in 1807. The organizing question — what kind of government did the Revolution create, and for whom? — is already visible in these conflicts.

## The war

**The campaigns.** — In the summer of 1776 a British army under General William Howe drove Washington's army out of New York and across New Jersey; the American cause looked finished. On Christmas night Washington crossed the Delaware and struck the Hessian garrison at Trenton, then won again at Princeton. In 1777 the British plan called for General John Burgoyne to march south from Canada and meet Howe coming north; Howe instead sailed to Philadelphia, leaving Burgoyne unsupported. In October 1777 Burgoyne surrendered nearly 6,000 men at Saratoga, New York. The victory convinced France that the Americans could win: in February 1778 France signed treaties of alliance and commerce with the United States, and Spain and the Netherlands joined the war against Britain later. In 1781 a French fleet under Admiral de Grasse blocked Chesapeake Bay while Washington and the French general Rochambeau trapped Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia; on 19 October 1781 Cornwallis surrendered about 7,000 men. The Treaty of Paris, signed in 1783, recognized American independence and set the western boundary at the Mississippi.

**Who fought, and why.** — The Continental Army never numbered more than about 18,000 fit for duty at once, and it was always short of food, clothing, and pay. About 200,000 Americans served at some point, most in the militia for short terms; perhaps 25,000 to 40,000 died, more from disease than battle. Black men served in both armies: the British offered freedom to enslaved people who escaped to their lines, and tens of thousands fled, while some 5,000 served in the Continental Army, including most of the 1st Rhode Island Regiment. Native nations were divided, mostly trying to preserve their lands by playing the empires against each other; the Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans, most of the Iroquois Confederacy and the Shawnee and Cherokee with Britain. Women followed the armies as cooks, laundresses, and nurses, and a few, like Deborah Sampson, disguised themselves as men and enlisted. The Revolution was a civil war among colonists as well as a war against Britain. Between one-fifth and one-third of the population remained loyal to the crown, and perhaps 60,000 to 80,000 loyalists left after 1783.

## The Articles of Confederation

**A league of states.** — The Articles of Confederation, drafted in 1777 and ratified in March 1781 after Virginia gave up its western land claims, created not a nation but a "firm league of friendship" among thirteen sovereign states. There was a one-house Congress, each state with one vote, and no executive and no national judiciary. Congress could declare war, make treaties, and run the post office, but it could not levy taxes or regulate commerce. It could only "requisition" money from the states, which often refused. Amendments required unanimous consent of all thirteen. The Articles reflected the colonists' fear of concentrated power — the very thing they had just fought — and their loyalty to their own colonies, now calling themselves states.

**The weaknesses.** — Congress won the war and negotiated the peace but could not pay for either. By 1783 the national debt was about $43 million, much of it owed to soldiers and foreign lenders, and Congress had no dependable revenue; it printed paper money that collapsed, then stopped. It could not compel Britain to give up its western forts or open its markets to American ships, and could not prevent the states from taxing each other's goods or issuing their own currencies. Madison, studying ancient and modern confederacies, concluded that a league of sovereign states could not hold together, because each state would pursue its own interest and the center had no power to check it. In 1783 unpaid army officers threatened to march on Congress; Washington talked them down, but the episode showed how thin the government's authority was.

**Shays's Rebellion.** — The crisis came to a head in western Massachusetts. After the war, taxes rose to pay state war debts, and farmers who could not pay faced seizure of their land and imprisonment for debt. In August 1786 a former Continental Army captain named Daniel Shays led several hundred farmers to shut down the courts in Northampton, preventing foreclosures. The movement spread; in January 1787 Shays's men marched on the federal armory at Springfield. The state raised a militia paid for by Boston merchants and crushed the rising at Petersham in February. The rebellion was small and failed, but its effect was large. To many, it showed that a republic could be torn apart by debt-ridden farmers and that the national government had no army to stop them. To others, it showed that the eastern merchant class would use the state to protect its own property. Both readings pointed toward a stronger national government.

## The Convention and the Constitution

**The Philadelphia Convention.** — In May 1787 delegates from twelve states met in Philadelphia, ostensibly to revise the Articles. They instead wrote a new frame of government, meeting in secret, with windows closed against the summer heat, so they could argue freely. George Washington presided; James Madison took notes that are the fullest record of the debates and came to be called the "Father of the Constitution." The delegates were mostly lawyers, planters, merchants, and public creditors — men of property and experience, not a cross-section of the population. They agreed that government must be republican, that it needed more power than the Articles gave it, and that it should be checked by a division of powers. They disagreed about how power should be shared among large and small states, and about slavery.

**The Virginia Plan and the New Jersey Plan.** — Madison and the Virginia delegation proposed a national government with a two-house legislature, an executive, and a judiciary; representation in both houses would be based on population or wealth, giving the large states control. Small states objected. William Paterson of New Jersey offered a counter-plan that kept the one-state-one-vote Congress and gave it only slightly more power. For weeks the convention deadlocked. The Connecticut delegation, led by Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth, proposed the compromise that broke it: a House apportioned by population, where the large states would dominate, and a Senate with two votes per state, where the small states would be equal. The "Great Compromise" was adopted in July. It was a bargain, and it left the Senate as the protector of state power.

**The three-fifths clause and slavery.** — The hardest issue was slavery. Southern delegates wanted enslaved people counted for representation; northern delegates objected that if enslaved people were property they should not be counted as persons for political power, and if they were persons they should not be property. The convention settled on the three-fifths clause: for both representation and direct taxes, each enslaved person would count as three-fifths of a free person. The clause gave the southern states extra seats in the House and extra votes in the Electoral College, without giving the enslaved any rights at all. The convention also agreed that Congress could not prohibit the importation of enslaved people before 1808, and added a fugitive slave clause. The word "slavery" does not appear in the document — the framers chose euphemisms — but the institution is written into its structure.

**The structure of the new government.** — The Constitution created a federal system: power divided between a national government and the states, the national government supreme in its sphere. Congress could tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, coin money, raise an army and navy, and make all laws "necessary and proper" to carry out its powers. The executive was a single president, chosen not by direct popular vote but by an Electoral College of electors chosen by the states, with a four-year term. The judiciary was a Supreme Court and such lower courts as Congress might create. Each branch could check the others: the president could veto, Congress could override by two-thirds, the Senate could reject treaties and appointments, and the courts could strike down laws. Article V allowed amendment by a demanding process. Article VII provided that the Constitution would take effect when nine of the thirteen states ratified it in specially elected conventions — not in the state legislatures, which the framers feared would reject it.

**Ratification and the Federalist.** — The fight over ratification was the first national political campaign. The Federalists, who supported the Constitution, included Washington, Madison, and Hamilton. The Antifederalists included George Mason and Patrick Henry of Virginia, Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts, and the anonymous "Brutus" of New York. They feared that a distant, powerful central government would destroy the states, concentrate wealth, and threaten liberty, and they pointed out that the Constitution contained no bill of rights. To answer them, Hamilton, Madison, and John Jay wrote eighty-five newspaper essays under the name "Publius" — the Federalist Papers. Federalist No. 10, by Madison, argued that a large republic would control the "mischiefs of faction" better than a small one, because a greater variety of interests would make it harder for any single faction to form a majority and oppress the rest. Federalist No. 51 argued that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition," and that the structure of government must supply "by opposite and rival interests" the defects of better motives. Federalist No. 84, by Hamilton, argued that a bill of rights was unnecessary; the argument failed, and the promise of one was decisive in several states. Virginia ratified by 89 to 79 and New York by 30 to 27, both only after that promise. The Constitution took effect in 1789; the first ten amendments, drafted by Madison, were ratified in 1791.

**Who counted, and who did not.** — The franchise was limited. Most states required voters to own property or pay taxes; New Jersey alone briefly allowed propertied women and free Black people to vote, then took the right away. Enslaved people were counted for representation but could not vote, and the three-fifths clause gave their owners more power in Congress. Native nations were treated as foreign peoples whose land could be taken by treaty or war, not as citizens. The Constitution protected property, including property in human beings, and left the states to decide who could vote. The Revolution had created a republic in which the people were sovereign — but the people, in practice, were white men of some property, and the boundaries of that category were the central political question for decades.

## Historians disagree

**Beard and the economic interpretation.** — In 1913 Charles A. Beard published *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution of the United States*, arguing that the framers were not disinterested patriots but men whose financial interests — public securities, land, shipping, manufacturing — would be served by a stronger national government. Beard's book changed how the Constitution was studied by asking whose interests it served. His evidence was incomplete and his method crude by later standards, and he was answered in the 1950s and after by historians who showed that the delegates' interests were varied and that many men with the same economic stake opposed the Constitution. But the question Beard raised — cui bono, who benefits? — has never gone away.

**Bailyn, Wood, and the ideological and social revolutions.** — Bernard Bailyn's *Ideological Origins* (1967) located the Revolution's causes in the political ideas the colonists had absorbed from English opposition writers. Gordon S. Wood's *The Radicalism of the American Revolution* (1992) went further, arguing that the Revolution transformed American society itself, replacing a world of hierarchy, patronage, and dependence with a society of equality where ordinary people claimed the right to govern themselves. Wood's critics ask whether the change was as complete as he says, pointing to the persistence of slavery, of limits on the vote, and of the power of the wealthy. The debate turns on what counts as the Revolution — the war and the founding documents, or the slower changes in how people lived.

## Chronology

- **1763** — Treaty of Paris ends the Seven Years' War; Britain issues the Proclamation of 1763.
- **1764** — The Sugar Act tightens customs collection and tries offenders without juries.
- **1765** — The Stamp Act imposes the first direct tax; the Stamp Act Congress meets in New York; the Sons of Liberty organize.
- **1766** — Parliament repeals the Stamp Act and passes the Declaratory Act.
- **1767** — The Townshend Acts tax imports and pay royal salaries.
- **1770** — The Boston Massacre; Parliament repeals the Townshend duties except the tax on tea.
- **1773** — The Tea Act; the Boston Tea Party destroys 342 chests of tea.
- **1774** — The Coercive (Intolerable) Acts; the Quebec Act; the First Continental Congress meets.
- **April 1775** — Lexington and Concord; the siege of Boston begins.
- **June 1775** — The Battle of Bunker Hill.
- **January 1776** — Thomas Paine publishes *Common Sense*.
- **4 July 1776** — The Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence.
- **1777** — The British surrender at Saratoga.
- **1778** — France signs treaties of alliance and commerce with the United States.
- **1781** — The Articles of Confederation take effect; Cornwallis surrenders at Yorktown.
- **1783** — The Treaty of Paris recognizes American independence.
- **1786–87** — Shays's Rebellion in western Massachusetts.
- **1787** — The Constitutional Convention meets in Philadelphia; the Constitution is signed on 17 September.
- **1787–88** — The Federalist Papers appear; the states ratify the Constitution.
- **1789–91** — The new government takes office; the first ten amendments are drafted and ratified.

## Questions to carry

- What did the colonists mean by "representation," and why could neither side concede the point?
- The Declaration says all men are created equal. Who was making that claim, and what did they do about slavery?
- Why did the Articles of Confederation fail — bad design, or a states' rights commitment that no design could overcome?
- Who gained, and who lost, from the Constitution of 1787?
- Did the Revolution change American society, or only its government? How would the answer look different from the perspective of a farmer, an enslaved person, a merchant, and a woman?
