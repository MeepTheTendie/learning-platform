# The Progressive Era

**Period:** c. 1890–1917 · **Region:** The United States

**A word that claimed to be a direction.** — The people who called themselves "progressive" agreed that industrial society had produced evils government should correct, and disagreed about nearly everything else: whether to break up the great corporations or merely regulate them, and what to do about the newest immigrants. Almost all wrote off black Southerners and the foreign-born as people to be managed rather than voters to be courted. The name described a claim, not a program, and the question the era leaves is what it fixed and who was left out.

## The industrial city

**A nation of cities.** — In 1900 the census counted 76 million Americans, up from 50 million in 1880. About a fifth lived in towns in 1860, close to two-fifths by 1900. New York held 3.4 million people, Chicago 1.7 million. The foreign-born were 13.6 percent of the population in 1900 and 14.7 percent in 1910, and far more in the great cities; New York's Lower East Side packed families at densities near anything in the industrial world.

**The tenement.** — Housing could not keep up. New York's "dumbbell" tenement, legalized in 1879, stood on a 25-foot lot with an air shaft too narrow to give light or air, water in the yard, and often one privy for a building. Jacob Riis photographed the blocks and published *How the Other Half Lives* in 1890, making the slum visible to readers who would never enter it. The Tenement House Act of 1901 required light, water, and indoor toilets in new buildings, but left old tenements mostly untouched.

**The mill and the mine.** — The industrial city ran on cheap labor. The 1900 census counted about 1.75 million children between ten and fifteen "gainfully occupied," many in Southern cotton mills, coal mines, and canneries. Steelworkers commonly worked twelve hours a day, seven days a week, for roughly $400 to $500 a year, when a family needed perhaps $800. On 25 March 1911 the Triangle Shirtwaist Company in New York burned; the doors had been locked against theft, and 146 workers died, most of them young Jewish and Italian women.

**The machine and the boss.** — City government was often a business, and its business was favors. Tammany Hall distributed jobs, coal, and naturalization papers to immigrants in exchange for votes, and collected from saloons, contractors, and utility companies that wanted franchises. The corruption was a system: a council could sell a streetcar franchise for a bribe, and the men who paid expected to recover it. The reform mayor Seth Low won New York in 1901, and the machine returned within two years.

## The muckrakers

**A new journalism.** — The exposure was the work of a new mass press. S. S. McClure's *McClure's Magazine* reached hundreds of thousands of readers; its January 1903 number is often treated as the beginning, with Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, Lincoln Steffens on the cities, and Ray Stannard Baker on the right to work. In a speech on 14 April 1906, Theodore Roosevelt compared the investigative journalist to the "Man with the Muck-rake" in Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress*, too busy raking filth to look up at the crown. The reporters took the insult as a badge, and "muckraker" entered the language.

**Tarbell and Standard Oil.** — Ida Tarbell was born in 1857 in the Pennsylvania oil country, where her father's business was among those ruined by John D. Rockefeller's combination. Her *History of the Standard Oil Company*, serialized from 1902 and published as a book in 1904, was an investigation rather than an outburst: she read court records, contracts, rebate schedules, and rivals' testimony. It showed how Standard Oil used secret freight rebates, spies, and pressure on the railroads to take first a quarter and then perhaps nine-tenths of refining. Rockefeller's defenders said he had cut the price of kerosene; Tarbell answered that the order had been bought with the law itself.

**Steffens and the cities.** — Lincoln Steffens began with "Tweed Days in St. Louis" in *McClure's* in October 1902 and collected the pieces as *The Shame of the Cities* in 1904. His finding was the same everywhere: corruption was not one party's work but a partnership between respectable business and the machine, and the voters who complained usually took their share. The good citizens and the bad politicians, he wrote, were the same men at different hours.

**Sinclair and the stockyards.** — The most famous muckraking book began as a serial in the socialist *Appeal to Reason* in 1905. Upton Sinclair spent weeks among the Chicago packing-house workers, and *The Jungle*, published in February 1906, told of a Lithuanian immigrant family destroyed by the yards. Sinclair aimed at the public's heart, he wrote, and hit it in the stomach: readers were shocked less by the human wreckage than by the thought that they had eaten tubercular pork. Roosevelt sent two officials to investigate, and their report forced the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act through Congress on 30 June 1906. The packers accepted regulation as the price of restoring confidence in their product.

## Settlements, the Social Gospel, and the new woman

**Toynbee Hall and Hull House.** — The settlement house was an English invention. Toynbee Hall opened in London's East End in 1884, and its idea — educated people living in a poor neighborhood and learning from their neighbors — crossed the Atlantic. On 18 September 1889 Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved into a large old house at 800 South Halsted Street on Chicago's Near West Side, in the immigrant 19th Ward. It stood between an undertaking establishment and a saloon; a Chicago wit called the three "Knight, Death, and the Devil."

**What the settlement did.** — Hull House ran a kindergarten and nursery, clubs for children and adults, a reading room, a gymnasium, a bathhouse, and a theater, and its residents investigated the neighborhood and turned findings into law. Florence Kelley became Illinois's first chief factory inspector in 1893; Julia Lathrop later headed the federal Children's Bureau; Alice Hamilton became the country's first great investigator of occupational disease. The Labor Museum, begun in 1900, set up old-world spinning wheels and looms where immigrant women could show their children the skills they had brought from Europe.

**The idea behind it.** — In an 1892 address, "The Subjective Necessity for Social Settlements," Addams named three motives: to be of use, to give educated young people a social outlet the professions did not, and to widen the circle of sympathy across class and language. The settlement assumed the immigrant had a culture worth preserving and that the resident had as much to learn as to teach. In *Twenty Years at Hull-House* (1910), Addams could describe South Italians as representing "the pathetic stupidity of agricultural people crowded into city tenements" in the same paragraph in which she mourned what the city did to them.

**The Social Gospel.** — The poverty of the city also produced a religious response. Washington Gladden, a Congregational minister in Ohio, preached that the church could not be silent about wages and hours. In New York, Walter Rauschenbusch came to believe that sin was social as well as personal; his *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) argued that the kingdom of God was to be built on earth and that the church must take the side of the poor. The Federal Council of Churches, formed in 1908, adopted a social creed that read like a reform platform.

**The new woman.** — By 1900 more than a fifth of women over fifteen worked for pay. Lillian Wald founded the Henry Street Settlement in 1893 and turned it into a public-health nursing service, and the National Consumers League, organized in 1899 under Florence Kelley, asked shoppers to buy only goods made under decent conditions. These organizations gave women a public role before they had the vote.

## The vote and the dry crusade

**Suffrage before the amendment.** — The campaign was older than the era. It began at Seneca Falls in 1848 and won its first foothold in the Wyoming Territory in 1869, which kept the vote when it became a state in 1890. Colorado followed in 1893, Utah and Idaho in 1896, and then the movement stalled for more than a decade. Washington, California, and Oregon followed between 1910 and 1912. The National American Woman Suffrage Association, formed in 1890, worked state by state under Carrie Chapman Catt, whose "Winning Plan" after 1915 pushed both the states and a federal amendment.

**The militant wing.** — A younger generation grew impatient with the state-by-state pace. Alice Paul and Lucy Burns organized a great procession in Washington on 3 March 1913, the day before Wilson's inauguration. Thousands marched up Pennsylvania Avenue while a hostile crowd surged into the street; the police did little and more than a hundred marchers were hurt. Paul founded the National Woman's Party, which picketed the White House from January 1917 with banners calling Wilson a hypocrite for fighting for democracy abroad while denying it at home. The picketers were arrested, imprisoned at Occoquan Workhouse in Virginia, and force-fed when they went on hunger strikes; in November 1917 guards beat and dragged a group of them.

**The color line in the movement.** — The suffrage movement was largely white and increasingly willing to trade Black women's rights for Southern support. At the 1913 Washington parade, Black women were told to march at the back; the journalist Ida B. Wells-Barnett refused and stepped into the Illinois delegation. Southern suffragists argued openly that giving women the vote would not threaten white supremacy, because the poll tax and the literacy test would keep Black women from the polls just as they kept Black men. The National Association of Colored Women, founded in 1896, worked for the vote on its own terms.

**Temperance and Prohibition.** — The oldest of the era's mass movements was the temperance crusade. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1874, grew under Frances Willard, who tied temperance to woman suffrage and labor reform. The Anti-Saloon League, founded in 1893, was narrower and more effective, working for local option, then state prohibition, then a federal amendment. Its case was that the saloon was the machine's clubhouse, the ruin of working families, and a foreign institution in an American land — which made the "wet" immigrants of the cities the target as much as the drink. By 1916 twenty-three states were dry; the Eighteenth Amendment was ratified in January 1919 and took effect in January 1920 under the Volstead Act.

**The Nineteenth Amendment.** — The federal amendment passed the House in 1918 and the Senate in June 1919, and was ratified on 18 August 1920, when Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state. It forbade denying the vote on account of sex, and left the poll tax, the literacy test, and the white primary untouched; Black women in the South would wait decades more. The movement split almost at once over the Equal Rights Amendment proposed in 1923.

## The machinery of reform

**The ballot and the primary.** — The progressive reforms of government were as much about who chose the choosers as about what government did. The secret ballot, printed by the state rather than the parties, ended the party ticket a boss could read at a glance. The direct primary let voters choose party nominees instead of leaving the choice to conventions run by bosses; Wisconsin adopted one in 1903 under Robert La Follette.

**Initiative, referendum, recall.** — Three devices gave voters a direct hand. The initiative let citizens propose a law; the referendum let them approve or reject a law the legislature had passed; the recall let them remove an official before the end of his term. South Dakota adopted the initiative and referendum in 1898, Oregon in 1902 and the recall in 1908, and the Progressive platform of 1912 urged the package on the states. The recall of judges was the most contested piece, because it seemed to put the courts under the same popular pressure the reformers wanted to escape.

**The Seventeenth Amendment.** — The Senate was chosen by state legislatures and had come to look like a club of millionaires and railroad lawyers. Direct election was a Populist demand the progressives made their own. The House passed the amendment repeatedly; the Senate held out until 1912, passing it on 13 May, and the states ratified it on 8 April 1913.

**The income tax and the Sixteenth Amendment.** — Reform cost money, and the money came from a new source. An income tax enacted in 1894 had been struck down by the Supreme Court in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust* (1895), and the Populists had demanded a graduated tax ever since. The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified on 3 February 1913, gave Congress power to tax incomes without apportioning the tax among the states. The Underwood Tariff of 1913 then lowered duties and made up the loss with the new tax.

## Roosevelt and the Square Deal

**Coal and trusts.** — Theodore Roosevelt became president in September 1901, when an anarchist's bullet killed William McKinley, and called his program the Square Deal. In 1902 the anthracite coal miners struck for higher wages, shorter hours, and recognition of their union; some 150,000 men stopped work and the winter threatened to leave the cities without coal. The owners refused to arbitrate, so Roosevelt threatened to send in the army and appointed a commission that gave the miners a raise and a shorter day but not recognition of the union. In 1904 the Supreme Court dissolved a railroad holding company in *Northern Securities Co. v. United States*, the first great trust prosecution under the Sherman Act; his administration brought some forty-four antitrust suits in all. The Elkins and Hepburn Acts of 1903 and 1906 strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission.

**The Jungle and the pure food laws.** — The meat scandals of 1906 gave Roosevelt the support to pass two laws business had long resisted. The Meat Inspection Act provided for federal inspection of packing houses, and the Pure Food and Drug Act forbade the misbranding and adulteration of food and drugs and led to the creation of the Food and Drug Administration. The laws were weaker than the muckrakers wanted and stronger than the packers had once accepted.

**Conservation.** — Roosevelt made conservation a national policy. The Newlands Reclamation Act of 1902 used the sale of western land to pay for irrigation dams and canals; the Forest Service, created in 1905 under Gifford Pinchot, brought the national forests under professional management; and a governors' conference at the White House in 1908 promoted conservation. By the end of his presidency the federal government had set aside some 230 million acres of forest, range, and park. Conservation was not one idea: Pinchot wanted the forests used renewably, while the naturalist John Muir wanted wilderness preserved untouched.

**The limits of the Square Deal.** — Roosevelt's reform had a color line. In 1906 he approved the dishonorable discharge of 167 Black soldiers of the 25th Infantry after a shooting in Brownsville, Texas, without a trial. He spoke of "race suicide" and worried that the old-stock American family was being outbred. He invited Booker T. Washington to dinner at the White House in 1901, which enraged the white South, but did almost nothing to protect Black Southerners' votes or lives, and his reclamation programs often took land from Indian nations.

## Taft, Roosevelt, and Wilson

**Taft.** — William Howard Taft, Roosevelt's chosen successor, won in 1908. He signed the Payne-Aldrich Tariff of 1909 and called it the best tariff bill the Republicans had ever passed, infuriating the reformers. A quarrel over conservation between Pinchot and Taft's interior secretary, Richard Ballinger, ended with Pinchot dismissed. Yet Taft's Justice Department brought some ninety antitrust suits, and in 1911 the Supreme Court dissolved Standard Oil and the American Tobacco Company. The suit that broke the party was the one against U.S. Steel, which named the company's 1907 purchase of Tennessee Coal and Iron — a purchase Roosevelt had personally approved during the Panic of 1907.

**1912.** — Roosevelt returned with a speech at Osawatomie, Kansas, in August 1910, calling for a "New Nationalism" in which the federal government would regulate big business and protect social welfare. Taft's conservatives kept control of the Republican convention in 1912, and Roosevelt's supporters walked out and formed the Progressive Party. Its platform demanded direct primaries, the direct election of senators, the initiative, referendum, and recall, equal suffrage, an eight-hour day, and the prohibition of child labor. Campaigning in Milwaukee, Roosevelt was shot in the chest and survived. In November, Woodrow Wilson won 435 electoral votes; Roosevelt took 88 and Taft 8, and the Republican split gave Wilson the White House with only 41.8 percent of the popular vote.

**Wilson's New Freedom.** — Wilson ran on a "New Freedom" that promised to restore competition by breaking up the trusts rather than regulating them from Washington, but in office he used federal power freely. The Underwood Tariff of 1913 cut rates and imposed the income tax; the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 created a central banking system with regional reserve banks; the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 policed unfair business practices; and the Clayton Antitrust Act declared that human labor was not a commodity. The Keating-Owen Act of 1916 barred from interstate commerce goods made by children under fourteen, and the Adamson Act granted railroad workers an eight-hour day. In 1916 he appointed Louis Brandeis to the Supreme Court and won reelection on the slogan "He kept us out of war."

## The limits of reform

**The color line.** — The Progressive Era was also the nadir of American race relations. In November 1898 a white mob in Wilmington, North Carolina, burned a Black newspaper office and overthrew the city's elected government. Disfranchisement spread through Southern constitutions, and race riots killed Black residents in Atlanta in 1906 and Springfield, Illinois, in 1908. After the Springfield riot, white and Black reformers founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People in 1909; W. E. B. Du Bois became editor of its journal, *The Crisis*, in 1910. Roughly a million and a half Black Southerners moved north between 1910 and 1920, and the violence that met them in East St. Louis in 1917 showed the North was not a refuge.

**Wilson and the federal color line.** — Woodrow Wilson came to office with a reputation as a Southern progressive. His administration segregated the federal civil service, which had been largely integrated since Reconstruction; Black employees were demoted or dismissed. Wilson told a Black editor that segregation was "not a humiliation but a benefit." In 1915 he allowed the White House to screen D. W. Griffith's *The Birth of a Nation*, a film that celebrated the Ku Klux Klan; the reported remark that it was "like writing history with lightning" is attributed rather than proven, but the screening itself was a signal.

**Immigrants, restriction, and eugenics.** — Reformers turned on the immigrants as well. The Dillingham Commission delivered a forty-one-volume report in 1911 that sorted "old" immigrants of northern and western Europe from "new" arrivals of the south and east and recommended restriction. Congress passed a literacy test for immigrants over Wilson's veto in 1917, along with an "Asiatic barred zone" that closed the door to most of Asia. Behind the restriction lay ideas rather than evidence: Madison Grant's *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916) warned of race suicide, and the Eugenics Record Office, founded in 1910, gave "scientific" cover to the belief that the unfit were breeding faster than the fit. Indiana passed the first compulsory sterilization law in 1907.

**Labor and the left.** — The labor movement was divided, and the law was against it. The American Federation of Labor organized skilled workers by craft and left the unskilled, most women, and most Black workers outside. The Industrial Workers of the World, founded in 1905, tried to organize all workers and met repression: the Lawrence textile strike of 1912 won partly on the strikers' discipline, but the Ludlow Massacre of April 1914, when National Guardsmen fired on a tent colony of striking Colorado coal miners and killed twenty-one people, mostly women and children. The courts used injunctions and the "freedom of contract" doctrine to block reform: *Lochner v. New York* (1905) struck down a ten-hour law for bakers, and only in *Muller v. Oregon* (1908) did the Court uphold a maximum-hours law for women.

**The end of the era.** — The First World War, which the United States entered in April 1917, swallowed the reform movement. The Committee on Public Information sold the war; the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918 punished dissent; and after the armistice the Red Scare of 1919–20 deported radicals and broke the left. The election of 1918 returned a Republican Congress, and the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations. Prohibition and sterilization laws had already shown that reform could be coercive as well as generous.

## The question of who the progressives were

**The debate.** — Historians have never agreed on what the Progressive movement was. Richard Hofstadter, in *The Age of Reform* (1955), described the reformers as old-stock provincials whose standing was slipping. Robert H. Wiebe, in *The Search for Order, 1877–1920* (1967), answered that they were a new middle class of professionals and managers building institutions to govern a society that had outgrown its old local forms. Gabriel Kolko, in *The Triumph of Conservatism* (1963), argued that business often wanted the regulation, because federal rules could stabilize markets. Nell Irvin Painter, in *Standing at Armageddon* (1987), put working people and Black Americans at the center and described the era as a time of racial and economic war whose gains belonged mostly to the white middle class.

**What the era left.** — The progressives built the income tax, the Federal Reserve, the direct election of senators, the initiative and referendum, factory inspection, the national forests, and the vote for women. They did not end child labor, which waited for 1938; they did not break the power of the trusts, which grew; and they did not touch the caste system that ruled the South. Progressivism tried to fix the city, the market, and the boss, and it left out the people the reformers had decided were not yet ready to be citizens.

## Chronology

- **1889** — Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr open Hull House in Chicago.
- **1890** — The National American Woman Suffrage Association is formed; Wyoming enters the Union with woman suffrage; the Sherman Antitrust Act.
- **1895** — *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust* strikes down the income tax.
- **1898** — South Dakota adopts the initiative and referendum; the Wilmington coup in North Carolina.
- **1901** — McKinley is assassinated; Theodore Roosevelt becomes president.
- **1902** — Roosevelt arbitrates the anthracite coal strike; the Newlands Reclamation Act; Oregon adopts the initiative and referendum.
- **1905** — *Lochner v. New York*; the Industrial Workers of the World and the Niagara Movement are founded.
- **1906** — *The Jungle*; the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act; the Atlanta race riot.
- **1908** — *Muller v. Oregon*; the Springfield race riot; the governors' conservation conference.
- **1909** — The NAACP is founded; the Payne-Aldrich Tariff.
- **1911** — The Triangle Shirtwaist fire; Standard Oil and American Tobacco are dissolved; California grants women the vote.
- **1912** — The Progressive Party and its platform; Wilson is elected; the Seventeenth Amendment passes Congress.
- **1913** — The Sixteenth and Seventeenth Amendments are ratified; the Underwood Tariff and the Federal Reserve Act; Wilson segregates the federal service.
- **1914** — The Federal Trade Commission and the Clayton Act; the Ludlow Massacre; war begins in Europe.
- **1916** — The Keating-Owen Child Labor Act; the Adamson Act; Wilson is reelected.
- **1917** — The United States enters the First World War; the immigration literacy test; the Eighteenth Amendment passes Congress.
- **1918** — *Hammer v. Dagenhart* strikes down the child labor law; the Sedition Act.
- **1919** — The Eighteenth Amendment is ratified; the Nineteenth Amendment passes Congress; the Red Scare.
- **1920** — The Nineteenth Amendment is ratified on 18 August; Prohibition takes effect.

## Questions to carry

- What did progressivism try to fix, and who was left out?
- The muckrakers wrote for mass magazines owned by the same business world they exposed. Did the exposure change conditions, or only what could be said?
- The Progressive Party of 1912 demanded an end to child labor and the vote for women while its leaders accepted a segregated South. How did the reformers live with the contradiction?
- Roosevelt's New Nationalism and Wilson's New Freedom disagreed about whether to regulate or to break up the trusts. Did the difference matter in what the government actually did?
- The era is named for progress. What does the name leave out, and what does it conceal?
