# Cold War America and the affluent society

**Period:** c. 1945–1960 · **Region:** The United States

**The war's end and the shape of the peace.** — In the spring of 1945 the United States stood at the height of its power. Its factories had outproduced the rest of the world combined; its gross national product had nearly doubled since 1939; its dead, about 400,000, were few beside the twenty million Soviet dead. In February, at Yalta, Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Joseph Stalin had agreed that liberated Europe would hold free elections and that Germany would be divided into occupation zones. In April Roosevelt died, and Harry S. Truman inherited the bomb, the alliance, and the quarrels. In July, at Potsdam, Truman, Stalin, and Clement Attlee settled little: Germany stayed divided, Poland's western border moved west, and the atomic bomb, tested in New Mexico on July 16, was used on Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9. Japan surrendered on August 15. The war that had made the United States the strongest nation on earth also left it with a problem no one had planned for: what to do with that strength, and against whom.

**The origins of the Cold War.** — Within eighteen months the wartime alliance turned into a rivalry that would last forty-four years. The dispute began over Poland and Eastern Europe. Stalin, whose armies occupied the region, treated friendly governments as a security belt against another invasion from the west; the United States and Britain read the same acts as the imposition of totalitarian rule. In March 1946 Churchill, out of office, spoke at Fulton, Missouri, of an "iron curtain" across Europe. In February, George F. Kennan, the American diplomat in Moscow, sent the "Long Telegram," arguing that Soviet policy was driven by a deep insecurity and that the answer was "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies." Containment became the word and the policy. Historians have argued about its causes ever since. The "orthodox" school of the 1950s — Herbert Feis, Thomas A. Bailey — blamed Soviet expansion. The "revisionists" of the 1960s — William Appleman Williams in *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959), Gabriel Kolko — blamed American capitalism and the drive to keep foreign markets open. The "post-revisionists" — John Lewis Gaddis, whose first book appeared in 1972 — blamed a security dilemma in which each side, acting from fear, confirmed the other's fear. The debate is not closed.

**The first crises.** — The rivalry hardened through a series of quarrels that were small in themselves and large in what they seemed to prove. In early 1946 the Soviet Union refused to withdraw its troops from northern Iran, where it had occupied the country during the war, and only pulled back after American and United Nations pressure. In the same months Moscow pressed Turkey for control of the straits between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, and a civil war between communists and royalists resumed in Greece. In September 1946 a former British official in Moscow, Frank Roberts, and the American chargé George Kennan both reported that Soviet leaders saw the world as divided and could not be reassured by concessions. In 1947 Britain told Washington that it could no longer afford to police the eastern Mediterranean, and the United States stepped into the gap. Each side read the other's moves as proof of intent: when the West organized Western Europe, Moscow saw encirclement; when Moscow tightened its grip on the east, Washington saw the blueprint of world conquest. Neither side was working from a single plan, which is one reason the conflict was so hard to end.

**Containment becomes policy.** — On March 12, 1947, Truman asked Congress for $400 million to support Greece and Turkey, whose governments were fighting communist insurgencies and Soviet pressure. The speech drew a line: "I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." This was the Truman Doctrine, and it committed the country to a global role it had avoided since 1919. In June, Secretary of State George C. Marshall proposed at Harvard a program of economic aid for a ruined Europe; the Marshall Plan, enacted in April 1948, sent about $13.3 billion over four years. Its authors believed, as Truman put it, that the "seeds of totalitarian regimes are nurtured by misery and want." In February 1948 a communist coup in Czechoslovakia and, in June, the Soviet blockade of the land routes into West Berlin tested the policy at once. Rather than force the blockade, the West supplied the city by air. The Berlin Airlift, from June 1948 to May 1949, flew some 277,000 flights and carried 2.3 million tons of food and coal; a plane landed about every minute at the height of it. In April 1949 the United States, Canada, and ten European states signed the North Atlantic Treaty, promising that an attack on one would be an attack on all. The Warsaw Pact followed in 1955.

**The bomb and the arms race.** — The atomic monopoly did not last. In August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic device, years earlier than American planners had expected. Truman responded by ordering work on a hydrogen bomb — a fusion weapon hundreds of times more powerful than the bombs of 1945 — and in April 1950 the National Security Council produced NSC-68, drafted under Paul Nitze, which argued that the Soviet Union sought "to impose its absolute authority over the rest of the world" and recommended a large and permanent military buildup. The first American thermonuclear test, Ivy Mike, vaporized an island in the Pacific in November 1952; the Soviet Union tested its own in August 1953. The doctrine of "massive retaliation," announced by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles in 1954, promised to answer aggression with nuclear force "at places and with means of our own choosing." The tests spread radioactive fallout across the globe, and the Castle Bravo test of March 1954, far more powerful than expected, contaminated Pacific islanders and a Japanese fishing crew. American schoolchildren practiced "duck and cover"; families built fallout shelters; civil-defense films explained the blast, the fire, and the radioactive dust. The bomb that had ended one war now shaped the daily imagination of the next.

**Living with the bomb.** — The federal government tried to make the unthinkable ordinary. The Federal Civil Defense Administration, created in 1950, distributed pamphlets, trained volunteers, and produced the film *Duck and Cover* (1951), in which a cartoon turtle named Bert taught children to crouch beneath their desks. Cities tested air-raid sirens; schools ran drills; families in the new suburbs were urged to stock canned food and water in a basement shelter. The advice was as much ritual as preparation: a desk offered no protection from a hydrogen blast, and everyone involved in making the films knew it. The point was to keep fear manageable, to turn a threat that could not be met into a set of small habits. The same years saw religion woven into the official language of the conflict. In 1954 Congress added the words "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance, and in 1956 it made "In God We Trust" the national motto. The struggle against "godless communism" was presented as a contest of faiths, and church membership rose through the decade.

**The fall of China and the Korean War.** — In 1949 the Chinese civil war ended with the victory of Mao Zedong's communists; Chiang Kai-shek's government fled to Taiwan, and on October 1 Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China. The "loss of China" became a domestic wound. Republicans charged that the Truman administration had betrayed an ally, and a "China Lobby" demanded support for Chiang. Then, on June 25, 1950, North Korean troops crossed the 38th parallel into South Korea. Truman committed American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war, acting through the United Nations, where the Soviet delegate happened to be boycotting the Security Council. The first months went badly; by August the United Nations force held only the Pusan perimeter. In September Douglas MacArthur's landing at Inchon turned the war around, and by November his troops neared the Yalu River, the border with China. Chinese forces then intervened in massive numbers, driving the allies back below the 38th parallel. MacArthur, who wanted to bomb China and use Nationalist troops, was relieved of command in April 1951. The fighting settled into a stalemate, and an armistice was signed on July 27, 1953, leaving Korea divided near where it had begun. About 36,500 Americans had died; Korean deaths, military and civilian, ran into the millions. The war made containment global and militarized, and it turned the defense budget from a wartime expense into a permanent one.

**The second Red Scare and McCarthyism.** — Fear of communism at home had been building since the late 1940s. In 1947 the House Committee on Un-American Activities investigated Hollywood and cited ten screenwriters and directors — the "Hollywood Ten" — for contempt of Congress after they refused to answer questions about their politics. In 1948 Whittaker Chambers, a former communist, accused Alger Hiss, a former State Department official, of espionage; Hiss was convicted of perjury in 1950. In 1951 Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union and, after appeals, executed in 1953. The Internal Security (McCarran) Act of 1950 required communist organizations to register; Truman's loyalty program and a web of state and local oaths put millions of government and school employees under scrutiny. The Lavender Scare drove gay and lesbian workers from federal service on the theory that they were vulnerable to blackmail. The loudest figure was Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin, who claimed in a speech at Wheeling, West Virginia, in February 1950 to hold a list of communists in the State Department. For four years he attacked officials, diplomats, and the army itself, often without evidence. His power collapsed in the televised Army–McCarthy hearings of 1954, and the Senate censured him that December. "McCarthyism" outlived him: it named a style of accusation that treated the charge as proof and the denial as further evidence. The courts supplied their own machinery of repression. Under the Smith Act of 1940, the government prosecuted the leaders of the Communist Party, and in *Dennis v. United States* (1951) the Supreme Court upheld their convictions, holding that the government could act against a conspiracy to advocate overthrow even without proof of an immediate attempt. Convictions, blacklists, and the fear of being named cost thousands of people their jobs in broadcasting, teaching, and the civil service. A few resisted: the journalist I. F. Stone, the broadcaster Edward R. Murrow, whose 1954 program on McCarthy helped turn opinion, and the senators who finally voted to censure him. Historians still disagree about how much of the scare was driven by McCarthy himself and how much by a broader, bipartisan panic that would have continued without him.

**The GI Bill and the postwar boom.** — The war had ended the Depression, and the peace did not bring it back. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 — the GI Bill — offered returning veterans money for college or training, low-interest loans for homes and farms, and a year of unemployment pay. Nearly eight million veterans used its education and training benefits; by 1947 veterans made up about half of all college students. The act helped avert a postwar jobs crisis and, in the long run, built a credentialed middle class. The economy grew at a pace the country had never known: gross national product rose from about $228 billion in 1945 to about $543 billion in 1960, and unemployment stayed low through most of the decade. Pent-up wartime savings and consumer demand fed a boom in cars, appliances, and houses. But the boom had a price and a politics. A wave of strikes in 1946 was met by the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, which restricted union tactics over Truman's veto. Inflation spiked when price controls ended. And not everyone shared the gains: Black workers, who had migrated north for war jobs, were the first laid off and the last hired; women who had entered the factories were pushed back toward the home; poverty persisted in the Appalachians, the rural South, and the barrios of the Southwest.

**The consumer republic.** — The boom was also a change in how Americans lived. In 1948 fewer than one home in ten had a television; by 1960 nearly nine in ten did, and the three networks reached a national audience at the same hour every evening. Advertising moved from print to the screen, and the new medium sold not only soap and cars but a way of life: the suburban house, the appliance-filled kitchen, the two-car garage. Credit became ordinary. The Diners Club card, introduced in 1950, was followed by bank cards and installment plans that let families buy now and pay later, a sharp break from the Depression habit of going without. The shopping center replaced the downtown street as the place to spend, and the supermarket replaced the corner grocer. The result was a material abundance that had no precedent in American life and a set of expectations — that each generation would live better than the last, that consumption measured the good life — that would prove as durable as the Cold War itself.

**The baby boom, suburbia, and the car.** — Between 1946 and 1964 Americans had about 76 million children, a surge so large it took its own name, the baby boom. Marriage came earlier and families were larger than they had been for a century. The new families needed houses, and builders supplied them on a new scale. In 1947 William Levitt began Levittown on Long Island, assembling houses on a concrete slab from prefabricated parts; the first development held more than 17,000 homes. Federal Housing Administration and Veterans Administration loans made the mortgages cheap, and federal highway money and cheap gasoline made the car the key to the suburb. The Interstate Highway Act of 1956, signed by Eisenhower, promised 41,000 miles of limited-access road with the federal government paying 90 percent. Between 1945 and 1960 the number of registered cars rose from about 26 million to about 74 million. Shopping centers, drive-in theaters, and motels followed the roads. Suburbia was not open to all. Federal lending rules and local covenants steered Black families away from new subdivisions, a practice called redlining; the Supreme Court had struck down court enforcement of racial covenants in *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948), but private and public discrimination continued. The result was a landscape that was both newly prosperous and rigidly sorted by race and class.

**The military-industrial complex.** — The permanent defense budget reshaped the economy. Defense spending, about $13 billion in 1948, reached roughly $50 billion by 1953 and stayed near $45–50 billion through the 1950s. The money built aircraft plants in California and Washington, electronics firms along Boston's Route 128, and research universities dependent on federal contracts. The Soviet launch of Sputnik in October 1957 shocked the country into a new competition; the National Aeronautics and Space Administration opened in 1958, the National Defense Education Act poured money into science and language teaching, and the "missile gap" became an issue in the 1960 election. In his farewell address of January 17, 1961, Eisenhower — a former general — warned of "the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex" and of a "scientific-technological elite." The warning came from the man who had presided over the buildup. It named, without resolving, the question of how a republic keeps a permanent war machine without being ruled by it.

**Truman and the race question.** — The federal government moved first on a narrow front. In 1947 Truman's Committee on Civil Rights published *To Secure These Rights*, which documented lynching, police violence, and the exclusion of Black Americans from voting, housing, and schools. In July 1948 Truman issued Executive Order 9981, ordering the desegregation of the armed forces, and the same year the Democratic Party adopted a civil rights plank that drove Southern delegates to walk out and run Strom Thurmond for president as a "Dixiecrat." Truman won the election anyway, in one of the great upsets of American politics, carrying Black voters in the northern cities by large margins. Desegregation of the military proceeded slowly through the Korean War, but by the mid-1950s Black and white soldiers served in the same units, and the experience fed a generation of veterans into the movement. The federal government had committed itself, on paper, to a principle it would spend the next two decades being forced to enforce.

**The beginnings of the civil rights movement.** — The war against Nazism had made racial hierarchy harder to defend, and the Cold War made it a public-relations liability. On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, unanimously held that racial segregation in public schools violated the equal protection clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote, overruling the "separate but equal" doctrine of *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896). A year later, in *Brown II*, the Court left the remedy to the lower courts and asked for desegregation "with all deliberate speed," a phrase that proved elastic enough for a decade of delay. In August 1955 Emmett Till, a fourteen-year-old from Chicago, was murdered in Mississippi; an all-white jury acquitted his killers, who later admitted the crime. In December 1955 Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery, Alabama, bus, and the Black community organized a boycott that lasted 381 days. A young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., led the Montgomery Improvement Association; the Supreme Court's *Browder v. Gayle* decision ended bus segregation in 1956. In September 1957 Eisenhower sent federal troops to Little Rock, Arkansas, to enforce a court order admitting nine Black students to Central High School, and Congress passed the first civil rights law since Reconstruction. In February 1960 Black students began sit-ins at a Greensboro, North Carolina, lunch counter. The movement was still young, and its greatest victories lay ahead, but the decade that prized conformity had produced a challenge to its deepest exclusion.

**The Eisenhower years.** — Dwight D. Eisenhower, the Allied commander in Europe, won the presidency in 1952 and again in 1956, defeating Adlai Stevenson both times. He called his approach "Modern Republicanism": he accepted the New Deal's basic programs, tried to hold down spending, and balanced the budget while ending the Korean War. He preferred what he called the "hidden hand" of delegation to open political combat, and his genial public manner concealed a careful manager. His strategy, worked out in the "Project Solarium" review of 1953, was the "New Look": rather than match Soviet manpower in Europe, the United States would rely on nuclear weapons and air power, which were cheaper, and would use the Central Intelligence Agency to act covertly where open force was too costly. Abroad he practiced containment with an eye on cost, avoiding direct war while authorizing covert action in Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954). He faced crises in the Suez in 1956, when Britain, France, and Israel invaded Egypt and the United States forced a withdrawal; in Hungary the same year, when a Soviet invasion crushed a revolt and the West did nothing; and in 1960, when an American U-2 spy plane was shot down over the Soviet Union and a summit collapsed. He left office warning against the very military establishment he had built. The affluence and the fear had grown together, and neither had ended.

- **February 1946** — Kennan's "Long Telegram" argues for containment.
- **March 1946** — Churchill speaks of an "iron curtain" at Fulton, Missouri.
- **March 1947** — Truman Doctrine: Truman asks Congress for aid to Greece and Turkey.
- **June 1947** — Marshall Plan proposed; Taft-Hartley Act passes over Truman's veto.
- **April 1948** — Marshall Plan enacted; Soviet blockade of Berlin begins in June.
- **June 1948–May 1949** — Berlin Airlift supplies West Berlin by air.
- **April 1949** — NATO founded.
- **August 1949** — Soviet Union tests its first atomic bomb.
- **October 1949** — Mao Zedong proclaims the People's Republic of China.
- **April 1950** — NSC-68 calls for a permanent military buildup.
- **February 1950** — McCarthy claims a list of communists in the State Department.
- **June 1950** — North Korea invades South Korea; Truman commits American forces.
- **September 1950** — MacArthur's landing at Inchon.
- **November 1950** — Chinese forces intervene in Korea.
- **April 1951** — Truman relieves MacArthur of command.
- **November 1952** — First American hydrogen bomb test (Ivy Mike).
- **July 1953** — Korean War armistice; Rosenbergs executed in June.
- **May 1954** — Brown v. Board of Education; Army–McCarthy hearings begin.
- **December 1954** — Senate censures McCarthy.
- **May 1955** — Brown II orders desegregation "with all deliberate speed."
- **December 1955** — Montgomery bus boycott begins after Rosa Parks's arrest.
- **June 1956** — Interstate Highway Act signed.

- **January 1961** — Eisenhower's farewell address warns of the military-industrial complex.

## Questions to carry

- How did the fear of communism shape what Americans were willing to do at home and abroad?
- The postwar boom lifted millions and left millions out. Who benefited, and who did not?
- Containment began as a policy in Europe. How did it spread to Asia, and what did that cost?
- Why did the Supreme Court's decision in Brown become a mass movement, and why did it meet such resistance?
- Historians still argue over who caused the Cold War. What evidence would settle the question, and what does the disagreement itself show?
