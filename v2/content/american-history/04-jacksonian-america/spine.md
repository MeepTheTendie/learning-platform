# Jacksonian America and westward expansion

**Period:** c. 1820–1848 · **Region:** The United States

## The republic of the "common man"

**A widening electorate.** — In 1824 about 350,000 white men voted for president. In 1828, the year Andrew Jackson won, roughly 1.1 million did; in 1840, some 2.4 million. The rules had changed: state after state wrote constitutions that dropped the property qualification for white male voters, and the new western states entered the Union with white male suffrage from the start. The political nation widened faster than at any time since independence. Jackson's opponents called the result the rule of "King Mob"; his supporters called it democracy. The question that runs through the period is who was allowed to stand inside it. Women, free Black people, Native nations, and the enslaved were shut out by law and custom even as the language of the "common man" became the ordinary language of American politics.

**"The farmers, mechanics, and laborers."** — The phrase is Jackson's own. In his veto of the bill to recharter the Second Bank of the United States, sent to the Senate on 10 July 1832, he wrote that when law adds "artificial distinctions" and makes "the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers—who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government." It is a definition of the people that is at once broad and narrow. Broad, because it reaches the ordinary working man and sets him against a "privileged order." Narrow, because the man it pictures is white, male, and a producer. The era took its name from this claim, and its limits were built into the claim.

## The market revolution

**Roads, canals, steamboats, rails.** — Between 1815 and 1840 the country was bound together by a transport system it had not possessed before. The Erie Canal, begun in 1817 and opened on 26 October 1825, ran 363 miles from the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo and cut the cost of moving a ton of freight from Buffalo to New York City from roughly $100 to roughly $10. The National Road pushed west from Cumberland, Maryland. Robert Fulton's steamboat had shown in 1807 that a vessel could work upstream against the current; by the 1830s hundreds of steamboats plied the Ohio, the Mississippi, and the Missouri. The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, chartered in 1827, was only the first; by 1840 there were more than 3,000 miles of track. Each improvement drew farmers farther from the coast into a national market, where they sold for cash rather than local barter and bought what they no longer made themselves.

**The cash nexus and the mill.** — The market reached into the household: farmers who had once spun and wove at home now bought cloth and sold grain, pork, and cotton for money. In New England textile towns, the Waltham-Lowell system gathered young women into company boardinghouses and paid them wages for tending power looms; by the 1830s Lowell, Massachusetts, held some 8,000 such workers. When the companies cut wages in 1834 and 1836, the women struck. In the countryside, Cyrus McCormick's reaper (1831) and John Deere's steel plow (1837) let fewer hands work more acres, and in *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) the Massachusetts court held that workers could combine to raise wages without being a criminal conspiracy. The new wealth was real, and so was its instability: the Panic of 1819 and the deeper Panic of 1837 threw banks, merchants, and wage-earners into bankruptcy.

**The paradox of the era.** — Charles Sellers, in *The Market Revolution* (1991), described a wrenching shift from a subsistence countryside to a commercial order, resisted by farmers and workers; Daniel Walker Howe, in *What Hath God Wrought* (2007), treats the same changes as broadly liberating and credits the Whigs with pushing them forward.

## Cotton and the spread of slavery

**The gin.** — In 1793 Eli Whitney built a machine for separating the sticky green seed of short-staple cotton from the fibre. Before the cotton gin, that cotton was slow to clean and barely worth growing on a large scale; after it, the crop could be prepared for market fast enough to pay. What it did was remake the South: cotton moved west and south into the fresh soils of Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, and eventually Texas, carried by planters and by the enslaved people they forced to move with them.

**The cotton kingdom.** — The United States produced about 3,000 bales of cotton in 1790, some 335,000 by 1820, about 1.35 million by 1840, and roughly 4.5 million by 1860. By 1840 cotton accounted for more than half the value of all American exports, and the phrase "King Cotton" expressed a genuine dependence: northern shipping, insurance, and banking, and the British textile industry itself, rested on the crop. The enslaved population grew from about 700,000 in 1790 to some two million by 1830 and four million by 1860, and the proportion held in the Deep South rose sharply. Slavery was not dying out, as some founders had expected; the gin and the western lands gave it a new and profitable life.

**The second middle passage.** — The internal slave trade was the engine that moved the system. Between 1790 and 1860 perhaps one million enslaved people were sold or carried from the Upper South — Virginia, Maryland, Kentucky — to the cotton country of the Lower South. The trade broke families apart by design, since the sale of individuals was where the money was. Edward Baptist, in *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014), argues that the cotton economy grew not only by expanding acreage but by intensifying the labor of enslaved people through violence, so that output per hand rose. His account is contested in its details, but it restores to the middle of the story the people whose forced labor made the crop.

**Resistance.** — The enslaved resisted constantly: by breaking tools, feigning illness, running away, and rising. The largest rebellion of the period came in Southampton County, Virginia, in August 1831, when Nat Turner and a group of followers killed some fifty-five to sixty white people over two days before the militia crushed them. Turner was hanged, and perhaps two hundred Black people, most of them uninvolved, were killed in reprisal. In the Virginia legislature of 1831–32, delegates debated gradual emancipation for the last time and then voted it down. The rebellion did not end slavery; it ended the South's willingness to discuss ending it.

## The Missouri Compromise and the politics of slavery

**The crisis of 1819–20.** — When Missouri applied for statehood in 1819, Representative James Tallmadge of New York proposed to bar any further introduction of slaves there and to free the children born to enslaved parents at twenty-five. The House passed the amendment; the Senate refused. The argument that followed was the first great sectional crisis of the republic. In 1820 the Missouri Compromise admitted Maine as a free state and Missouri as a slave state, preserving the balance in the Senate, and drew a line at 36°30′ north of which slavery would be forbidden in the territory of the Louisiana Purchase. Missouri's own constitution then barred free Black people from the state, and a second compromise in 1821 let it in. Thomas Jefferson, then seventy-seven, called the crisis "a fire bell in the night." The line held for a generation, and then broke.

**Slavery as the national question.** — The compromise did not quiet the issue; it organized it. Abolitionists sent petitions to Congress, and from 1836 to 1844 the House adopted a "gag rule" that tabled them without debate, until John Quincy Adams, returned to the House after his presidency, fought it off. The annexation of Texas in 1845 and the Mexican cession in 1848 reopened every question the line of 1820 had been meant to close. By the end of the period the argument was no longer whether slavery would expand, but whether the federal government had any power over it at all.

## Jackson and the "common man"

**1824 and the "corrupt bargain."** — The election of 1824 was a four-way race: Jackson won the most popular and electoral votes but not a majority, and the choice went to the House of Representatives. Speaker Henry Clay, who had finished fourth, threw his support to John Quincy Adams, who won on the first ballot; Adams then named Clay secretary of state. Jackson's supporters called it a "corrupt bargain," and the charge stuck. In 1828 Jackson defeated Adams in a campaign of unprecedented popular mobilization, marked by mass rallies, partisan newspapers, and personal abuse on both sides. Jackson took 56 percent of the popular vote and 178 electoral votes to Adams's 83.

**Spoils and the party.** — Jackson replaced many federal officeholders with his own supporters, and his defenders gave the practice a name: "to the victor belong the spoils," said Senator William Marcy of New York in 1832. The practice helped build the first mass political parties, with newspapers, local committees, and patronage binding voters to organizations.

## Nullification

**The tariff and the doctrine.** — The tariff of 1828, which southerners called the "Tariff of Abominations," raised duties on imported manufactures and, in southern eyes, taxed the agricultural South for the benefit of the industrial North. John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, then vice president, wrote anonymously in the *South Carolina Exposition and Protest* (1828) that a state could declare a federal law unconstitutional and nullify it within its borders. The theory defended the minority section, and behind it slavery, since a federal power over the tariff might one day reach slavery in the territories. When Congress passed a new tariff in 1832 that kept the protective principle, South Carolina called a state convention, declared both tariffs null and void, and threatened secession if the federal government tried to collect them.

**Jackson's answer.** — Jackson's *Proclamation to the People of South Carolina* (December 1832) rejected nullification outright: the Union, he wrote, was perpetual, and no state could set aside a federal law while remaining in it. He asked Congress for a Force Bill authorizing him to use the army and navy to collect the duties, and at the same time threw his weight behind a compromise tariff introduced by Clay that lowered duties gradually over ten years. South Carolina, its bluff called, repealed its ordinance and nullified the Force Bill instead. The crisis passed; the constitutional question did not. Jackson's own Vice President, Calhoun, resigned and went home to the Senate to argue for the South, and the doctrine of state resistance became a standing feature of southern politics.

## Indian removal

**The Removal Act.** — In May 1830 Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act, which authorized the president to exchange lands west of the Mississippi for the homelands of the southeastern nations in return for their "voluntary" emigration. It was neither voluntary nor confined to the Southeast. In the Old Northwest, the Black Hawk War of 1832 ended Sauk resistance in Illinois and Wisconsin. In Florida, the Second Seminole War dragged on from 1835 to 1842, and the Seminole leader Osceola was seized under a flag of truce and died in prison. The Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek, and Seminole were all pushed west along routes of disease and hunger.

**The Cherokee and the Trail of Tears.** — The Cherokee had adopted a written constitution, a legislature, and, in 1827, a newspaper, the *Cherokee Phoenix*; they held the land by treaty. When Georgia extended its laws over Cherokee territory, the Cherokee took the case to the Supreme Court. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the Court held that the Cherokee were not a foreign state and so could not sue; but in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) it ruled that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee country, because the relationship with Native nations belonged to the federal government alone. Jackson is said to have remarked, "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" — the words are reported rather than recorded, but the federal government did not enforce it. In 1835 a minority faction signed the Treaty of New Echota, ceding the Cherokee homeland without the consent of the principal chief, John Ross. In 1838–39 the United States Army gathered some 16,000 Cherokee and removed them west; on the march known as the Trail of Tears, about 4,000 died. The phrase "who counted as a citizen" is sharpest here: Native nations were treated as neither foreign states nor citizens, but as obstacles to be moved.

## The Bank War

**The veto.** — The Second Bank of the United States was a federal-chartered institution, partly government-owned, that held the government's deposits and regulated the currency; its president, Nicholas Biddle, ran it from Philadelphia. Clay and Webster pushed a recharter bill through Congress in 1832, expecting Jackson to sign it or lose the election; Jackson vetoed it on 10 July 1832 and made the veto a direct appeal to the people. The Bank, he argued, was an "exclusive privilege," a monopoly that made "the rich richer and the potent more powerful," and foreign stockholders held more than eight million dollars of its stock.

**Removal of the deposits.** — Reelected in 1832, Jackson resolved to break the Bank before its charter expired. In 1833 he ordered the removal of federal deposits, and when Treasury Secretary William Duane refused, Jackson replaced him with Roger Taney, who did it. The Senate censured Jackson in 1834, and the censure was expunged in 1837. In 1836 Jackson issued the Specie Circular, requiring that federal land be paid for in gold or silver. The economy then broke: the Panic of 1837 brought bank failures, falling prices, and years of hard times, and Jackson's successor, Martin Van Buren, took the blame. Whether Jackson killed the Bank or merely caught the collapse is still argued. Bray Hammond's *Banks and Politics in America* (1957) reads Jackson as the tool of state bankers and speculators who wanted the Bank out of the way; Schlesinger's *The Age of Jackson* (1945) reads him as the champion of the producing classes against the moneyed interest. The two books use the same documents to tell opposite stories.

## The second party system

**Democrats and Whigs.** — The Bank War hardened a party system. Jackson's followers became the Democratic Party, committed to states' rights, limited federal power, westward expansion, and the white common man. Their opponents took the name Whigs, after the English party that had opposed royal power, and gathered around Clay's "American System": a protective tariff, a national bank, and federal money for roads and canals. The Whigs drew strength from commerce, manufacturing, and the market towns, and from evangelical reformers; the Democrats drew on the southern plantation and the northern immigrant and laboring wards. Both were national parties with support in every section, which is exactly why they held the slavery question at arm's length.

**The log cabin campaign.** — In 1836 Van Buren, Jackson's chosen heir, won the presidency. In 1840 the Whigs nominated William Henry Harrison and ran him as a simple man of the people; the "log cabin and hard cider" campaign, with its slogan "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too," was the first presidential contest fought with mass imagery and spectacle, and turnout rose to about 80 percent of eligible voters. Harrison died of illness a month after his inauguration in 1841, and Vice President John Tyler, a Virginian who did not share the Whigs' nationalist economics, blocked much of their program. In 1844 the Democrat James K. Polk, an avowed expansionist, narrowly defeated Clay and set the country on the road to Texas and Oregon.

## Reform

**The Second Great Awakening.** — A wave of revivals beginning around 1800, and strongest in the 1820s and 1830s, remade American religion. The "new measures" of the Presbyterian evangelist Charles Grandison Finney in Rochester, New York, in 1830–31 preached that conversion was a choice a person could make and that a Christian was obliged to improve the world. The theology fed reform: if sin was a matter of will, then so was its remedy, and drunkards, slaveholders, and unjust laws could all be changed.

**Temperance.** — The American Temperance Society, founded in 1826 with Lyman Beecher among its leaders, campaigned against distilled liquor and claimed more than a million members within a decade. The Washingtonian movement of 1840 gave the cause a working-class face, and the "Maine law" of 1851 made that state the first to prohibit the sale of alcohol. Temperance was the largest reform movement of the age, and it was often led by women who found in it a public role they were otherwise denied.

**Abolitionism.** — On 1 January 1831 William Lloyd Garrison published the first number of *The Liberator* in Boston and demanded immediate, uncompensated emancipation, rejecting the gradual schemes of earlier antislavery men. In 1832 the New England Anti-Slavery Society formed, and in 1833 the American Anti-Slavery Society, with Theodore Weld as a leading organizer. Free Black activists had been at the work longer: David Walker's *Appeal* (1829) had called on enslaved people to resist, and Maria W. Stewart had spoken publicly in Boston in the early 1830s. The Grimké sisters, Sarah and Angelina, daughters of a South Carolina slaveholding family, lectured for the cause in the North, and in 1837 a mob in Alton, Illinois, killed the abolitionist printer Elijah Lovejoy. In 1845 Frederick Douglass published his *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* and in 1847 began his newspaper the *North Star*; his account of the fight with the overseer Edward Covey in August 1833 became the most famous single scene of the antislavery argument.

**Women's rights and Seneca Falls.** — Abolition gave women both an argument and a grievance: when Lucretia Mott and other American women delegates were seated but not allowed to speak at the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in 1840, Elizabeth Cady Stanton took note. On 19–20 July 1848, in the Wesleyan Chapel at Seneca Falls, New York, Stanton, Mott, Martha Wright, Jane Hunt, and Mary Ann McClintock convened the first women's rights convention. Its *Declaration of Sentiments*, modeled sentence by sentence on the Declaration of Independence, declared "that all men and women are created equal" and listed the injuries of law and custom: no vote, no property in marriage, no access to colleges or the professions. The demand for the elective franchise was the most contested item, and Frederick Douglass spoke in its favor. The convention did not win the vote — that took until 1920 — but it converted a private complaint into a public claim of citizenship.

**Other experiments.** — Reform reached the schools and the asylum: Horace Mann, from 1837 secretary of the Massachusetts board of education, pressed for common schools, and Dorothea Dix documented the treatment of the mentally ill.

## Manifest Destiny

**The phrase.** — In the *United States Magazine and Democratic Review* for July–August 1845, the editor John L. O'Sullivan wrote that it was the nation's "manifest destiny to overspread and to possess the whole of the continent which Providence has given us for the development of the great experiment of liberty." The phrase named a mood already widespread: that the United States was fated to expand to the Pacific, and that its expansion would carry freedom. The freedom, in practice, was for white settlers; expansion rested on the dispossession of Native nations, the seizure of Mexican land, and the extension of slavery into new territory.

**Texas and Oregon.** — American settlers had moved into Mexican Texas in the 1820s, and in 1836 they declared independence and defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto; the Republic of Texas asked for annexation, which northern antislavery opinion resisted until March 1845, when Congress and President Tyler admitted it. In the Northwest, the United States and Britain had jointly occupied Oregon since 1818; expansionists demanded "54°40′ or fight," and in June 1846 the two powers agreed to extend the boundary along the 49th parallel.

## The Mexican-American War and the Gold Rush

**The war.** — Mexico had never recognized the loss of Texas and placed its border at the Nueces River; the United States claimed the Rio Grande, farther south. In April 1846, after Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to the disputed strip, Mexican cavalry attacked an American patrol, and Polk told Congress that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil." Congress declared war on 13 May 1846, and General Winfield Scott took Mexico City in September 1847. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed 2 February 1848, gave the United States some 525,000 square miles — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming — in return for $15 million. In August 1846, while the war was still being fought, Congressman David Wilmot of Pennsylvania proposed to bar slavery from any territory taken from Mexico; the Wilmot Proviso failed, but it turned the war into a fight over the extension of slavery, and in 1848 the Free Soil Party carried it into national politics.

**The Gold Rush.** — On 24 January 1848, at Sutter's Mill on the American River, James Marshall found gold. The news drew some 80,000 "forty-niners" in 1849 alone, from the eastern states, Mexico, Chile, Europe, Australia, and China. California's Native peoples were devastated by disease, violence, and the 1850 "Act for the Government and Protection of Indians," which legalized a form of indenture; their numbers fell from perhaps 150,000 before the rush to around 30,000 by 1870. Foreign miners, especially Latin American and Chinese, were taxed and driven from the richest claims. California entered the Union as a free state in September 1850, as part of the Compromise of 1850, and the question of who belonged in the new West — white man, immigrant, Mexican, Chinese, Native, or enslaved — was no longer avoidable.

## Chronology

- **1793** — Eli Whitney builds the cotton gin; the cotton boom begins.
- **1819–20** — The Missouri crisis; the Tallmadge amendment and the compromise of 1820 draw the 36°30′ line.
- **1824** — Four-way presidential race; the House chooses John Quincy Adams, and Jackson's men cry "corrupt bargain."
- **26 October 1825** — The Erie Canal opens, tying the Great Lakes to the Hudson.
- **1826** — The American Temperance Society is founded.
- **1828** — Jackson defeats Adams; South Carolina protests the "Tariff of Abominations."
- **1829** — David Walker publishes his *Appeal*.
- **1830** — The Indian Removal Act; the first national Black convention meets in Philadelphia.
- **1 January 1831** — Garrison begins *The Liberator*.
- **1831** — Nat Turner's rebellion in Virginia; *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*.
- **1832** — *Worcester v. Georgia*; Jackson vetoes the Bank bill; the nullification crisis; the Black Hawk War.
- **1833** — Jackson removes the federal deposits; the American Anti-Slavery Society is founded; the Compromise Tariff ends the nullification crisis.
- **1836** — The House adopts the gag rule; the Second Seminole War begins; Van Buren is elected.
- **1837** — The Panic of 1837; Elijah Lovejoy is killed in Alton, Illinois; Horace Mann leads Massachusetts education reform.
- **1838–39** — The Cherokee are removed west on the Trail of Tears.
- **1840** — The "log cabin" campaign elects William Henry Harrison; the Washingtonian temperance movement begins.
- **1841** — Harrison dies a month after taking office; John Tyler becomes president.
- **1842** — *Commonwealth v. Hunt* upholds the legality of labor combinations; the Second Seminole War ends.
- **1844** — Samuel Morse sends "What hath God wrought"; James K. Polk is elected on an expansionist platform.
- **1845** — Texas is annexed; O'Sullivan writes of "manifest destiny"; Douglass publishes his *Narrative*.
- **1846** — The Oregon Treaty sets the 49th parallel; the Mexican-American War begins; the Wilmot Proviso is introduced.
- **24 January 1848** — Gold is found at Sutter's Mill.
- **2 February 1848** — The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo cedes the Mexican Cession.
- **19–20 July 1848** — The Seneca Falls convention issues the *Declaration of Sentiments*.
- **1849** — The forty-niners rush to California; the Free Soil Party contests the election.

## Questions to carry

- Jackson spoke for "the farmers, mechanics, and laborers." Who did that phrase include, and who did it leave out?
- The cotton gin made slavery more profitable at the very moment northern states were abolishing it. What does that timing tell us about the politics of the 1820s and 1830s?
- The Supreme Court ruled for the Cherokee in 1832 and the Cherokee were still removed. What does that reveal about where power actually lay?
- The *Declaration of Sentiments* borrowed the language of the Declaration of Independence. What does it gain, and what does it reveal, by making that borrowing?
- Manifest Destiny promised freedom and delivered dispossession. How did Americans of the time reconcile the two, and how should a historian weigh their words against their acts?
