# The Civil War

**Period:** 1861–1865 · **Region:** The United States

## The union dissolves

**The election of 1860.** — On 6 November 1860, Abraham Lincoln won the presidency with 1,866,452 votes, about 39.8 per cent of the popular vote, and 180 of 303 electoral votes. He carried every free state except part of New Jersey and won not one electoral vote in the fifteen slave states, where his name did not appear on the ballot in ten of them. The Republican platform pledged to bar slavery from the federal territories but not to touch it where it already existed. That was enough. In the four months between his election and his inauguration, the lower South concluded that a party pledged against the extension of slavery was a party pledged to its eventual extinction, and that remaining in the Union meant waiting to be abolished. South Carolina seceded on 20 December 1860; Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed by 1 February 1861.

**The Confederate States of America.** — Delegates from the seceded states met at Montgomery, Alabama, on 4 February 1861, adopted a constitution, and chose Jefferson Davis of Mississippi as president. The Confederate constitution copied the United States Constitution closely, but its differences reveal the purpose of the project. It protected slavery directly: no law could impair "the right of property in negro slaves," and the institution was guaranteed in any territory the Confederacy might acquire. Its preamble invoked "the favor and guidance of Almighty God," which the United States Constitution had omitted. On 21 March 1861, at Savannah, the Confederate vice-president, Alexander H. Stephens, said what many colleagues preferred to leave unsaid: the new government rested "upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery — subordination to the superior race — is his natural and normal condition." The war that followed began as a war to restore the Union and ended as a war to abolish slavery; how that change came about is the question this account follows.

**Secession as a constitutional quarrel.** — Many Confederates, then and later, described the war as a dispute over states' rights rather than a defense of slavery. The secession ordinances and Stephens's speech are the evidence against that reading, but the debate is not closed: historians still weigh how far the ordinary Southern soldier fought for slavery and how far for his home, his neighbors, and his sense of honor. James M. McPherson's reading of soldiers' letters and Gary W. Gallagher's study of Confederate morale give different answers. What is not seriously disputed is that the seceding states named slavery, in their own official documents, as the interest they were protecting.

**Fort Sumter.** — In Charleston harbor the federal garrison of Fort Sumter, under Major Robert Anderson, refused to surrender. On 12 April 1861 Confederate batteries opened fire; after thirty-four hours Anderson surrendered, and the next day Lincoln called on the states for 75,000 militia to suppress the rebellion. That call turned a secession of seven states into a war of eleven. Virginia seceded on 17 April, followed by Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina; the western counties of Virginia broke away and entered the Union as West Virginia in 1863. Four slave states — Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri — stayed in the Union, all of them divided, and Lincoln held them by a mixture of patronage, arrest, and military occupation.

**The failure of compromise.** — The war was not inevitable on election day. The Crittenden Compromise, which would have extended the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific and protected slavery south of it, failed in the Senate in January 1861; a peace conference at Washington in February produced a proposal Congress rejected. Lincoln held that secession was illegal and would not bargain over the extension of slavery. What ended negotiation was not that compromise was impossible in principle but that the two sides meant different things by the Union: for Lincoln a permanent nation, for the seceders a voluntary compact they could leave.

## Two nations at war

**The resources.** — The North held about 22 million people; the South about 9 million, of whom some 3.5 million were enslaved and, in the eyes of the Confederate state, property rather than citizens. The North produced more than 90 per cent of the nation's manufactured goods, the great majority of its firearms, and most of its railroad locomotives and rolling stock; the South had almost no works for cannon, rifles, or engines at the start and depended on imports and captured equipment. The South's advantages were different. It fought on the defensive in a territory as large as western Europe, so its armies could move on interior lines and choose when and where to give battle. It hoped that "King Cotton" would bring Britain or France into the war, since their textile mills depended on Southern fiber. And it had a large class of men accustomed to horses, firearms, and outdoor work. Early histories treated Northern industry as decisive; the war nevertheless lasted four years, and the Confederacy's collapse owed as much to political and economic exhaustion as to factory output.

**The armies.** — Both sides began with tiny regular forces — about 16,000 men in the United States Army — and filled them with volunteers. Roughly 2.75 million men served in the Union army and navy; estimates for Confederate service run from 750,000 to a million. The typical soldier was a young man in his twenties, a farmer or a clerk, who enlisted at first for ninety days and then for three years. Both governments soon turned to conscription: the Confederacy in April 1862, the Union in March 1863. Disease killed far more men than combat in the first two years, as measles, typhoid, dysentery, and pneumonia swept through camps of recruits from isolated farms who had never been exposed to them.

**The strategy.** — Winfield Scott, the aging general-in-chief, proposed the "Anaconda Plan": blockade the Southern coast, seize the Mississippi, and squeeze the Confederacy until it could not fight. Lincoln and his generals wanted a quicker result and pushed for offensives against Richmond, which became the Confederate capital in May 1861. The blockade was proclaimed on 19 April 1861 and grew steadily tighter, though it was never absolute; fast blockade runners brought rifles, medicine, and luxuries from Nassau and Bermuda. Cotton diplomacy failed. Britain found other fiber in Egypt and India, and after 1862 antislavery opinion made formal alliance with the Confederacy politically impossible.

## The first year

**Bull Run.** — The first great battle was fought on 21 July 1861 at Manassas Junction, Virginia, along a stream called Bull Run, thirty miles from Washington. Brigadier General Irvin McDowell led about 35,000 Union troops against some 22,000 Confederates under Pierre G. T. Beauregard and Joseph E. Johnston. The Union attack went well at first; then Confederate reinforcements under Thomas J. Jackson held Henry Hill — where, according to a much-repeated story, a fellow officer called out, "There stands Jackson like a stone wall" — and the Union line broke. The retreat became a rout. The battle killed the illusion, on both sides, of a short war.

**The war in the West.** — Union strategy found its most effective commander in the West. Ulysses S. Grant, an obscure former captain working in his father's leather store in Galena, Illinois, captured Fort Henry on the Tennessee River and Fort Donelson on the Cumberland in February 1862; at Donelson he demanded unconditional surrender, which earned him the nickname "Unconditional Surrender" Grant. On 6–7 April 1862, at Shiloh on the Tennessee, Grant's army was surprised and nearly beaten before it recovered and drove the Confederates back; about 23,000 men fell on both sides, and the Confederate commander Albert Sidney Johnston was killed. In April a Union fleet under David G. Farragut took New Orleans, the largest Southern city. By summer the Union controlled the upper Mississippi and the mouth of the river, though Vicksburg still barred the middle.

**McClellan and the Peninsula.** — The Union's eastern army, the Army of the Potomac, was organized and trained by Major General George B. McClellan, a superb administrator and a cautious field commander who consistently overestimated the enemy's numbers. In the spring of 1862 McClellan moved the army by water to the Virginia peninsula between the York and James rivers and advanced on Richmond. In late May, Joseph E. Johnston, commanding the Confederate army, was wounded at Seven Pines; Robert E. Lee took his place. In the Seven Days' battles, 25 June to 1 July, Lee attacked repeatedly and drove McClellan back from the gates of Richmond. In August, Lee defeated another Union army under John Pope at Second Bull Run, and in September he crossed the Potomac into Maryland.

**Antietam.** — On 17 September 1862, near the Maryland town of Sharpsburg, Lee's army and McClellan's met along Antietam Creek. The fighting lasted one day and produced about 22,700 casualties — the bloodiest single day in American military history. The battle was a tactical draw; Lee withdrew to Virginia, and that withdrawal let Lincoln call it a strategic victory. Five days later, on 22 September, Lincoln issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, announcing that on 1 January 1863 he would declare free the enslaved people in states still in rebellion. McClellan was removed in November. His replacement, Ambrose E. Burnside, attacked the heights behind Fredericksburg in December and lost about 12,600 men in a day.

**The contrabands.** — The enslaved themselves forced the question of emancipation before Lincoln acted. In May 1861, at Fort Monroe, Virginia, the Union general Benjamin F. Butler declared three men who had escaped to his lines "contraband of war" rather than returning them, on the reasoning that their labor served the Confederacy. The label stuck, and thousands of enslaved people fled to Union lines, where they worked as laborers, teamsters, and cooks, and gave the Union information. Congress passed a First Confiscation Act in August 1861 and a Second in July 1862, and authorized the president to employ "persons of African descent" in the military. The pressure from below shaped what the government was willing to do from above.

## Emancipation

**The shift of war aims.** — Lincoln's first priority was always the Union. In a letter to the editor Horace Greeley published on 22 August 1862, he wrote: "If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that." Yet Lincoln also believed slavery was a moral wrong, and he had drafted a proclamation before the letter was published. The final Emancipation Proclamation, issued on 1 January 1863, rested explicitly on his authority as commander-in-chief and on "military necessity." It declared free the enslaved people in the states still in rebellion, named them one by one, and left untouched the four border slave states that had stayed in the Union and the parts of Louisiana and Virginia already under Union control. It also declared that such persons "of suitable condition" would be received into the armed service of the United States. The proclamation did not free a single person it did not already, in law, have the power to reach; what it did was change the war's purpose. Whether it was a cynical expedient or a moral turning point, or both at once, is a question the text invites and does not settle.

**Black soldiers and the United States Colored Troops.** — After January 1863 the Union began recruiting black troops, and in May 1863 the War Department created the Bureau of Colored Troops. Some 180,000 black soldiers served in the United States Colored Troops, roughly a tenth of the Union army, along with some 20,000 black sailors; about 40,000 died. The 54th Massachusetts Infantry, raised in the spring of 1863, led an assault on Fort Wagner, South Carolina, on 18 July 1863 and lost about a third of its men. Black soldiers were at first paid $10 a month, with $3 deducted for clothing, while white privates received $13; Congress equalized pay, retroactively, in June 1864. Captured black soldiers faced enslavement or execution rather than exchange as prisoners, and at Fort Pillow, Tennessee, in April 1864, Confederate troops killed large numbers of black and white Union soldiers after they had surrendered. Corporal James Henry Gooding of the 54th Massachusetts wrote to Lincoln in September 1863 to ask why his regiment, which had "done a Soldiers Duty," could not "have a Soldiers pay," and he pointed out that the government demanded of the Confederacy that it treat all Union soldiers alike. The letter shows a black soldier claiming, in the language of citizenship, the rights the proclamation had promised.

**The meaning of emancipation.** — By making the destruction of slavery a condition of Union victory, the war bound the two purposes together. Enslaved people had already been leaving plantations and serving the Union; the proclamation made their freedom an official aim. In March 1865, in his Second Inaugural Address, Lincoln described slavery as the war's cause and the war as the judgment for it: "if God wills that it continue until all the wealth piled by the bondsman's two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil shall be sunk, and until every drop of blood drawn with the lash shall be paid by another drawn with the sword," then "the judgments of the Lord are true and righteous altogether." Contested: Gallagher has argued that most Union soldiers fought to restore the Union and that emancipation was a means to that end, not the end itself; McPherson reads the soldiers' letters as evidence that a growing number came to see emancipation as inseparable from Union victory.

## The turning point

**Gettysburg.** — In June 1863 Lee again invaded the North, moving his Army of Northern Virginia into Pennsylvania. Lincoln replaced Joseph Hooker with George G. Meade as commander of the Army of the Potomac three days before the battle. On 1 July the two armies collided at Gettysburg; on the second day they fought over Little Round Top, the Wheatfield, the Peach Orchard, and Devil's Den, where the 20th Maine under Joshua L. Chamberlain held the Union left. On the third day Lee sent about 12,500 men under George Pickett and others across open ground against the Union center on Cemetery Ridge. Pickett's Charge failed, and Lee's army retreated on 4 July. The battle cost about 51,000 casualties. Meade was criticized for not pursuing and destroying Lee's army, and Lincoln was deeply disappointed; whether a vigorous pursuit could have ended the war in 1863 remains a favorite counterfactual.

**Vicksburg.** — On the same day Lee retreated from Gettysburg, the Confederate garrison at Vicksburg, Mississippi, surrendered to Grant. Grant had spent months trying to take the city, finally marching his army down the west bank, crossing below it, and defeating the Confederates in a series of battles before laying siege. After six weeks, on 4 July 1863, the Confederate commander John C. Pemberton surrendered about 30,000 men. With the fall of Port Hudson a few days later, the Union controlled the whole Mississippi; Lincoln wrote that "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea." The Confederacy was cut in two.

**Chattanooga.** — In September 1863 the Union army under William S. Rosecrans was defeated at Chickamauga in northern Georgia and trapped in Chattanooga. Grant was given command, and with reinforcements under Hooker and Sherman he broke the siege in November, routing Braxton Bragg's army at Lookout Mountain and Missionary Ridge. Chattanooga became the base for the Union advance into Georgia the following spring, and Grant's reputation carried him to Washington.

## Total war

**Grant and the Overland Campaign.** — In March 1864 Grant was made lieutenant general and general-in-chief of all Union armies. He set the Army of the Potomac and the western army under Sherman to advance at the same time, so that the Confederacy could not shift troops from one front to the other. Through May and June Grant attacked Lee in Virginia: the Wilderness, Spotsylvania Court House, and Cold Harbor. In about six weeks the Army of the Potomac lost roughly 55,000 men killed, wounded, and missing. Grant kept moving south and east, writing that he proposed "to fight it out on this line if it takes all summer." By mid-June he had pinned Lee in the trenches around Petersburg, and the siege there lasted nine months. The strategy was attrition: the side with more men and supplies could accept the losses, and the side with fewer could not.

**Sherman and the march.** — William T. Sherman commanded the Union armies in the West. In the spring of 1864 he advanced from Chattanooga toward Atlanta against Joseph E. Johnston, and after Johnston was replaced by the aggressive John Bell Hood, Sherman took Atlanta on 2 September 1864. The victory came in time to lift Northern morale before the November election. In November Sherman left Atlanta with about 60,000 men, cut his supply lines, and marched to the sea, living off the country and destroying railroads, factories, and stores along a path some sixty miles wide. He took Savannah in December, then turned north through the Carolinas, where his troops burned much of Columbia, South Carolina, in February 1865. Sherman's purpose was to break the South's ability and will to continue the war; the campaign against property and morale is the clearest American example of what came to be called total war.

**The home fronts.** — The North financed the war with greenbacks (paper money issued under the Legal Tender Act of February 1862), an income tax, and a new national banking system, and its economy expanded as factories, railroads, and farms met wartime demand. Prices rose faster than wages, and women took on new work as clerks, factory hands, nurses, and organizers of relief; the United States Sanitary Commission, run largely by women, raised money and supplies. The South, cut off from imports and short of food, suffered far more. Inflation reached extraordinary levels by 1865, and in April 1863 a crowd of women in Richmond, many of them soldiers' wives, marched on the government for bread and looted shops. Enslaved workers slowed their labor, fled, or resisted, and the Confederate economy lost the labor on which it depended. Both home fronts were also political battlegrounds, with fierce arguments over conscription, civil liberties, and the war's cost.

**Conscription and dissent.** — The Confederate Conscription Act of April 1862 was the first national draft in American history; it allowed substitutes and exempted certain occupations, and a later provision exempted one white man on each plantation with twenty or more enslaved workers — the "twenty Negro law," which poor whites resented as a rich man's war. The Union's Enrollment Act of March 1863 allowed a man to buy his way out by paying a $300 commutation fee or hiring a substitute. In July 1863, in New York City, the draft riots killed about 120 people over four days; much of the mob's fury was aimed at black residents, who were blamed for the war and feared as competitors for work. Northern dissenters, called Copperheads, wanted peace and opposed emancipation; the Ohio Democrat Clement L. Vallandigham was arrested for his speeches, and Lincoln used the suspension of habeas corpus to hold or try critics and draft resisters.

**The election of 1864.** — Lincoln ran for re-election against McClellan, whom he had removed from command. The Democratic platform called the war a failure and hinted at a negotiated peace that would leave slavery standing. In the summer of 1864 the war was stalemated before Petersburg and Atlanta had not fallen, and Lincoln expected to lose. Sherman's capture of Atlanta in September and Philip Sheridan's victories in the Shenandoah Valley turned the mood. Lincoln won about 55 per cent of the popular vote and 212 of 233 electoral votes; the soldiers' vote went heavily for him. His victory meant the war would continue to unconditional victory, and on 31 January 1865 the House of Representatives passed the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolished slavery throughout the United States.

## The end

**The collapse of the Confederacy.** — In the autumn of 1864 Hood's Confederate army advanced into Tennessee, was checked at Franklin on 30 November, and was destroyed by George H. Thomas at Nashville in mid-December. Sherman marched north through the Carolinas. Around Petersburg the siege ground on until 1 April 1865, when Sheridan's cavalry broke the Confederate line at Five Forks; Lee abandoned Petersburg and Richmond. On 9 April 1865, at Appomattox Court House, Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant. Grant's terms were generous: officers kept their sidearms, the men were paroled and sent home, and no one was to be tried for treason. Johnston surrendered to Sherman in North Carolina on 26 April, and the remaining Confederate forces laid down their arms by June.

**The cost.** — The war killed more Americans than any other conflict before or since. For a century the accepted figure was about 620,000 military deaths; in 2011 the historian J. David Hacker, using census records, estimated the true number at around 750,000, a figure that includes both Union and Confederate dead and both combat and disease. Two-thirds or more died of disease rather than wounds. Prisons on both sides, overcrowded and short of food and medicine, killed thousands: at Andersonville, Georgia, about 13,000 Union prisoners died, and at Elmira, New York, about 3,000 Confederates. Drew Gilpin Faust has argued that the sheer scale of death forced Americans to invent new ways of burying, counting, and mourning the dead — national cemeteries, the search for the missing, embalming, and the idea that the soldier's sacrifice obligated the nation — and that the "work of death" reshaped what Americans thought their country owed its citizens.

**Aftermath.** — On 14 April 1865, five days after Appomattox, Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre in Washington by John Wilkes Booth; Andrew Johnson became president. The war settled two things: the Union could not be dissolved, and slavery was ended by the Thirteenth Amendment. It left open what freedom would mean for four million formerly enslaved people, and that question fell to Reconstruction. The wartime transformation of the war's purpose — from restoring the Union to abolishing slavery — was the ground on which the postwar argument stood.

## Chronology

- **6 November 1860** — Lincoln elected president with no electoral votes in the South.
- **20 December 1860** — South Carolina secedes; six more states follow by 1 February 1861.
- **4 February 1861** — Confederate States of America formed at Montgomery, Alabama; Jefferson Davis chosen president.
- **12–13 April 1861** — Confederate batteries fire on Fort Sumter; Anderson surrenders.
- **15 April 1861** — Lincoln calls for 75,000 militia; Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina secede.
- **21 July 1861** — First Bull Run; the Union army is routed.
- **6–7 April 1862** — Shiloh; about 23,000 casualties.
- **16 April 1862** — Confederate Conscription Act, the first national draft.
- **25 April 1862** — Farragut captures New Orleans.
- **25 June–1 July 1862** — Seven Days' battles; Lee drives McClellan from the Peninsula.
- **17 September 1862** — Antietam; about 22,700 casualties; Lee retreats.
- **22 September 1862** — Lincoln issues the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation.
- **13 December 1862** — Fredericksburg; Burnside's army shattered.
- **1 January 1863** — Final Emancipation Proclamation; black enlistment authorized.
- **3 March 1863** — Union Enrollment Act (conscription).
- **1–3 July 1863** — Gettysburg; about 51,000 casualties; Pickett's Charge on the third day.
- **4 July 1863** — Vicksburg surrenders; the Mississippi opens.
- **13–16 July 1863** — New York City draft riots.
- **18 July 1863** — 54th Massachusetts assaults Fort Wagner.
- **23–25 November 1863** — Chattanooga; Bragg's army broken.
- **May–June 1864** — Overland Campaign: the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor.
- **2 September 1864** — Atlanta falls to Sherman.
- **8 November 1864** — Lincoln re-elected over McClellan.
- **15 November–21 December 1864** — Sherman's March to the Sea.
- **31 January 1865** — House passes the Thirteenth Amendment.
- **4 March 1865** — Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address.
- **1–3 April 1865** — Five Forks; Petersburg and Richmond evacuated.
- **9 April 1865** — Lee surrenders to Grant at Appomattox Court House.
- **14 April 1865** — Lincoln shot at Ford's Theatre; dies the next morning.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the seven states of the lower South secede when Lincoln had promised not to touch slavery where it already existed?
- Was the Emancipation Proclamation a moral act, a military measure, or both — and what in the text can help decide?
- What did black soldiers and their families expect from the Union, and how did the government answer them?
- Gettysburg and Vicksburg were decided in the same week. Which mattered more, and to whom?
- If the Confederacy survived until 1865, how far was Northern industrial superiority really decisive?
