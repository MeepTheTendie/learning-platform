# Reconstruction

**Period:** 1865–1877 · **Region:** The United States, especially the South

**The Savannah meeting.** — On 12 January 1865, at Savannah, General William Tecumseh Sherman and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton asked twenty black ministers, led by the Baptist minister Garrison Frazier, what the freedpeople most wanted. Frazier answered that freedom meant living as other people did and owning land; "the way we can best take care of ourselves," he said, "is to have land, and turn it and till it by our own labor." Four days later Sherman set aside the Sea Islands and a coastal strip for black settlement in forty-acre plots. Within a year President Andrew Johnson's pardons had restored most of that land to its former owners. The episode holds the problem of the era: emancipation had destroyed slavery but had not settled what freedom would be worth. This unit follows one question: why did the promise of emancipation give way, within a generation, to segregation and disenfranchisement?

**A revolution in law, an open question in fact.** — The Thirteenth Amendment, passed by the House on 31 January 1865 and ratified on 6 December 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted"—an exception with a long life. It freed about four million people, the largest seizure of property in American history, and said nothing about land, wages, schools, the vote, or protection from violence. Freedpeople filled the silence: they searched for relatives sold away, legalized marriages, founded churches and schools, and demanded land.

## Presidential Reconstruction

**Lincoln's plan and Johnson's.** — Lincoln's Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction (December 1863) offered a pardon to most Confederates who swore loyalty and let a state reorganize once ten per cent of its 1860 voters had done so. Congress replied with the Wade–Davis Bill (July 1864), which demanded a majority oath and barred former Confederate officeholders from voting; Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Shot on 14 April 1865, he was succeeded by Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Unionist and former slaveholder. Johnson's plan, announced on 29 May 1865, offered amnesty to nearly all Confederates who swore allegiance but reserved pardons to himself for fourteen classes, including high officials, officers above colonel, and owners of more than $20,000 in taxable property. He granted roughly 13,500 pardons. To rejoin the Union a state had to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, repudiate its war debt, and void secession—but not enfranchise black men, which Johnson opposed.

**The return of the old order.** — By December 1865 every former Confederate state except Texas had reorganized under white-only elections, often choosing the men who had led secession. Georgia sent Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy's vice president, to the Senate. Congress refused to seat the Southern delegations, deciding that Reconstruction was a federal question it would define.

**The Black Codes.** — In 1865–66 the new legislatures passed the Black Codes. Mississippi's (November 1865) required black workers to carry written labor contracts, criminalized hiring away another's worker, allowed apprenticeship of black children judged unsupported, barred black testimony against whites in many cases, and imposed special taxes. South Carolina's was harsher; several states banned interracial marriage. The codes did not restore slavery but built a subordinate caste, binding black labor to the land and turning unemployment into crime. Northerners were less shocked by the racial insult than by the proof that the old ruling class meant to keep control of black labor. In 1866 white mobs killed some forty-six black residents in Memphis in May and more than thirty in New Orleans in July.

## Congressional Reconstruction

**The break with Johnson.** — The Thirty-Ninth Congress (December 1865) created a Joint Committee on Reconstruction, led by Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner, which held that the war had given Congress power to remake the South and that black suffrage was needed to protect the Union's victory. In 1866 Congress passed a bill extending the Freedmen's Bureau and a Civil Rights Act making all persons born in the United States citizens with equal rights to contract, sue, testify, hold property, and be protected by law. Johnson vetoed both as exceeding federal power; Congress overrode the Civil Rights Act on 9 April 1866 and passed a revised Bureau bill over another veto in July.

**The Fourteenth Amendment.** — Passed in June 1866 and ratified in July 1868, the Fourteenth Amendment was the most consequential addition to the Constitution. Section 1 made all persons born or naturalized in the United States citizens of nation and state, and forbade any state to abridge privileges or immunities, to deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process, or to deny any person equal protection of the laws. Section 2 reduced representation in proportion to adult male citizens excluded from voting—a penalty on the white South, not a suffrage guarantee. Section 3 disqualified former Confederates from office unless Congress removed the disability; Section 4 repudiated the Confederate debt; Section 5 gave Congress enforcement power. The language was deliberately broad, and its authors disagreed about it: Senator Jacob Howard said the privileges or immunities clause applied the first eight amendments to the states, while others thought it did less. The Republican landslide of 1866 was read as approval.

**The Reconstruction Acts and impeachment.** — In March 1867, over Johnson's vetoes, Congress divided the ten unreconstructed states into five military districts and required each to write a constitution with black male suffrage and to ratify the Fourteenth Amendment before readmission. Tennessee, which had already ratified, escaped. The army registered voters and supervised elections. Congress also passed the Tenure of Office Act, requiring Senate consent to remove certain officials. When Johnson dismissed Secretary of War Edwin Stanton in February 1868, the House impeached him; the Senate failed by one vote to convict (35–19), and Johnson finished his term. The episode established that removal required a political consensus that partisanship alone could not supply.

**The Fifteenth Amendment.** — Passed in February 1869 and ratified in March 1870, it declared that the vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." It did not grant the vote directly and did not forbid literacy tests, poll taxes, or violence; it barred only the stated grounds. It also left women of every race without the vote.

## The Freedmen's Bureau

**An agency for the transition.** — The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, created in March 1865 under General Oliver Otis Howard, distributed rations, ran hospitals, supervised labor contracts, heard complaints, and—with northern benevolent societies—helped found schools, among them Howard, Fisk, and Hampton. Its authority was temporary and its land policy collapsed at once. In late 1865 Johnson ordered abandoned land restored to pardoned owners, reversing the Bureau's Circular 13. Its labor rules pressed freedpeople to sign annual contracts and punished those who refused work. A January 1866 circular by Colonel Samuel Thomas, assistant commissioner for Mississippi, told freedpeople they must contract for the year, could expect no property from the government, and, if found idle, would be treated as vagrants and "made to labor on public works." The Bureau protected freedpeople from the worst abuses, but it aimed to turn slaves into wage laborers, not landowners or full citizens. Its reauthorization lapsed in 1872.

## Black political life and the first black legislators

**The conventions and the vote.** — The Reconstruction Acts opened a political world. In 1867–68 the Southern states held constitutional conventions elected with black participation; about a quarter of the delegates across the South were black, and South Carolina's was majority black. The new constitutions provided universal male suffrage, abolished property qualifications for office, created public schools, and reformed the courts. Union Leagues, meeting in churches and schools under army protection, organized black voters.

**Black officeholders.** — The charge of "negro rule" was false: black men never held more than a minority of offices, and no Southern state had a black governor for long. But some 2,000 black men held public office. Hiram Rhodes Revels of Mississippi was seated in the Senate on 25 February 1870, filling the seat once held by Jefferson Davis; Blanche K. Bruce, also of Mississippi, served in the Senate from 1875 to 1881. Joseph H. Rainey of South Carolina became the first black member of the House in December 1870; Robert Brown Elliott, also of South Carolina, served 1871–74 and was among the ablest debaters of his generation. John R. Lynch of Mississippi and James T. Rapier of Alabama reached the House; P. B. S. Pinchback served briefly as acting governor of Louisiana in 1872–73. In South Carolina the legislature was majority black in the early 1870s. Many of these men had been free before the war. They argued the case for black citizenship in constitutional terms, as Elliott's speech on the civil rights bill in January 1874 shows. White Southerners answered with the labels "carpetbagger," "scalawag," and manipulated black voter—weapons that cast their own defeat as the work of outsiders.

## White terror and the counter-revolution

**The Klan.** — The Ku Klux Klan was founded in December 1865 at Pulaski, Tennessee, by six former Confederate officers, and spread across the South. Its prescript, revised in 1868, describes an "institution of Chivalry, Humanity, Mercy, and Patriotism" that protects the weak and defends the Constitution. Its membership interrogatories tell another story: a candidate had to oppose "negro equality, both social and political," favor "a white man's government," and swear never to reveal the Order's secrets on pain of death. The gap between the words and the questions is the gap between a myth and a program. In practice the Klan and its imitators rode at night, whipped and murdered black and white Republicans, and burned schools and churches, aiming at the one thing that made Republican government possible: black voting.

**The campaign of 1870s terror.** — In April 1873 at Colfax, Louisiana, a white mob attacked a black militia and Republican officials; after the defenders surrendered, about 150 black men were killed. In September 1874 the White League fought state militia and police in New Orleans, killing more than thirty and briefly seizing the government. In Mississippi in 1875 a coordinated campaign of intimidation—the "Mississippi Plan"—drove Republicans from the state. In South Carolina in 1876 the Hamburg massacre and the Ellenton riot, with the Red Shirts, broke the Republican hold.

**The federal response and its limits.** — The Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871 made it a federal crime to interfere with voting or to conspire to deprive citizens of rights, and the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 let the president suspend habeas corpus. Grant used them: in October 1871 he imposed martial law in nine South Carolina counties, and federal convictions broke the first Klan. But the laws ran through federal courts and juries, and the courts were narrowing. In United States v. Cruikshank (1876) the Supreme Court overturned the Colfax convictions, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment restrained states, not private individuals, and that the federal government had no general power to punish private violence. The decision made the Enforcement Acts nearly unenforceable where they were most needed.

## The Grant years

**Enforcement and scandal.** — Ulysses S. Grant, elected in 1868 and re-elected in 1872, was the last president to attempt a serious defense of Reconstruction: he signed the Enforcement Acts, sent prosecutors and troops into the South, and backed the Fifteenth Amendment. His administration was also the most corrupt of the century. In September 1869 Jay Gould and James Fisk tried to corner the gold market with the help of Grant's brother-in-law, in the "Black Friday" panic. In 1872 the Crédit Mobilier scandal exposed bribes to congressmen by Union Pacific builders, implicating Vice President Schuyler Colfax. In 1875 the Whiskey Ring was broken, involving Grant's private secretary Orville Babcock; in 1876 Secretary of War William Belknap resigned and was impeached over trading-post bribes. The scandals weakened the party's claim to moral leadership.

**The reform mood.** — In 1872 Liberal Republicans broke away to oppose Grant, complaining of corruption and "bayonet rule" in the South; they nominated Horace Greeley, whom the Democrats also backed. Grant won easily, but the critique took root. Many Northern Republicans were tiring of the Southern question and had begun to see black Southerners as a dependent laboring class rather than independent citizens—a shift Heather Cox Richardson has traced. That change in sentiment mattered more than any single scandal.

## Panic, depression, and the turn to other questions

**The Panic of 1873.** — On 18 September 1873 the banking house of Jay Cooke & Company failed, brought down by the collapse of Northern Pacific Railroad financing. The stock exchange closed for ten days, and a depression followed until 1879. Railroads and banks failed and urban unemployment rose. Politics turned to currency, tariffs, and jobs; the argument over greenbacks and the gold standard absorbed the energy that had gone into Reconstruction. In 1877 the Great Railroad Strike shut down traffic from Baltimore to Chicago and was broken by federal troops. The "labor question" replaced the "Southern question" in Northern politics. As the depression deepened, many voters came to see the Southern Republican governments as corrupt and dependent on troops, and "home rule" began to sound reasonable even to Republicans. By 1876 Reconstruction had lost much of its Northern base.

## The courts narrow the amendments

**The Slaughter-House Cases.** — In 1873 the Supreme Court gave the Fourteenth Amendment its first important reading and narrowed it sharply. By 5–4, it held that the privileges or immunities clause protected only a few rights of national citizenship, not the general civil rights of citizens, which remained under state control. That removed the most natural textual basis for federal protection of civil rights. The Court soon held that the amendment did not give women the vote (Minor v. Happersett, 1875), having upheld Illinois's exclusion of women from the bar (Bradwell v. Illinois, 1873).

**The state-action doctrine.** — Cruikshank (1876) and United States v. Reese (1876) completed the retreat, establishing that the war amendments barred discrimination by state governments but not by private persons, and that Congress could not reach private violence or the devices—literacy tests, poll taxes, fraud—by which black men were kept from voting. The full consequence came in 1883, when the Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875 in the Civil Rights Cases, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment did not authorize Congress to forbid discrimination in inns, theaters, and railroads because those were private businesses. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented, but the majority's reading stood for more than fifty years. The Court had done what no army could: narrowed the constitutional promise until the Southern states could ignore it.

## The election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877

**The disputed returns.** — In 1876 Rutherford B. Hayes, Republican governor of Ohio, ran against Samuel J. Tilden, Democratic governor of New York. Tilden won the popular vote by about 250,000 and appeared to have 184 electoral votes, one short of a majority, but returns from Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina—the three states still governed by Republicans—were disputed, as was one from Oregon. If Hayes won all twenty, he would have 185 and the presidency.

**The Electoral Commission and the bargain.** — Congress created an Electoral Commission of fifteen—five House members, five senators, five Supreme Court justices—which by 8–7 along party lines awarded every disputed vote to Hayes. The Democratic House threatened a filibuster, and in February 1877 Southern Democrats and Ohio Republicans met at the Wormley House hotel. What was agreed is debated: the essentials were that Hayes would be inaugurated, troops would leave the statehouses of South Carolina and Louisiana, a Southerner would get a Cabinet post (David M. Key of Tennessee became postmaster general), and the administration would not oppose "home rule" and would look kindly on aid for Southern internal improvements, especially the Texas and Pacific Railroad. C. Vann Woodward argued in Reunion and Reaction (1951) that this was a genuine economic bargain; Allan Peskin (1973) doubted any single deal was struck. Either way, Hayes was inaugurated on 5 March 1877, and within weeks the last troops left. Reconstruction was over.

## Redemption and the new order

**The Redeemers.** — White Democratic governments, calling themselves Redeemers, took power across the South between 1870 and 1877. They cut taxes and public spending, dismantled the Reconstruction school systems, and rewrote law to protect plantation labor. They did not at once strip black men of the vote; for a decade or more black Southerners still voted in many places. The full architecture of segregation and disenfranchisement was built later, in the 1890s and after.

**Disenfranchisement.** — Mississippi's constitution of 1890 invented the model: a poll tax, a literacy test, and an "understanding" clause letting registrars judge whether an applicant's reading was satisfactory, while illiterate white men could register. Louisiana added the grandfather clause in 1898, exempting men whose fathers or grandfathers had voted before 1867. Other states copied these devices, backed by violence and fraud. In 1898 a white mob in Wilmington, North Carolina, overthrew the elected city government and killed an estimated sixty to three hundred black residents. By the early twentieth century black registration in the Deep South had fallen to a few per cent of its former level. The Fifteenth Amendment remained on the books, but the courts did nothing against the new devices.

**Segregation and the convict-lease system.** — Southern states separated the races on railroads and in schools in the 1880s and 1890s, and in 1896 the Supreme Court upheld them in Plessy v. Ferguson under "separate but equal." The convict-lease system grew from the Thirteenth Amendment's exception for criminal punishment: states convicted black men, often on flimsy charges, and leased them to plantations, mines, and railroads in conditions close to slavery. It survived into the twentieth century, not abolished in Alabama until 1928. On the land, sharecropping and the crop-lien system replaced wage labor, and by 1880 about forty per cent of Southern farms were worked by tenants perpetually in debt to the merchant who advanced seed and supplies. Some fled: in the "Exodus" to Kansas in 1879, some twenty thousand to forty thousand left the South.

## The Lost Cause and the memory of the war

**The making of a myth.** — The white South did not merely restore its power; it wrote a story to justify it. The Lost Cause, named by Edward A. Pollard in a book of 1866, held that the Confederacy had fought for states' rights and honor rather than slavery, that its soldiers were noble, and that Reconstruction had been corruption and "negro rule" imposed by outsiders. Jubal Early and the Southern Historical Society promoted it in the 1870s; the United Confederate Veterans (1889) and United Daughters of the Confederacy (1894) carried it into the next century, raising monuments, shaping textbooks, and teaching a version of the war in which slavery was incidental.

**Memory and reunion.** — The Lost Cause met a Northern need. By the 1880s and 1890s, white Northerners and Southerners wanted a common story that honored both sides without arguing about slavery. David W. Blight calls this the reconciliationist memory and argues that it triumphed over the emancipationist memory—the memory, kept alive largely by black Americans, that the war's meaning was the destruction of slavery. The fiftieth anniversary of Gettysburg in 1913, addressed by President Woodrow Wilson, was a reunion of white veterans in which black veterans were segregated and emancipation unmentioned. Popular culture carried the same message: Thomas Dixon's The Clansman (1905) and D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation (1915) presented the Klan as the savior of white civilization, and Gone with the Wind (novel 1936, film 1939) fixed the Lost Cause in the national imagination. The scholarly version was the Dunning School, named for William Archibald Dunning of Columbia, whose Reconstruction, Political and Economic (1907) taught that Reconstruction was a mistake imposed on a prostrate South. Against that tide stood W. E. B. Du Bois's Black Reconstruction in America (1935), the first major work to treat freedpeople as political actors, and institutions such as the NAACP (1909) and the Juneteenth celebrations.

## Why it failed

**The debate.** — The Dunning School blamed black suffrage and Radical vindictiveness and praised the "redemption" of the South; that view is now rejected, though it shaped popular memory for decades. Du Bois (1935) argued that the enslaved had staged a "general strike" during the war and that Reconstruction was a genuine experiment in interracial democracy, overthrown by a counter-revolution of the planter class supported by Northern capital. Eric Foner's Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution (1988) is the modern synthesis: a real social revolution from below, defeated by the refusal to give freedpeople land, the North's fading commitment, the Supreme Court's narrowing of the amendments, and organized violence. C. Vann Woodward argued in 1955 that segregation itself was a relatively late construction, built in the 1890s after a period of more fluid race relations—evidence that the outcome was not inevitable. Steven Hahn's A Nation Under Our Feet (2003) follows black political life from slavery onward and stresses its grassroots strength; Gregory P. Downs's After Appomattox (2015) argues that the army's occupation was the real engine of Reconstruction and that its withdrawal, not the Compromise of 1877 alone, was the decisive break.

**The contingency.** — Reconstruction failed because a democracy that had just fought a war over slavery could not sustain the will to remake the society slavery had built. The amendments survived, and their promise was revived in the twentieth century, but in the generation after 1877 the federal government looked away while the South rebuilt white supremacy. That choice was contested at every step, and it was not the only possible outcome. The black legislators, the Union Leagues, the Bureau agents, and the ordinary freedpeople who demanded land and schools and the vote left a record the Lost Cause could not erase.

## Chronology

- **1 January 1863** — The Emancipation Proclamation takes effect in the rebelling states.
- **December 1863** — Lincoln's Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction offers the "ten per cent" plan.
- **31 January 1865** — The House passes the Thirteenth Amendment.
- **3 March 1865** — The Freedmen's Bureau is created.
- **14–15 April 1865** — Lincoln is shot and dies; Andrew Johnson becomes president.
- **29 May 1865** — Johnson announces his plan of amnesty and reconstruction.
- **6 December 1865** — The Thirteenth Amendment is ratified; the Klan is founded at Pulaski, Tennessee.
- **December 1865** — The Thirty-Ninth Congress refuses to seat the Southern delegations.
- **April 1866** — Congress overrides Johnson's veto of the Civil Rights Act of 1866.
- **13 June 1866** — Congress passes the Fourteenth Amendment; it is ratified on 9 July 1868.
- **May and July 1866** — White mobs kill black residents in Memphis and New Orleans.
- **2 March 1867** — The Reconstruction Acts divide the South into five military districts.
- **24 February 1868** — The House impeaches Andrew Johnson; the Senate acquits him on 16 May by one vote.
- **February 1869** — Congress passes the Fifteenth Amendment; it is ratified on 30 March 1870.
- **25 February 1870** — Hiram Rhodes Revels is seated in the United States Senate.
- **April 1873** — The Colfax massacre in Louisiana kills about 150 black men.
- **September 1873** — The Panic begins with the failure of Jay Cooke & Company.
- **September 1874** — The White League fights in New Orleans at the Battle of Liberty Place.
- **1875** — The Mississippi Plan and the Whiskey Ring scandal; the Civil Rights Act passes in March.
- **1876** — United States v. Cruikshank narrows the Fourteenth Amendment; the Hamburg and Ellenton massacres.
- **November 1876** — Hayes and Tilden contest the election; the Electoral Commission awards the presidency to Hayes in February 1877.
- **April 1877** — Federal troops withdraw from the Southern statehouses; Reconstruction ends.
- **1890** — Mississippi's constitution begins the systematic disenfranchisement of black voters.
- **1896** — Plessy v. Ferguson upholds "separate but equal."
- **1898** — The Wilmington coup in North Carolina; Louisiana's grandfather clause.
- **1907** — The Dunning School's Reconstruction, Political and Economic appears.
- **1935** — W. E. B. Du Bois publishes Black Reconstruction in America.
- **1988** — Eric Foner publishes Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the federal government refuse to give freedpeople land, and how much did that decision shape everything that followed?
- What did the Fourteenth Amendment's authors mean by "equal protection," and who decided what it meant?
- The Klan's prescript calls the Order an institution of chivalry and patriotism. What does the gap between its words and its questions tell us about how political violence justifies itself?
- Was Reconstruction defeated by a single event, such as the Compromise of 1877, or by a series of choices spread over a decade?
- If segregation and disenfranchisement were built in the 1890s rather than immediately after 1877, what changed in the meantime?
