# The Gilded Age and industrialization

**Period:** c. 1877–1900 · **Region:** The United States

**A name from a novel.** — In 1873 Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner published *The Gilded Age: A Tale of To-Day*, a satire about a country they thought had been covered with a thin layer of gold over base metal. The label stuck to the decades after Reconstruction, from the withdrawal of the last federal troops from the South in 1877 to the turn of the century. The United States changed faster in those years than at any earlier time: the population rose from about 38.5 million in 1870 to 76 million in 1900, railroad trackage from some 53,000 miles to nearly 200,000, and steel output from almost nothing before the Bessemer converters of the 1860s to more than ten million tons a year by 1900, when the country made more steel than Britain and Germany together. Yet the gulf between rich and poor widened, hundreds of thousands of children under fifteen worked in factories and mines, and the Supreme Court in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) gave a constitutional blessing to racial segregation. The organizing question is not whether the age brought progress but for whom, and at what cost.

**Two words for the same men.** — Contemporaries could not agree on what to call the new industrialists. Defenders said "captains of industry"; critics said "robber barons," borrowing the name of the medieval German lords who taxed traffic on the Rhine. The choice was itself an argument about whether the great fortunes were earned by service or seized by extraction.

## The corporation, the railroad, and the market

**A new legal person.** — The characteristic institution of the age was not the factory, which was older, but the corporation. Before the Civil War most businesses were owned by individuals or partners personally liable for the firm's debts; after it, state law made limited liability easy, so that investors risked only what they put in, and New Jersey's statute of 1889 let one corporation hold the stock of others. The form gathered capital from strangers, shielded them from ruin, and let a business outlive its founders. The "trust" took its name from the device Standard Oil used: stockholders exchanged their shares for certificates in a single trust, and a board of trustees ran the combination as one firm; when the trust was attacked, lawyers turned to the New Jersey holding company and did the same under another name.

**The iron road.** — The railroads were the first great corporations. The first transcontinental line was joined at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869, where the Union Pacific, built west from Omaha with Irish and veteran labor, met the Central Pacific, built east from Sacramento with Chinese labor. The roads standardized the gauge of track, divided the country into four standard time zones on 18 November 1883, and strung telegraph wire along their rights of way. The telegraph, consolidated in Western Union, let a merchant in Chicago learn a price in New York in minutes. Refrigerated cars carried dressed beef east, and mail-order houses — Montgomery Ward from 1872, Sears, Roebuck from 1888 — sold to farmers by catalogue. The result was a single national market in which producers everywhere competed, and farmers who had borrowed at fixed rates watched crop prices fall.

**Carnegie and Rockefeller.** — Two men became the emblems of the new wealth. Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919), born in a weaver's cottage in Dunfermline, Scotland, built a steel empire around Pittsburgh. He adopted the Bessemer converter early and integrated every stage of production — ore fields, coke ovens, blast furnaces, rolling mills, and the ships and railroads that moved the ore — so that his costs fell below his rivals'. In 1901 he sold the company to a syndicate led by the banker J. P. Morgan for $480 million, and the new United States Steel Corporation became the first billion-dollar company. John D. Rockefeller (1839–1937) took a different road: Standard Oil, incorporated in Ohio in 1870, refined about a quarter of the nation's oil by 1872 and perhaps ninety percent by 1880, cutting costs by consolidating refineries and negotiating secret rebates from the railroads that shipped his oil.

**The case for the age.** — The new wealth had a philosophy. Herbert Spencer, whose ideas were often summed up as "social Darwinism," argued that society advanced through the free struggle of individuals and that protecting the weak only preserved the unfit. The Yale professor William Graham Sumner put the case in *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other* (1883): the "forgotten man" was the sober worker who paid taxes and obeyed the law while reformers spent his money on the improvident. Andrew Carnegie gave the creed its most famous form in "Wealth," published in the *North American Review* in June 1889 and afterward known as "The Gospel of Wealth." Competition, he wrote, produced "great inequality of environment, the concentration of business ... in the hands of a few," and this was "not only beneficial, but essential for the future progress of the race." The rich man was a trustee who held his surplus "as trust funds" for the community; the essay ended, "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced." Whether the gospel described the age or excused it is the question the sources ask.

**The state and the market.** — Government did not stand aside. It gave the railroads some 130 million acres in land grants. In *Munn v. Illinois* (1877) the Supreme Court upheld state regulation of grain elevators as businesses "affected with a public interest"; nine years later *Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway v. Illinois* (1886) held that states could not regulate interstate rates, and Congress answered with the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887, creating the Interstate Commerce Commission. In 1890 the Sherman Antitrust Act made "every contract, combination ... or conspiracy, in restraint of trade" a crime, but for more than a decade it was used far more often against unions than against the trusts; in *United States v. E. C. Knight* (1895) the Court held that manufacturing was not interstate commerce, and the sugar trust escaped it.

## Immigration and the city

**The new arrivals.** — Between 1860 and 1900 some fourteen million immigrants entered the United States. The earlier stream came mainly from northern and western Europe; after about 1880 the majority came from southern and eastern Europe — Italy, Poland, the Russian empire, Austria-Hungary, Greece — and from China on the west coast until the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred laborers. Most settled in cities, partly because the best free land was gone and partly because the jobs were urban. The 1890 census found, for the first time, that a majority of Americans lived in places of 2,500 people or more. New York held about 3.4 million people by 1900, Chicago 1.7 million, Philadelphia 1.3 million.

**The tenement and the sweatshop.** — Housing could not keep up. In New York the tenement house became the standard dwelling of the poor; the "dumbbell" design legalized in 1879 left a narrow air shaft down the middle and packed as many as twenty-four families onto a 25-foot lot, with water in the yard and often a single privy for the building. Jacob Riis, a Danish-born police reporter, photographed the blocks and published *How the Other Half Lives* in 1890, and his images did more than any statistic to make the slum visible. Much of the clothing the country wore was made in tenement rooms by families working for piece rates, with children beside their parents. Tuberculosis spread in the crowded dark; infant mortality in the worst wards ran above one in five.

**Machine politics and reform.** — City government was often a machine. Tammany Hall in New York distributed jobs, coal, and naturalization papers to immigrants in exchange for votes, and its leader William M. Tweed was convicted of corruption in 1873 after the cartoonist Thomas Nast and the *New York Times* exposed the ring's theft from the city treasury. Yet the machine also answered the door. Against it stood a generation of reformers: Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House on Chicago's Near West Side in 1889, one of hundreds of settlement houses; charity organization societies investigated each case; and the Salvation Army, arriving from Britain in 1880, offered soup and shelter. By 1900 more than a fifth of women over fifteen worked for pay, most of them young and unmarried, and the census counted 1.75 million children between ten and fifteen "gainfully occupied" — cheap labor that helped hold down the wages of adult men.

## Labor and the great strikes

**The Knights of Labor.** — The first national union of consequence was the Noble and Holy Order of the Knights of Labor, founded in 1869 by the Philadelphia garment cutter Uriah Stephens and led from 1879 by Terence Powderly. The Knights were unusual in admitting all workers, whatever their trade, sex, or race — though in practice the order often excluded the Chinese — and in aiming at a cooperative commonwealth rather than only better wages. Their motto, "an injury to one is the concern of all," expressed a broad solidarity, and their platform called for the eight-hour day, equal pay for women, and the abolition of child labor. Membership exploded after a successful strike against Jay Gould's railroads in 1885, reaching perhaps 700,000 in 1886; then came Haymarket, and by the 1890s the order was a shadow.

**The American Federation of Labor.** — A different model was founded in 1886, when a group of craft unions met in Columbus, Ohio, and formed the American Federation of Labor under Samuel Gompers, a cigar maker born in London. The Federation organized skilled workers by trade, not all workers together; it concentrated on wages, hours, and working conditions rather than a new social order; and it used strikes, boycotts, and collective bargaining. Its "pure and simple" unionism was narrower than the Knights' vision and more durable, though it largely excluded the unskilled, most women, and most Black workers. It survived the depression of the 1890s and grew into the dominant labor body of the next century.

**1877: the Great Railroad Strike.** — The first nationwide strike began in July 1877, when the Baltimore and Ohio and other eastern railroads cut wages for the second time in a year. Work stopped at Martinsburg, West Virginia, and spread to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, and St. Louis. In Pittsburgh a crowd fought the state militia, and the Pennsylvania Railroad's roundhouse, shops, and hundreds of cars were burned; more than twenty people were killed there and perhaps a hundred across the country. President Rutherford B. Hayes sent federal troops to several cities, the first use of the army in a labor dispute on such a scale. The strike collapsed within weeks, but it showed that the new industrial order could be stopped.

**1886: Haymarket.** — The eight-hour movement called a general strike for 1 May 1886. Two days later, police fired on strikers at the McCormick Reaper works in Chicago, killing several. On the evening of 4 May a crowd gathered in Haymarket Square; as the police advanced, someone threw a bomb, and the police fired into the crowd. Seven policemen and at least four civilians died. No one established who threw the bomb, but eight anarchists were tried, and the trial was a spectacle of prejudice; four were hanged on 11 November 1887. In 1893 the Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors, declaring the trial unfair, and paid for it with his political life. Haymarket set back the eight-hour movement for a generation.

**1892: Homestead.** — In 1892 the Carnegie Steel Company's works at Homestead, Pennsylvania, became the most famous of the era's defeats. Carnegie was in Scotland; his partner Henry Clay Frick cut the wages of the skilled workers who belonged to the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, built a fence three miles long, and hired 300 armed Pinkerton guards to protect strikebreakers. On 6 July, as the Pinkertons approached by barge on the Monongahela River, a battle broke out; ten men died, most of them strikers or bystanders. The governor sent the state militia, the union was broken, and the mills reopened with non-union labor. The steel industry stayed largely non-union until the 1930s.

**1894: Pullman.** — The Pullman Palace Car Company built a model town south of Chicago where its workers lived in company houses and paid company rents. In 1893, in the depression, the company cut wages by about a quarter but did not cut rents. The workers struck in May 1894 and appealed to the new American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs, which had already won a strike against the Great Northern. The union refused to handle Pullman cars, and the boycott spread across the western railroads. The federal government obtained an injunction against the boycott on the ground that it obstructed the mails and interstate commerce; when the union ignored it, President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over the protest of the Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld. Riots killed a dozen people; Debs was jailed for contempt; and the Supreme Court upheld the injunction in *In re Debs* (1895).

## Populism and the farmers' revolt

**The farmers organize.** — Farmers had long grievances: falling crop prices, freight rates set by railroads they did not control, and credit that was scarce and dear. The Patrons of Husbandry, or Grange, founded in 1867, had built cooperatives and pressed for state regulation; the Farmers' Alliances of the 1880s, stronger in the South and the Plains, went further. Their "sub-treasury" plan proposed that the government build warehouses where farmers could store crops and borrow against them at low interest, and their demand for "free silver" — the unlimited coinage of silver as well as gold — promised to inflate the currency and raise prices. The Alliance in the South tried to build an interracial movement, but the white Democratic parties used fraud and violence against Black voters, and after 1892 many Southern Populists dropped their Black allies to win white votes.

**The Omaha Platform.** — In July 1892 the People's Party, formed from the Alliances, met in Omaha and adopted a platform on the anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. It declared that "the fruits of the toil of millions are boldly stolen to build up colossal fortunes for a few," that the country was dividing into "tramps and millionaires," and that "the railroad corporations will either own the people or the people must own the railroads." It demanded a national currency issued by the government, a graduated income tax, postal savings banks, the free coinage of silver at sixteen to one, government ownership of the railroads and the telegraph, the secret ballot, the direct election of senators, an eight-hour day on government work, and the restriction of "undesirable emigration." The party's candidate, James B. Weaver, won more than a million popular votes and twenty-two electoral votes. Mary Elizabeth Lease, a Kansas orator, told farmers they should "raise less corn and more hell."

**1896 and the Cross of Gold.** — The election of 1896 decided the movement's fate. The Democrats, meeting in Chicago, were captured by the silver wing, and William Jennings Bryan of Nebraska closed the debate with a speech defending free silver: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns; you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The Populists, faced with a choice between Bryan and their own candidate, endorsed Bryan in a fusion that many regretted; the silver issue swallowed their larger program. Bryan campaigned across the country, but the Republican William McKinley, backed by the industrial and financial centers, raised and spent far more money and won the electoral vote 271 to 176. The defeat broke the People's Party, though many of its ideas — the income tax, the direct election of senators, the secret ballot — were later enacted.

## Jim Crow and disenfranchisement

**The end of Reconstruction.** — The withdrawal of federal troops from the South after the disputed election of 1876 — the Compromise of 1877 — left the state governments to white "Redeemer" coalitions that had already begun to undo Reconstruction. The Supreme Court narrowed the constitutional promises of the war years: in the *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) it read the Fourteenth Amendment's "privileges or immunities" clause narrowly; in the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883) it struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875, holding that the amendment forbade state action but not the private discrimination of innkeepers and railroads; and in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) it upheld a Louisiana law requiring separate railway cars. Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote that such laws did not "necessarily imply the inferiority of either race" and that if separation stamped the colored race with a badge of inferiority it was "solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it." Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented: "Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."

**Disenfranchisement.** — The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) forbade denial of the vote on grounds of race, so the Southern states found other grounds. Mississippi's constitution of 1890 imposed a poll tax and a literacy test with an "understanding" clause that let a registrar decide whether an applicant could explain a passage of the constitution — a discretion applied to white men and rarely to Black ones. Louisiana in 1898 added a "grandfather clause," exempting from the tests any man whose father or grandfather had voted before 1867, which meant before Black suffrage. By 1900 Black registration had collapsed — in Louisiana, from about 130,000 in 1896 to some 5,000 in 1904. With the vote went the juries, the offices, and much of the protection of the law.

**Violence and the counter-argument.** — Violence enforced the system. Between 1882 and 1900 more than two thousand Black men and women were lynched in the United States, most of them in the South, and the charge was often a fabricated accusation of assault. Ida B. Wells, a Memphis journalist, investigated the cases and published *Southern Horrors* in 1892, arguing that lynching was a weapon of economic and political terror rather than a response to crime; a mob destroyed her newspaper office, and she moved north. Convict leasing put thousands of Black men, arrested on trivial charges, to work on railroads, mines, and plantations under private guard; sharecropping and the crop-lien system kept tenants in debt. Within the Black community two strategies competed. Booker T. Washington, born into slavery and later head of the Tuskegee Institute, told the Atlanta Cotton States and International Exposition in September 1895: "In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress." He urged Black Southerners to win economic standing through industrial education before pressing for political rights. W. E. B. Du Bois argued in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) that Washington's program asked too much and offered too little, and that the vote and higher education could not wait.

## The closing of the frontier

**The end of the line.** — In 1890 the Census Bureau announced that it could no longer draw a continuous frontier line of settlement. The Homestead Act of 1862 had offered 160 acres to any settler who would improve them for five years, and the railroads advertised their grant lands in Europe and carried buyers west. Land rushes followed: the opening of the Unassigned Lands in Oklahoma on 22 April 1889 drew some 50,000 settlers in a day. The white settlement of the Plains depended on the destruction of the Plains Indians' economy. The buffalo were hunted nearly to extinction; the Dawes Act of 1887 broke communal tribal land into individual allotments, a policy meant to turn Indians into farmers that instead transferred millions of acres to white buyers; and on 29 December 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek in South Dakota, the army's Seventh Cavalry killed some 150 to 300 Lakota, most of them women and children.

**Turner's thesis and its critics.** — In 1893 the young historian Frederick Jackson Turner told the American Historical Association that the frontier had been the decisive force in American life: the encounter with free land, he argued, had produced individualism, democracy, and a distinct national character, and its closing marked the end of the first great era of American history. The essay, "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," became one of the most influential and disputed pieces of American scholarship ever written. Later historians pointed out that Turner's frontier was a line of white settlement that ignored the Indian nations, the Mexican communities of the Southwest, and the women who did much of the work of settlement, and that the West was less a place of free land than a series of conquests over water, minerals, and labor. Patricia Nelson Limerick's *The Legacy of Conquest* (1987) made the case that the West should be studied as a region with its own history, not as the passing of a line.

## The Social Gospel

**A faith for the city.** — The poverty of the industrial city produced a religious response. Washington Gladden, a Congregational minister in Columbus, Ohio, preached in the 1870s and 1880s that the church could not be silent about wages and hours. In New York, Walter Rauschenbusch, a Baptist minister in the Hell's Kitchen district, came to believe that sin was social as well as personal; his *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) argued that the kingdom of God was a condition to be built on earth and that the church must take the side of the poor. The Social Gospel inspired institutional churches with kitchens, gymnasiums, and classes and supported the eight-hour day and the right of workers to organize.

## The ledger

**The balance.** — The age built much that lasted: the modern corporation and the national market, the transcontinental railroad and the telegraph, the public library and the research university, the settlement house and the modern city. It also built the trust and the company town, the poll tax and the chain gang, the slum and the child-labor mill. Real wages for industrial workers rose over the period, though unevenly, and the country became the largest industrial producer in the world. The gains were real, and they were distributed very unequally. For the immigrant steelworker in Homestead, the Black farmer in Georgia, and the seamstress in a New York tenement, the gilded surface and the base metal underneath were the same object seen from different sides.

## Chronology

- **1869** — The first transcontinental railroad is joined at Promontory Summit, Utah; the Knights of Labor are founded.
- **1870** — Standard Oil is incorporated in Ohio; the Fifteenth Amendment is ratified.
- **1873** — Twain and Warner publish *The Gilded Age*; the Panic of 1873 begins a long depression.
- **1877** — The Great Railroad Strike; Reconstruction ends with the Compromise of 1877; *Munn v. Illinois*.
- **1882** — The Standard Oil Trust is formed; the Chinese Exclusion Act.
- **1883** — The Civil Rights Cases strike down the Civil Rights Act of 1875; standard time zones are adopted.
- **1886** — The American Federation of Labor is founded; Haymarket Square; the Wabash case.
- **1887** — The Interstate Commerce Act; the Dawes Act; four anarchists are hanged for Haymarket.
- **1889** — Carnegie's "Wealth" appears; New Jersey's holding-company law; Hull House opens; Oklahoma land rush.
- **1890** — The Census Bureau declares the frontier closed; the Sherman Antitrust Act; Mississippi's disenfranchising constitution; Wounded Knee.
- **1892** — Homestead strike; the Omaha Platform and the founding of the People's Party; Ida B. Wells publishes *Southern Horrors*; Ellis Island opens.
- **1893** — Turner's frontier essay; the Panic of 1893; Altgeld pardons the Haymarket defendants.
- **1894** — Pullman strike and boycott; Coxey's Army; *In re Debs*.
- **1895** — Booker T. Washington's Atlanta address; *United States v. E. C. Knight*.
- **1896** — *Plessy v. Ferguson*; Bryan's "Cross of Gold"; McKinley defeats Bryan; Louisiana's grandfather clause.
- **1898** — The United States acquires an overseas empire in the Spanish-American War; the merger wave begins.
- **1900** — Population reaches 76 million; the United States is the world's leading industrial producer.

## Questions to carry

- The age produced both enormous wealth and enormous poverty. Which of the two was the more important fact about it, and for whom?
- Carnegie called the rich the trustees of their surplus; the Populists called them thieves. What does each claim assume about how wealth is created?
- The Supreme Court said segregation did not imply inferiority. What did the law actually do, and what did it allow others to do?
- The Knights of Labor tried to unite all workers; the American Federation of Labor organized skilled trades only. Which strategy did the times reward, and which did justice require?
- Turner said the frontier made Americans democratic. If he was wrong, what did make the country what it became?
