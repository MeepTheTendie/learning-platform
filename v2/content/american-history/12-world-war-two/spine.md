# The Second World War

**Period:** c. 1941–1945 · **Region:** The United States and the world

**A war the country had not wanted.** — On the morning of Sunday, 7 December 1941, Japanese carrier aircraft attacked the United States Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, in the Territory of Hawaii. In under two hours they sank or damaged eight battleships, destroyed 188 aircraft, and killed 2,403 Americans, 68 of them civilians. The next afternoon President Franklin D. Roosevelt told a joint session of Congress that the date "will live in infamy," and Congress declared war on Japan with a single dissenting vote — Jeannette Rankin of Montana, who had also voted against the war in 1917. Four days later Germany and Italy declared war on the United States, and Congress answered in kind. A nation that had refused to join the League of Nations and had spent the 1930s legislating neutrality was now a belligerent in the largest war ever fought. By the time it ended, some 16 million Americans had served, about 405,000 were dead, the federal budget had grown more than tenfold, and the United States stood as the only great power whose industrial base had not been bombed, with an atomic bomb in hand and armies of occupation in Germany and Japan. How the war transformed American society and America's place in the world is the question this unit carries.

## From neutrality to belligerence

**The isolationist inheritance.** — The generation that fought the Second World War had been raised on the memory of the First. A Senate committee chaired by Gerald P. Nye of North Dakota spent 1934 and 1935 arguing that American entry in 1917 had been engineered by bankers and munitions makers, and the charge, whether fair or not, hardened opinion against another European war. Between 1935 and 1939 Congress passed a series of Neutrality Acts that forbade selling arms to belligerents and lending to them, and that made no distinction between aggressor and victim. Roosevelt signed them and disliked them; he wanted discretion to help Britain and France, and he got only a little. The country's reluctance had a name: isolationism, though non-interventionism is more accurate, since the United States stayed engaged in the Western Hemisphere and the Pacific. The America First Committee, founded in September 1940 and led by the Chicago businessman Robert E. Wood, drew perhaps 800,000 members; its most famous spokesman was the aviator Charles A. Lindbergh, who argued that the war was a European quarrel and that American democracy could survive only if it stayed out. Roosevelt, running for an unprecedented third term in 1940, promised that American boys would not be sent into foreign wars. His opponent, the Republican Wendell Willkie, largely agreed.

**Aid short of war.** — Roosevelt moved the country by steps, each small enough to defend as prudence. In September 1940, after Britain gave the United States leases on naval bases from Newfoundland to Trinidad, he traded fifty overage destroyers for them by executive agreement, without Congress. That same month Congress passed the first peacetime draft in American history, the Selective Training and Service Act, registering men between twenty-one and thirty-five. In a fireside chat on 29 December 1940 Roosevelt told the country that it must become "the great arsenal of democracy," and on 6 January 1941 he named four freedoms worth fighting for: freedom of speech and worship, freedom from want and fear. The plan came in March 1941, when Congress passed the Lend-Lease Act by 260 to 165 in the House and 60 to 31 in the Senate. Lend-Lease let the president sell, lend, lease, or transfer war material to any country whose defense he judged vital to the United States. Over the war it moved about $50 billion in goods, roughly $31 billion to Britain and $11 billion to the Soviet Union, and it converted American industry into the supply line of the Allied coalition before a single American soldier was committed.

**The Atlantic Charter.** — In August 1941 Roosevelt and the British prime minister, Winston Churchill, met aboard warships in Placentia Bay, Newfoundland, and issued a joint statement of aims that became known as the Atlantic Charter. It promised no territorial aggrandizement, the right of peoples to choose their own governments, equal access to trade and raw materials, improved labor standards and social security, freedom from fear and want, and the abandonment of force pending "a wider and permanent system of general security." It was a statement of principles, not a treaty. Its significance was that the president of a still-neutral country had joined the leader of a belligerent in defining what the war was for, and that the definition pointed past the war toward a postwar order. The charter also papered over real disagreements — about the British empire, about colonies, about free trade — that would surface once the fighting stopped.

**The slide into the shooting war.** — By the autumn of 1941 the United States was fighting an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic. In September a German U-boat fired on the destroyer USS *Greer*; Roosevelt ordered the navy to shoot on sight. In October a U-boat sank the destroyer USS *Reuben James*, killing 115 men, the first American warship lost. The country was not neutral in fact, only in law. Then Japan decided the question. Its war in China had bogged down, and its advance into French Indochina in July 1941 brought American retaliation: Roosevelt froze Japanese assets and cut off the oil that supplied most of Japan's fuel. The Japanese high command faced a choice between withdrawing from China and seizing the oil and rubber of Southeast Asia. It chose the second, and planned a strike at the American fleet to keep it out of the way. The attack on Pearl Harbor settled the American debate that diplomacy had not.

## The European war

**The Battle of the Atlantic.** — The first battle the United States had to win was the one for the sea lanes. Germany's submarines, the U-boats, hunted the convoys that carried men and supplies from North America to Britain and the Soviet Union. In 1942 they sank ships faster than the Allies could build them, especially along the American east coast, where cities failed to dim their lights and merchantmen sailed silhouetted against the shore. The crisis eased in 1943 for several reasons: the convoy system with air cover from escort carriers, improved radar and sonar, long-range aircraft that closed the "black pit" in the mid-Atlantic, and the breaking of German naval codes at Bletchley Park in England, which let Allied convoys route around the wolf packs. The Allies also out-built the losses: American yards launched 2,710 Liberty ships, a standardized cargo vessel welded rather than riveted, one of them assembled in four days and fifteen hours in November 1942 as a publicity stunt. By May 1943 the U-boats were being sunk faster than they could be replaced, and Dönitz withdrew them from the North Atlantic. The Battle of the Atlantic was the war's longest campaign and the one on which every other campaign depended.

**The air war over Germany.** — The Western Allies bombed Germany by night and by day. The Royal Air Force's Bomber Command flew at night and aimed at whole cities; the United States Army Air Forces flew by day and claimed to aim at specific factories and rail yards, though smoke and cloud often made precision impossible. The campaign killed an estimated 300,000 to 600,000 German civilians and diverted German aircraft and anti-aircraft guns from the fronts, but whether it shortened the war is disputed. The postwar United States Strategic Bombing Survey found that German war production rose through most of the bombing; the firebombing of Dresden in February 1945, which killed perhaps 25,000 people, became the emblem of the argument.

**North Africa.** — The United States first fought Germany in North Africa. In November 1942, under the command of Dwight D. Eisenhower, an American force landed in Morocco and Algeria — Operation Torch — while the British Eighth Army under Bernard Montgomery attacked the German and Italian armies at El Alamein in Egypt. The Americans were green, and at Kasserine Pass in February 1943 a German counterattack under Erwin Rommel mauled them; Eisenhower replaced the commander and tightened discipline. The two Allied forces squeezed the Axis into Tunisia, and on 13 May 1943 some 275,000 German and Italian soldiers surrendered. It was the first large Axis capitulation and it gave the Allies the southern shore of the Mediterranean.

**Italy.** — From North Africa the Allies crossed to Sicily in July 1943, and Mussolini fell from power on 25 July. Italy signed an armistice in September, but Germany occupied the peninsula and fought on. The campaign up Italy was slow and costly: the landings at Salerno and Anzio, the four battles at Monte Cassino. Rome fell on 4 June 1944, two days before D-Day in France. The campaign tied down German divisions but never produced the quick collapse its planners had hoped for.

**The Eastern Front.** — The war in Europe was decided above all in the east, where Germany and the Soviet Union fought the largest land battles in history. The German invasion of June 1941, Operation Barbarossa, reached the gates of Moscow before winter; the Soviet counteroffensive of December 1941 saved the capital. In 1942 the Germans drove toward the oil of the Caucasus and the city of Stalingrad on the Volga; the battle there, from August 1942 to February 1943, ended with the surrender of the German Sixth Army and roughly 91,000 prisoners. The tank battle at Kursk in July 1943 ended the German offensive in the east for good. The Red Army then advanced westward, and by the time it reached Berlin in April 1945 it had destroyed the greater part of the German army. Soviet losses were staggering — perhaps 27 million dead, soldiers and civilians together — and the memory of them shaped Soviet demands in the peace.

**D-Day and the defeat of Germany.** — The Western Allied invasion of France, Operation Overlord, was the largest amphibious operation ever attempted. On 6 June 1944, under Eisenhower's supreme command, some 5,000 vessels and 11,000 aircraft put about 156,000 troops ashore on five beaches in Normandy, two American, two British, one Canadian. The Americans at Omaha Beach took the worst of it, with perhaps 2,000 casualties in a morning. The beachhead held. In July the Americans broke out at Saint-Lô in Operation Cobra, and Paris was liberated on 25 August. That winter the Germans counterattacked in the Ardennes — the Battle of the Bulge, fought from December 1944 to January 1945 — the largest battle in American history, with some 610,000 Americans engaged and about 19,000 killed. It failed. In March 1945 the Allies crossed the Rhine, and in April American and Soviet forces met on the Elbe. Adolf Hitler killed himself in his Berlin bunker on 30 April, and Germany surrendered on 8 May 1945, V-E Day.

## The Pacific war

**Midway and the turn.** — In the Pacific the United States fought a naval war over an ocean, and it began badly. The Japanese took Guam, Wake, Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore, and the Philippines, where the American and Filipino garrison held out on the Bataan peninsula until April 1942 and then endured the Bataan Death March, in which thousands died. The turn came at the Battle of Midway, 4–7 June 1942, where American carriers, forewarned by code-breaking, sank four Japanese fleet carriers — *Akagi*, *Kaga*, *Soryu*, and *Hiryu* — against the loss of the carrier *Yorktown*. Japan's trained carrier aviators were killed in a single day, and the Japanese navy never recovered the offensive. Midway ended the string of Japanese victories and made an American advance possible.

**Island hopping.** — The American strategy in the Pacific, devised by the navy and carried out under Douglas MacArthur in the southwest and Chester Nimitz in the central Pacific, was not to retake every island but to seize the ones that mattered — airfields and anchorages — and bypass the rest, leaving their garrisons to wither. The Marines and soldiers who did the seizing fought some of the war's worst battles. Tarawa in November 1943 cost about 1,000 American dead in three days; Peleliu in 1944, expected to take days, took two months; Iwo Jima, taken in February and March 1945, cost nearly 7,000 American dead and produced the famous photograph of Marines raising the flag on Mount Suribachi. Okinawa, invaded on 1 April 1945, was the largest and bloodiest: about 12,000 Americans killed, more than 100,000 Japanese dead, and perhaps 100,000 Okinawan civilians, many of them driven to suicide or killed in the crossfire. Japanese pilots flew kamikaze missions, deliberately crashing bomb-laden aircraft into ships; the navy suffered more casualties at Okinawa than in any other battle. In the Philippines, MacArthur waded ashore at Leyte in October 1944, and the Battle of Leyte Gulf, the largest naval battle in history, destroyed the Japanese fleet as a fighting force. By the summer of 1945 the United States was bombing Japanese cities almost at will. The firebombing of Tokyo on the night of 9–10 March 1945 killed about 100,000 people and burned sixteen square miles, the deadliest single air raid of the war.

## The home front

**The arsenal.** — The war ended the Great Depression by the simple expedient of spending more money than anyone had imagined. Federal spending rose from about $9 billion in 1939 to nearly $100 billion in 1945; the gross national product rose from about $91 billion to more than $200 billion. The War Production Board, created in January 1942 and headed by the Sears executive Donald Nelson, directed raw materials and set priorities. The War Manpower Commission moved workers, the Office of Price Administration rationed sugar, coffee, meat, gasoline, and shoes and imposed price controls, and the National War Labor Board arbitrated wage disputes in exchange for no-strike pledges. American factories turned out about 300,000 aircraft, 88,000 tanks, 2.4 million trucks, and 41 billion rounds of small-arms ammunition. Henry Ford's Willow Run plant near Detroit, built to make B-24 bombers, reached a peak of one bomber an hour in 1944 after a rocky start. The federal government itself became the country's largest source of jobs, and to pay for the war it reached deep into ordinary pockets: the income tax, once a levy on the rich, became a mass tax, and in 1943 Congress introduced payroll withholding, so that the government took its share before the worker saw the pay.

**Rosie the Riveter.** — With men in uniform, women entered the paid workforce in numbers no one had expected. About 6 million women took war jobs, and the share of women in the civilian labor force rose from roughly 27 percent in 1940 to about 37 percent in 1945. In the aircraft industry alone, women went from a small fraction to some 310,000 workers, about 65 percent of the total. The government and industry advertised the change. The song "Rosie the Riveter" appeared in 1942; the artist Norman Rockwell put a muscular Rosie on the cover of the *Saturday Evening Post* in May 1943; and a Westinghouse poster showed a woman in a red bandana flexing her arm under the words "We Can Do It!" — an image later attached to Rosie, though it was made for internal factory morale and was not widely seen during the war. Women were paid less than men for the same work, were often excluded from unions and from the best jobs, and were pushed out when the men returned. The war nonetheless made the case, which the peace did not immediately accept, that women could do industrial work.

**The Double V and the Tuskegee Airmen.** — Black Americans pressed the war into service for their own claims. In 1941 the labor leader A. Philip Randolph threatened a mass march on Washington unless the defense industries hired Black workers; Roosevelt answered on 25 June 1941 with Executive Order 8802, which barred discrimination in defense employment and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee. After Pearl Harbor the *Pittsburgh Courier*, a Black newspaper, launched the Double V campaign — victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home — after a reader named James G. Thompson wrote to ask what the war meant for him. About 1.2 million Black Americans served, most in segregated units and most in labor and service roles. The Tuskegee Airmen, trained at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, flew with the 99th Pursuit Squadron and the 332nd Fighter Group; the 332nd earned a reputation for protecting bombers and for refusing to lose them to enemy fighters. The 761st Tank Battalion fought in Europe, and the Navy commissioned its first Black officers and Doris Miller, a mess attendant at Pearl Harbor, became the first Black sailor awarded the Navy Cross. Segregation held. At Port Chicago in California in July 1944, an explosion killed 320 men, most of them Black; when fifty sailors refused to return to loading munitions under the same officers, they were tried for mutiny and convicted. The war's rhetoric of freedom made the contradiction harder to defend, but the military remained segregated until 1948.

**Japanese American internment.** — The war's sharpest home-front injustice fell on Japanese Americans. On 19 February 1942 Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, which authorized the secretary of war and military commanders to designate areas from which "any or all persons may be excluded." The order named no ethnic group, but it was applied almost entirely to people of Japanese descent on the West Coast. Under Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, some 120,000 men, women, and children — nearly 70,000 of them American citizens — were forced from their homes on short notice and confined in ten camps in remote parts of California, Arizona, Utah, Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado, and Arkansas. They lost homes, farms, businesses, and savings; the government estimated the property loss in the billions of dollars. No Japanese American was convicted of espionage or sabotage. The Supreme Court upheld the policy in *Korematsu v. United States* in December 1944, with Justice Hugo Black writing for the majority; Justice Robert Jackson dissented, warning that the decision validated a racial classification and "lies about like a loaded weapon" for the future, and Justice Frank Murphy called it a "legalization of racism." Japanese Americans nonetheless served, and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, fighting in Italy and France, became the most decorated unit in American military history for its size. The convictions of Fred Korematsu and others were later overturned, and in 1988 Congress apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving person who had been confined.

**The Manhattan Project.** — The bomb had its origins in a letter. In August 1939 the physicist Leo Szilard, with Albert Einstein's signature, warned Roosevelt that Germany might build a nuclear weapon and urged American research. The work began slowly and accelerated after Pearl Harbor under the Manhattan Engineer District, directed by Brigadier General Leslie Groves with the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer leading the laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico. The project employed about 130,000 people at sites including Oak Ridge, Tennessee, where uranium was enriched, and Hanford, Washington, where plutonium was made, at a cost of roughly $2 billion. On 16 July 1945 the first nuclear device was detonated at the Trinity site in the New Mexico desert. The interim committee advising the president recommended using it against Japan without warning, and on 26 July the Potsdam Declaration demanded Japan's unconditional surrender, threatening "prompt and utter destruction" but not naming the bomb. Japan did not reply. On 6 August a uranium bomb, "Little Boy," destroyed Hiroshima, killing perhaps 70,000 people at once and tens of thousands more from radiation and burns. On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, and its armies invaded Manchuria the next day; on 9 August a plutonium bomb, "Fat Man," destroyed Nagasaki. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August and signed the formal instrument aboard the battleship USS *Missouri* on 2 September 1945. Whether the bombs were necessary is one of the most contested questions in American history. Richard B. Frank argues that Japan's leaders were not close to surrender and that the bombs, with Soviet entry, ended the war before an invasion of Kyushu that would have killed far more; Gar Alperovitz argues that Japan was already seeking terms and that the bomb was used in part to end the war before the Soviet Union could claim a share of the occupation. The documents do not settle it.

## The war's consequences

**A new place in the world.** — The war left the United States in a position no nation had held before. Its economy was about half the world's industrial output; its currency, the dollar, became the anchor of the postwar financial system agreed at Bretton Woods in July 1944, which created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. Its army and navy were deployed from Germany to Japan, and it held a monopoly on atomic weapons until 1949. It had taken the lead in founding the United Nations, whose charter was signed at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, and it had committed itself, through the Atlantic Charter and the UN, to a role in the world that the Senate's rejection of the League had foreclosed a generation earlier. That commitment was not simply idealistic: the war taught that oceans were no longer a shield, and the peace was organized to keep the next war far from American shores.

**The reckoning at home.** — At home the war changed work, family, and geography. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 — the GI Bill — offered returning veterans college tuition, low-interest home and business loans, and unemployment pay, and it helped build the postwar suburbs and the largest expansion of higher education in the country's history. The marriage and birth rates rose, beginning the baby boom. Millions of Americans moved to war plants in California, Michigan, and the South, and the Black migration out of the South accelerated. The war also left unresolved what it had exposed: the contradiction between fighting racism abroad and practicing it at home, the place of women in the workforce, and the power of a federal government grown used to managing the economy. It made them unavoidable.

## Questions to carry

- The United States debated intervention for two years and entered the war only after an attack. How much of the outcome was decided by that delay?
- Lend-Lease made the country the arsenal of democracy before it was a belligerent. Did aid short of war make the fighting more likely, or merely make it winnable?
- Executive Order 9066 named no ethnic group and was applied to one. What does the gap between the order's language and its use reveal about how law and prejudice work together?
- The Tuskegee Airmen and the 442nd fought for a country that segregated them. What did their service change, and what did it not?
- Frank and Alperovitz reach opposite conclusions from the same documents about the atomic bomb. Which evidence would settle the question, and does it exist?
