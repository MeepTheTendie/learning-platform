# The conservative turn and after

**Period:** c. 1975 to the present · **Region:** The United States

**The end of the long boom.** — Between 1945 and 1970 the American economy grew at about 3.8 percent a year, wages generally rose with productivity, and roughly a third of non-farm workers belonged to a union. The settlement rested on fixed exchange rates agreed at Bretton Woods in 1944, on cheap energy, and on the unchallenged dominance of American manufacturing. All three weakened at once. On 15 August 1971 President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold, ending Bretton Woods. In October 1973, after the Yom Kippur War, the Arab members of OPEC embargoed oil to the United States, and the price of a barrel rose from about $3 to about $12. Prices and joblessness then climbed together — a combination the British politician Iain Macleod had named "stagflation," and one the standard Keynesian remedies of the day could not fix. Consumer prices rose 12.3 percent in 1974 and about 13.5 percent in 1980; unemployment reached 9 percent in 1975 and 10.8 percent in late 1982. The damage fell unevenly. In Youngstown, Ohio, the Youngstown Sheet & Tube Company announced on 19 September 1977 that it would close its Campbell Works, putting about 5,000 people out of work; the "Black Monday" that followed became shorthand for the deindustrialization of the Great Lakes. The economy's center of gravity moved south and west — to Houston, Phoenix, and Atlanta — and political power moved with it as the census reapportioned House seats. In California, voters passed Proposition 13 in June 1978, cutting property taxes by about 57 percent and setting off a national tax revolt. President Jimmy Carter called the mood a "crisis of confidence" in a speech of 15 July 1979 and asked Americans to conserve energy; the word "malaise" now attached to the speech was never his. To break inflation, Carter's appointee Paul Volcker, chairman of the Federal Reserve, raised the federal funds rate to about 20 percent in 1981 — a cure that deepened the recession before it worked.

**The New Right and the religious right.** — The movement that answered the crisis had been assembling for years. Barry Goldwater's landslide defeat in 1964 left a network of conservative activists — Young Americans for Freedom, the magazine *National Review*, and direct-mail lists — and it was in that campaign that Ronald Reagan delivered "A Time for Choosing," the speech that made him a national figure. Richard Nixon's "southern strategy" courted white voters alienated by civil rights, and his 1972 landslide showed the reach of the new coalition. Business organized too: in 1971 Lewis Powell, soon to join the Supreme Court, sent the United States Chamber of Commerce a memorandum urging corporate America into politics, and the Heritage Foundation (1973) and later the American Legislative Exchange Council turned that advice into institutions. Two decisions of the 1970s gave the coalition its most reliable issues. *Roe v. Wade* (22 January 1973) struck down most state bans on abortion. The proposed Equal Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1972, was fought to a standstill by Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA, which argued that the amendment would draft women into combat and erase protections; it fell three states short of ratification when its extended deadline expired in 1982. In 1978 the Internal Revenue Service moved to deny tax exemptions to private schools that discriminated by race, a step that alarmed segregated Christian academies and helped politicize evangelical congregations. In 1979 the televangelist Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority, and by 1980 white evangelicals were voting Republican in numbers that had been unthinkable a decade earlier. The movement was not only Southern or only religious; it drew on suburban homeowners, small-business owners, and the tax revolt as much as on the pulpit.

**The Reagan revolution.** — In November 1980 Reagan defeated Carter with 50.7 percent of the popular vote and 489 electoral votes to Carter's 49; the independent John Anderson took 6.6 percent, and Republicans captured the Senate for the first time since 1954. Reagan took office on 20 January 1981, the day the Iranian government released the American hostages held since November 1979. Two events set the tone of his first year. On 30 March 1981 he was shot by John Hinckley Jr. and nearly killed, recovering within weeks. In August he fired 11,345 striking air-traffic controllers who had defied a federal ban on public-sector strikes, decertified their union, and signaled that the era of accommodation with organized labor was over. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut income taxes by 25 percent over three years and reduced the top marginal rate from 70 to 50 percent; the Tax Reform Act of 1986 brought it down to 28 percent. Supply-side economists, who argued that cutting taxes would so stimulate growth that revenues would rise, supplied the theory; the result was a large increase in the deficit instead. Defense spending rose by more than a trillion dollars over five years, and the national debt roughly tripled, from about $900 billion in 1980 to about $2.8 trillion in 1988. The recession of 1981–82 pushed unemployment to 10.8 percent, but recovery came by 1983 and Reagan won a second landslide in 1984. In foreign policy he called the Soviet Union an "evil empire" in March 1983 and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, a missile-defense program that critics called "Star Wars." His second term was clouded by the Iran-Contra affair, in which officials secretly sold arms to Iran and diverted the proceeds to Nicaraguan rebels, or Contras; the Tower Commission reported in 1987, and several officials were convicted, though most convictions were later overturned on appeal.

**The end of the Cold War.** — Reagan's military buildup coincided with a change in Moscow. Mikhail Gorbachev became general secretary of the Communist Party in 1985 and pursued *glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (economic restructuring). The two leaders met at Geneva in 1985 and at Reykjavík in 1986, where they nearly agreed to eliminate all nuclear weapons; the breakthrough came with the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty of 8 December 1987, the first agreement to scrap an entire class of missiles. In a speech at the Brandenburg Gate on 12 June 1987 Reagan demanded, "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall." The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, and the Soviet Union dissolved on 26 December 1991. How much credit Reagan's arms buildup deserves is contested. The historian John Lewis Gaddis argues in *The Cold War: A New History* (2005) that containment, pressure, and the Western alliance's resolve were decisive; others emphasize the Soviet economy's internal exhaustion, the cost of the arms race, and Gorbachev's own choices, and note that the collapse was peaceful because Moscow chose not to intervene. The debate matters because both parties claimed the outcome as vindication of their own foreign policy.

**The culture wars.** — As the Cold War wound down, political conflict turned inward. The historian James Davison Hunter gave the conflict a name in *Culture Wars: The Struggle to Define America* (1991), describing a division that cut across denominations and parties and turned on family, sexuality, education, and the meaning of the nation's past. The AIDS epidemic made the stakes concrete: the Centers for Disease Control reported the first cases on 5 June 1981, and by the end of the decade more than 100,000 Americans had been diagnosed; Reagan did not address the epidemic publicly until 1985, and activists in ACT UP accused his administration of neglect. The 1988 presidential campaign produced the "Willie Horton" advertisement, which attacked Governor Michael Dukakis over a furloughed prisoner who committed rape, and the charge became a durable argument about crime and race. In 1991 the Senate confirmed Clarence Thomas to the Supreme Court after Anita Hill, a law professor who had worked for him, testified that he had sexually harassed her; Thomas called the hearings a "high-tech lynching." At the 1992 Republican convention Pat Buchanan declared that a "religious war" was under way "for the soul of America," a phrase that entered the language as the culture war. Gun control, school prayer, and the Defense of Marriage Act of 1996 joined abortion on the list of issues that sorted the parties by culture as well as class. The conflict was rhetorical as much as legislative: it named a realignment in which the parties increasingly stood for opposed visions of family and faith.

**The Clinton years and the boom.** — The Democrat Bill Clinton won the presidency in 1992 with 43 percent of the vote against George H. W. Bush's 37.5 percent, while the independent Ross Perot took 18.9 percent — the strongest third-party showing since 1912. Clinton governed as a "New Democrat," promising to end welfare "as we know it" and signing the North American Free Trade Agreement in 1993. In 1994 Republicans, led by Newt Gingrich and a ten-point "Contract with America," won control of the House for the first time in forty years and took the Senate as well. The two branches collided in 1995–96, when a budget standoff shut down the federal government twice. Clinton signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act in August 1996, replacing the open-ended welfare entitlement with time-limited, work-linked aid, and he won re-election that November over Bob Dole. The economy then boomed: the budget, in deficit since 1969, showed a surplus of $69 billion in 1998, and the NASDAQ stock index, driven by internet companies, peaked at 5,048 on 10 March 2000 before collapsing. Clinton's second term was consumed by scandal. In December 1998 the House impeached him for perjury and obstruction over his affair with a White House intern, Monica Lewinsky; the Senate acquitted him in February 1999. The episode hardened the conviction on each side that the other was lawless — a template for the next two decades.

**The contested election of 2000.** — The election of 2000 was decided by 537 votes in Florida and by the Supreme Court. Al Gore, Clinton's vice president, won the national popular vote by 543,895 ballots, but the electoral college turned on Florida, where a machine recount left George W. Bush ahead by a few hundred votes. The state's punch-card ballots produced "hanging chads" and "pregnant chads" — incompletely punched holes that machines read differently on different passes — and Palm Beach County's "butterfly ballot" led some voters to mark the wrong candidate. For thirty-six days county canvassing boards recounted by hand while courts argued about deadlines. On 12 December 2000 the Supreme Court, in *Bush v. Gore*, divided 5–4 and stopped the recount, holding that no consistent standard could be applied in the time left; the five justices in the majority said the decision was limited to the case. Bush carried Florida and the presidency, 271 electoral votes to 266. Gore conceded the next evening. The Court's intervention, and the fact that the popular-vote winner lost, damaged confidence in the system on both sides.

**September 11 and the wars.** — On the morning of 11 September 2001 nineteen hijackers affiliated with al Qaeda seized four airliners; two struck the World Trade Center in New York, one hit the Pentagon, and one, after passengers fought back, crashed in a field in Pennsylvania. The attacks killed 2,977 people. On 20 September President George W. Bush addressed a joint session of Congress, identified al Qaeda and its leader Osama bin Laden, demanded that the Taliban government of Afghanistan surrender them, and announced a "war on terror" that would be global in reach and long in duration. Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act on 26 October 2001, expanding surveillance and search powers: Section 215 allowed the Federal Bureau of Investigation to obtain "any tangible things" — business records, library records, and more — with a secret order from a special court, and Section 213 authorized "delayed notice" searches, soon called "sneak and peek" warrants. The war in Afghanistan began on 7 October 2001 and quickly toppled the Taliban, though bin Laden escaped. In 2002 Bush named an "axis of evil" of Iraq, Iran, and North Korea, and in March 2003 the United States invaded Iraq, arguing that Saddam Hussein held weapons of mass destruction; no such stockpiles were found. A banner reading "Mission Accomplished" framed Bush's speech aboard the USS *Abraham Lincoln* on 1 May 2003, but an insurgency followed, and photographs of prisoner abuse at Abu Ghraib appeared in 2004. The war in Iraq killed more than 4,400 American service members; the Afghan war, by 2021, more than 2,400. Guantánamo Bay, opened in January 2002, held detainees for years without trial. The wars, and the surveillance state built alongside them, became the most contested legacies of the decade.

**The financial crisis and the Great Recession.** — The boom that followed the 2001 recession rested heavily on housing. Low interest rates and loose lending standards drew borrowers into subprime mortgages — loans with low initial "teaser" rates that reset sharply higher — and Wall Street bundled those loans into securities sold around the world. When home prices stopped rising in 2006 and 2007, the securities lost value. In March 2008 the investment bank Bear Stearns was rescued by JPMorgan Chase with federal backing; in September the government took over the mortgage giants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and on 15 September 2008 Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy — the largest in American history — while the insurer American International Group was rescued the next day. Credit froze. In October Congress passed the Troubled Asset Relief Program, or TARP, authorizing $700 billion to buy troubled assets, and the Federal Reserve and Treasury poured money into the banks. The recession that followed was the deepest since the 1930s: unemployment reached 10 percent in October 2009, roughly 8.7 million jobs disappeared, and millions of families lost their homes to foreclosure. The automakers General Motors and Chrysler were rescued in 2009. In 2010 Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act, which imposed new capital and consumer-protection rules, though critics argued it left the largest banks "too big to fail."

**Obama, the Tea Party, and the backlash.** — The crisis carried Barack Obama, a first-term senator from Illinois, into the presidency in November 2008; he won 52.9 percent of the popular vote and 365 electoral votes and became the first African American president. His signature domestic achievement, the Affordable Care Act, signed on 23 March 2010, required most Americans to carry health insurance or pay a penalty — the "individual mandate" — and expanded Medicaid and insurance subsidies. The law, and the bank bailouts before it, provoked a protest movement that took the name Tea Party, after the Boston tax revolt of 1773; its rallies demanded lower taxes and smaller government, and its energy helped Republicans win 63 House seats in the 2010 midterms. In *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* (21 January 2010) the Supreme Court held that corporations and unions could spend unlimited sums on independent political speech, and the money that followed reshaped campaigns. A standoff over the debt ceiling in 2011 led Standard & Poor's to downgrade the federal government's credit rating for the first time. In 2012 the Court upheld the health law's mandate as a tax in *NFIB v. Sebelius*, and in 2013 *Shelby County v. Holder* struck down the formula that had required certain states, mostly in the South, to obtain federal approval before changing voting rules. The backlash was not only about policy. Obama's presidency accelerated a demographic and cultural shift that its opponents experienced as displacement, and the anger ran in both directions. In 2016 Donald Trump, a businessman and television personality, won the presidency on a platform of immigration restriction and economic nationalism; he lost the popular vote by nearly 2.9 million but won 306 electoral votes. In 2020, amid the COVID-19 pandemic, he lost to Joe Biden, who won 306 electoral votes and more than seven million more popular votes; Trump refused to accept the result, and on 6 January 2021 a crowd of his supporters stormed the Capitol as Congress certified the count. In 2022 the Supreme Court's *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* overturned *Roe v. Wade*, returning abortion law to the states.

**The courts and the law.** — The Supreme Court became a central arena of the realignment because its members serve for life and because the parties increasingly chose justices by ideological reliability. The modern fight began in 1987, when the Senate rejected Robert Bork, whose originalist jurisprudence — the view that the Constitution should be read according to its original public meaning — was seen as too extreme; Anthony Kennedy was confirmed instead. The 1991 Thomas hearings made confirmation a televised cultural battle. In 2016 the Republican Senate majority refused to hold a hearing for Merrick Garland, Obama's nominee, in an election year; after Trump's victory, Neil Gorsuch was confirmed in 2017 and the Senate abolished the filibuster for Supreme Court nominees. Brett Kavanaugh was confirmed in 2018 after a contested hearing, and Amy Coney Barrett in 2020, days before the election. The decisions tracked the change: *Bush v. Gore* (2000); *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008), recognizing an individual right to keep and bear arms; *Citizens United* (2010); *Shelby County* (2013); *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015), requiring states to license and recognize same-sex marriages; and *Dobbs* (2022). Each decision was contested not only for its holding but for the authority of the Court itself, which by the 2020s commanded less public confidence than at any point in decades.

**Polarization and the present.** — The realignment that began in the 1970s produced a politics more sorted and more evenly divided than at any time in the modern era. Southern white voters moved from the Democratic to the Republican coalition; the Northeast and the Pacific Coast moved the other way; the two parties became more internally homogeneous and further apart. The media fragmented along the same lines — from three networks and a handful of newspapers to cable news, talk radio, and then the internet and social media, which rewarded attention and anger. Trust in government, above 70 percent in the mid-1960s, fell below 20 percent for much of the 2010s. Turnout, which had slumped, rose again in 2018, 2020, and 2022. Demographic change — a rising share of Americans born abroad or born to immigrants, and a shrinking white majority — was both a cause and a subject of the conflict. Whether the conservative turn was a lasting realignment, a long oscillation, or a temporary reaction is still argued; what is not in dispute is that the political landscape of 1975 no longer describes the country. The organizing question — how did American politics and society realign after the 1960s? — has no single answer, only a set of connected ones: economic crisis, movement organizing, judicial appointment, cultural conflict, and the changing face of the electorate.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the economic crisis of the 1970s discredit the New Deal order, and what took its place?
- Was the conservative turn a grassroots movement, a reaction to crisis, or both — and what evidence would settle it?
- How did the Cold War end, and who deserves credit: American pressure, Soviet decline, or Gorbachev's choices?
- What did the war on terror change about the relationship between the federal government and individual rights?
- How did the realignment after the 1960s reshape both parties, and what about American politics stayed the same?
