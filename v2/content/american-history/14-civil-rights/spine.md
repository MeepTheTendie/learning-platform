# The Civil Rights Movement

**Period:** c. 1954–1968 · **Region:** The United States, especially the South

**The decision.** — On 17 May 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, a unanimous Supreme Court held that racial segregation in public schools violated the equal protection clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote, overruling the "separate but equal" doctrine the Court had endorsed in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896. The decision was the work of a legal campaign built over more than two decades, and it opened a struggle that ran another fourteen years. It also left the central question open: the Court did not say when or how desegregation had to happen. What the movement achieved in law, and what it did not achieve in fact, is the question that runs through the period.

## The legal strategy and Brown

**Houston's plan.** — The campaign began at Howard University Law School, where Charles Hamilton Houston, its dean from 1929, set out to train a cadre of black lawyers who could use the courts against segregation. His student and successor at the NAACP, Thurgood Marshall, carried the strategy forward. Their approach, laid out in the Margold Report of 1931, was to attack segregation where it was weakest: not the segregated elementary schools protected by decades of precedent, but the graduate and professional schools where no separate black institution could plausibly be called equal. The NAACP won Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada in 1938, which barred sending a black applicant out of state in place of admitting him, and Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents in 1950, which forced black students into white graduate programs. Each case narrowed the room in which "separate but equal" could stand.

**Five cases, one question.** — By 1952 the NAACP Legal Defense Fund had five cases before the Court, consolidated under the name of Oliver Brown, a Topeka welder and father of a third-grader. They came from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and the District of Columbia. The Kansas case was listed first because it was the least threatening to Southern opinion, because Topeka's schools were equal in buildings and teachers, which forced the Court to decide whether segregation itself was lawful. The Delaware case was the only one the plaintiffs had won below. The Court heard argument in December 1952, could not agree, and ordered reargument on the original meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment.

**The opinion.** — Warren, appointed chief justice in 1953, worked to make the decision unanimous. He conceded that the history of the amendment's adoption was "at best, inconclusive" on the question of schools. Instead he rested the ruling on the place education had come to hold in American life and on the effects of segregation on children. "To separate them from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race," he wrote, "generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone." The opinion cited social-science work, including the doll studies of Kenneth and Mamie Clark, in a footnote; critics then and since have argued that the Court leaned on contested psychology rather than on the constitutional text. Brown II, decided on 31 May 1955, sent the cases back to the district courts and asked for desegregation "with all deliberate speed" — a phrase elastic enough to mean years of delay.

**The Southern answer.** — On 12 March 1956, nineteen senators and seventy-seven representatives, all from states of the former Confederacy, signed the Declaration of Constitutional Principles, soon called the Southern Manifesto. It called Brown "a clear abuse of judicial power," denied that the Fourteenth Amendment had ever been meant to touch the schools, and pledged "all lawful means" to reverse the decision. Virginia's governor, Thomas Stanley, called for "massive resistance"; Prince Edward County shut its public schools from 1959 to 1964, sending white children to private academies. In Cooper v. Aaron (1958) the Court held that state officials were bound by Brown, but a decade after the ruling barely one black child in a hundred in the Deep South sat in a classroom with white children.

## Emmett Till

**The murder.** — In August 1955 Emmett Till, a fourteen-year-old from Chicago visiting relatives in the Mississippi Delta, was accused of whistling at a white woman, Carolyn Bryant, at a store in Money. On 28 August, Roy Bryant, her husband, and his half-brother J. W. Milam took Till from his great-uncle's house, beat him, shot him, and sank his body in the Tallahatchie River with a cotton-gin fan wired to his neck. His mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, insisted on an open-casket funeral in Chicago and allowed photographs; Jet magazine published them, and the images traveled far beyond the Delta. In September an all-white jury acquitted Bryant and Milam after about an hour of deliberation. The two men confessed to the killing in a paid interview in Look magazine the next year, beyond reach of a second trial under the double-jeopardy rule.

**Why it mattered.** — The case showed how completely the protection of black life depended on local courts and local juries, and how little the federal government could or would do when a state chose not to prosecute. There was no federal anti-lynching statute; the crime was left to Mississippi. Till's killing preceded the Montgomery bus boycott by three months and became, for many young people, the moment the movement became personal. Rosa Parks said she thought of Till when she refused her seat.

## Montgomery and King

**The boycott.** — On 1 December 1955, Rosa Parks, a seamstress and secretary of the Montgomery NAACP, refused to give up her seat on a city bus. E. D. Nixon, a Pullman porter and local NAACP leader, and Jo Ann Robinson of the Women's Political Council helped turn the arrest into a boycott, which began on 5 December. That evening the Montgomery Improvement Association elected as its president a twenty-six-year-old Baptist minister new to the city, Martin Luther King Jr. The boycott demanded courteous treatment, first-come seating, and the hiring of black drivers. It lasted 381 days. A carpool of hundreds of vehicles carried workers to their jobs; King's home was bombed in January 1956, and the organizers were convicted under an anti-boycott law. On 5 June 1956 a three-judge federal court held in Browder v. Gayle that Alabama's bus segregation was unconstitutional; the Supreme Court affirmed on 13 November, and the boycott ended on 20 December 1956.

**King and the method.** — Montgomery made King a national figure and gave the movement a method: nonviolent direct action, drawn from Thoreau, from Gandhi's campaigns in India, from the Social Gospel of the black church, and from the example of labor sit-down strikes. King borrowed from the abolitionist Theodore Parker the phrase that "the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice." He worked closely with Bayard Rustin and Stanley Levison, who brought organizing experience and a wider political network. In January 1957, with Ralph Abernathy and other ministers, he founded the Southern Christian Leadership Conference to spread the campaign across the South. His prominence was contested from the start; older organizers such as Nixon felt the newcomer had been handed credit they had earned.

## Massive resistance and Little Rock

**The Nine at Central High.** — In September 1957, nine black students were to enter Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, under a court-ordered desegregation plan. Governor Orval Faubus called out the National Guard to block them, and on 4 September Elizabeth Eckford walked alone through a jeering crowd. After a meeting with President Dwight Eisenhower failed to move Faubus, Eisenhower federalized the Arkansas Guard on 24 September and sent about a thousand soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division to escort the students to class. The "Little Rock Nine" attended under army protection for the year, but the following year Faubus closed all four of the city's high schools, and they stayed shut for a year. The episode established two things: that a president could enforce a federal court order with troops, and that a governor could resist for a long time by other means.

## The sit-ins and SNCC

**Greensboro, 1 February 1960.** — Four freshmen at North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College — Ezell Blair Jr., David Richmond, Franklin McCain, and Joseph McNeil — sat down at the whites-only lunch counter of a Woolworth's in Greensboro and asked to be served. They came back the next day with more students. Within weeks the sit-ins had spread to more than fifty cities in thirteen states, and lunch counters, department stores, and libraries became sites of a new, youth-led protest. In Nashville, students trained by the seminarian James Lawson, among them Diane Nash and John Lewis, organized a disciplined campaign that drew on the same nonviolent method. The sit-ins applied economic pressure that downtown merchants could measure in lost sales.

**SNCC.** — In April 1960, at Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, Ella Baker convened the student leaders. She urged them to form their own organization rather than a youth wing of the SCLC, and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee was born. SNCC adopted "jail, no bail," refusing to pay fines that would fund the segregated system, and sent organizers into the deepest parts of the South to work on voter registration. Its first chairman was Marion Barry; later chairmen included Charles McDew and, in 1963, John Lewis. The students' impatience with the older organizations' caution would shape the rest of the decade.

## The Freedom Rides

**Testing the terminals.** — In 1960 the Supreme Court held in Boynton v. Virginia that segregation in interstate bus terminals was unlawful. In May 1961 the Congress of Racial Equality, led by James Farmer, sent two buses from Washington, D.C., through the Deep South to test whether the ruling was enforced. In Anniston, Alabama, a mob firebombed one bus and beat the riders who escaped; in Birmingham riders were attacked while police arrived late; in Montgomery, John Lewis and the white rider Jim Zwerg were beaten in the street. Attorney General Robert Kennedy, who had hoped to keep the issue out of the headlines, eventually petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission, which on 22 September 1961 ordered an end to segregation in interstate terminal facilities, effective 1 November. The rides showed that a court ruling meant little without federal enforcement, and they forced the Kennedy administration to act.

## Birmingham and the Children's Crusade

**Project C.** — In the spring of 1963 the SCLC and the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights, led by the Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth, opened a campaign in Birmingham, which King called the most segregated large city in the South. Marches and sit-ins met with arrests; Public Safety Commissioner Eugene "Bull" Connor used mass detention to break the movement. King was arrested on 12 April, and on 16 April, from his cell, he wrote the "Letter from Birmingham Jail," answering white clergymen who had urged patience: "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere." The campaign stalled as adult marchers ran short and bail money drained away. King agreed to a plan that used schoolchildren. On 2 May, hundreds of children marched and were arrested; on 3 May, Connor's police turned fire hoses and dogs on them, and the photographs were printed around the country.

**The settlement and the aftermath.** — On 10 May, city leaders agreed to desegregate downtown facilities and to begin hiring black workers, though enforcement lagged and the Ku Klux Klan bombed the Gaston Motel, where King had stayed. On 11 June, President John Kennedy went on television to call civil rights a "moral issue" and to promise a bill. That night, Medgar Evers, the NAACP field secretary in Mississippi, was shot dead in his driveway in Jackson. On 15 September 1963, a bomb at the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham killed four girls: Addie Mae Collins, Denise McNair, Carole Robertson, and Cynthia Wesley.

## The March on Washington and the Civil Rights Act

**The march.** — On 28 August 1963, some 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. A. Philip Randolph had proposed such a march in 1941 and called it off when Roosevelt banned discrimination in defense industries; Bayard Rustin organized the logistics this time. The demands went beyond integration to jobs and a minimum wage. John Lewis's prepared speech, which criticized the Kennedy bill as too weak, was edited under pressure from older leaders. King's closing address, which left his written text when Mahalia Jackson called out to him, gave the day its phrase: "I have a dream."

**The Act.** — Kennedy was assassinated on 22 November 1963, and President Lyndon Johnson made the bill his own. The House passed it in February 1964; in the Senate, Southern Democrats filibustered for fifty-seven days until the Senate voted cloture on 10 June 1964, by 71 to 29 — the first time it had ever closed debate on a civil rights bill. Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on 2 July 1964. Title II opened restaurants, hotels, theaters, and other places of public accommodation; Title VI let the federal government withhold money from discriminating programs; Title VII barred discrimination in hiring and pay by race, color, religion, sex, or national origin and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The word "sex" had been added by an amendment offered by Howard W. Smith of Virginia.

## Freedom Summer and Mississippi

**The summer of 1964.** — The Council of Federated Organizations, a coalition of SNCC, CORE, the SCLC, and the NAACP led in Mississippi by Bob Moses, brought about a thousand mostly white volunteers to register voters and run "freedom schools." On 21 June, three workers — James Chaney, a black Mississippian, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, two white New Yorkers — were stopped, jailed, and then murdered by Klansmen near Philadelphia, Mississippi. Their bodies were found on 4 August. The state brought no murder charges; a federal jury convicted seven men in 1967 on civil rights charges, and the case became known as "Mississippi Burning."

**The MFDP.** — The summer's political project was the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, which challenged the all-white regular delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City in August 1964. Fannie Lou Hamer, a sharecropper turned organizer, testified before the credentials committee about the beatings and the denial of the vote she had endured: "Is this America?" Johnson called an emergency press conference to push the testimony off the evening news. The convention offered the MFDP two at-large seats and a pledge of reform; the party rejected the compromise as tokenism. For many young organizers the lesson was that the national party would not risk its Southern wing, and the experience pushed SNCC toward a harder line.

## Selma and the Voting Rights Act

**Bloody Sunday.** — In early 1965 the SCLC joined the Dallas County Voters League in Selma, Alabama, where Sheriff Jim Clark had kept black registration near one per cent. On 7 March, about 600 marchers set out across the Edmund Pettus Bridge toward Montgomery; state troopers and mounted deputies attacked them with nightsticks and tear gas. John Lewis's skull was fractured. Television carried the scene that night, and the response was national outrage. On 9 March, King led a second march to the bridge and turned back, honoring a court order; that night a white minister from Boston, James Reeb, was beaten by segregationists and died two days later. On 15 March, Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress and adopted the movement's hymn: "We shall overcome." On 21 March the marchers set out again, under federal protection, and reached Montgomery on 25 March, 25,000 strong. That night Viola Liuzzo, a white volunteer from Detroit, was shot dead on the highway back to Selma.

**The Act.** — Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965. Section 2 restated the Fifteenth Amendment's ban nationwide. Section 4(b) supplied a formula: a state or county was "covered" if it used a test or device and had registration or turnout below fifty per cent in November 1964. Covered jurisdictions lost the use of literacy tests and similar devices, and Section 5 required them to obtain "preclearance" from the Justice Department or a federal court before changing any voting rule. Federal examiners could register voters directly. The effect was immediate: by the end of 1965 a quarter of a million black voters had registered, and in Mississippi black registration rose from about seven per cent in 1964 to about sixty per cent by 1968.

## Black Power and the turn

**Malcolm X.** — Through the early 1960s the Nation of Islam's most visible spokesman, Malcolm X, offered a critique of integration and nonviolence that many in the northern cities found more convincing than King's. In "The Ballot or the Bullet," delivered on 3 April 1964, he argued that the vote was a means and not an end, and that black Americans should carry their case to the United Nations. He broke with Elijah Muhammad in 1964, made a pilgrimage to Mecca, and founded the Organization of Afro-American Unity. He was assassinated in Harlem on 21 February 1965.

**Watts.** — On 11 August 1965, five days after Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act, a police stop in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles set off six days of uprising: thirty-four dead, more than a thousand injured, some four thousand arrested. The McCone Commission blamed a "sense of hopelessness" and reported that those who took part were not outsiders but residents. Legal equality in the South had not changed the conditions of northern black neighborhoods, where segregation was produced by housing markets and school boundaries rather than by statute.

**Black Power.** — In June 1966 James Meredith was shot and wounded on a one-man "March Against Fear" through Mississippi. SNCC's new chairman, Stokely Carmichael, took up the march and told a crowd in Greenwood, "We want Black Power." The slogan, and the Lowndes County Freedom Organization with its black panther emblem, expressed a demand for political power and self-determination rather than integration. In October 1966 Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party in Oakland. King's Chicago campaign against housing segregation met hostile crowds, and the phrase was widely heard as a rejection of nonviolence and of integration, feeding a white backlash in the congressional elections that fall.

## The long hot summers

**1967.** — In July 1967 uprisings in Newark, New Jersey, left twenty-six dead, and in Detroit, Michigan, forty-three. President Johnson's National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders, chaired by Governor Otto Kerner, reported in March 1968 that the country was "moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal," and named white racism and the conditions of the urban poor among the causes. Johnson, who had appointed the commission, did not act on its recommendations. The legal right to sit at a lunch counter or cast a ballot did not by itself change where a family could live, what school a child attended, or what work was available.

## 1968: Memphis, the assassination, and the Fair Housing Act

**Memphis.** — In early 1968 black sanitation workers in Memphis struck for recognition and better pay, marching under signs reading "I AM A MAN." King went to support them and to prepare the Poor People's Campaign, a planned encampment in Washington to press for jobs and income. On the evening of 4 April 1968, standing on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel, he was shot and killed. James Earl Ray was arrested and convicted of the murder. Uprisings broke out in more than a hundred cities.

**The Fair Housing Act.** — On 11 April 1968, a week after King's death, Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1968, whose Title VIII banned discrimination in the sale and rental of housing. It was the last of the great civil rights statutes and the one that had taken the longest to pass; fair-housing bills had been introduced every year since 1966 and had died in the Senate. The Poor People's Campaign went forward under Ralph Abernathy, and its encampment on the Mall, Resurrection City, was cleared in June.

## What remained undone

**The gap between law and life.** — The movement destroyed the legal foundations of segregation: the separate-but-equal rule, the poll tax, the literacy test, and the whites-only lunch counter and ballot box. It did not destroy segregation itself. In the North, segregation had been produced by housing markets, school district lines, and federal mortgage policy rather than by statute, and the 1968 act's enforcement was weak. The Supreme Court upheld busing remedies in Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971) but limited them across district lines in Milliken v. Bradley (1974), and white families left the cities. Black unemployment and poverty remained far above white levels, and the Kerner Commission's warning about two societies was not refuted by the statutes.

**The debate.** — Historians disagree about when the movement ended. Taylor Branch treats the years around King as a moral drama; other scholars tell it as a longer struggle beginning in the 1930s, or as one centered less on King than on the local organizers, many of them women, who did the daily work. The question the period opened — what the movement achieved and what it left undone — has no settled answer, but the statutes of 1964, 1965, and 1968, and the unfinished geography of American segregation, mark the distance between law and life.

## Questions to carry

- Brown declared segregation unconstitutional but left the remedy to the lower courts. How much did that choice shape the fourteen years that followed?
- The movement used federal courts and federal statutes against state and local governments. What does that reliance assume about where rights are safest?
- Nonviolent direct action was met with televised violence, and the images moved the country. How much did the strategy depend on the response it provoked, and what did that cost?
- Black Power is sometimes described as a break with the movement and sometimes as a continuation of it. What evidence in the period supports each reading?
- By 1968 the legal architecture of Southern segregation had fallen, yet northern cities were more segregated than before. What did the legal victories not reach?
