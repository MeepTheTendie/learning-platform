# The early republic

**Period:** c. 1789–1820 · **Region:** The United States

**A government on trial.** — On 30 April 1789, George Washington took the oath of office on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City, then the seat of the national government. The Constitution he swore to uphold had been ratified by eleven states only after a bitter contest, and two states, North Carolina and Rhode Island, were still outside the Union. The country he governed held about four million people, roughly one in five enslaved. There was no federal income tax, no standing army worth the name, no national bank, and no settled answer to whether a government of delegated and divided powers could hold together a sprawling, quarrelsome, and expanding society. The organizing question is whether the new republic could survive its own disagreements. It nearly did not; its survival owed more to choices, accidents, and the deaths of a few men than to any plan.

**The first Congress and the machinery of state.** — The first Congress under the Constitution met on 4 March 1789 and set about building institutions the document only sketched. The Judiciary Act of 1789 created a Supreme Court of six justices, thirteen district courts, and three circuit courts, and left unresolved a question settled only in 1803: who had the last word on what the Constitution meant. The same Congress created the departments of State, Treasury, and War and the office of Attorney General, and sent twelve amendments to the states, of which ten were ratified by 1791 and became the Bill of Rights. Congress also had to decide what to do about the war debt: the federal government owed about $54 million, some $21.5 million of it its own and the rest the debts of the individual states. How that debt was paid, and who profited, produced the first great factional fight of the new government.

**Hamilton's financial program.** — Alexander Hamilton, Washington's Secretary of the Treasury, put before Congress between 1790 and 1791 a set of reports that amounted to a plan for a modern fiscal state. In the Report on Public Credit (January 1790) he proposed to fund the national debt at face value and to assume the states' war debts, so that the credit of the United States would rest on the whole Union rather than on thirteen separate borrowers. In the Report on a National Bank (December 1790) he proposed a Bank of the United States, capitalized at $10 million and partly owned by the government, to hold public deposits and issue a uniform currency. In the Report on Manufactures (December 1791) he proposed protective tariffs and bounties to encourage domestic industry. Congress passed the first two; the third it shelved. To pay the interest, Hamilton proposed an excise on distilled spirits, passed in 1791.

The plan was brilliant and divisive. Funding the debt at face value rewarded the holders of government certificates, many of whom had sold them cheap to speculators, and assumption meant that states which had already paid down their debts, like Virginia, would help pay those of states which had not, like Massachusetts. Madison, who had been Hamilton's ally in the fight for the Constitution, broke with him over both points. More fundamentally, the bank raised the question of whether the Constitution's grant to Congress of power to make "all laws which shall be necessary and proper" allowed Congress to charter a corporation the document never mentioned. Hamilton said yes: the power to create the means was implied by the grant of the ends. Jefferson and Madison said no: a bank was not necessary, only convenient, and the Tenth Amendment reserved undelegated powers to the states. Washington signed the bank bill in February 1791. The argument between implied and strict construction would outlast both men.

The deadlock over assumption was broken by a bargain in June 1790, arranged over dinner at Jefferson's lodgings in New York, in which southern votes for assumption were traded for northern votes to place the permanent capital on the Potomac. Jefferson later described the dinner as the beginning of his political awakening; historians have argued about how much was actually decided there. The capital moved to Philadelphia for ten years and then to the Potomac in 1800.

**The first party system.** — Faction was not supposed to exist. The men who wrote the Constitution generally regarded organized opposition as corruption, and Washington's Farewell Address would warn against it in 1796. But the fight over Hamilton's program, and then over foreign policy, produced two recognizable groupings. The Federalists, strongest in New England, the middle states, and the commercial cities, and led by Hamilton and John Adams, favored a strong fiscal and commercial national state, close ties with Britain, and a loose reading of the Constitution. The Democratic-Republicans, strongest in the South and the backcountry, and led by Jefferson and Madison, favored a limited central government, an agrarian and expansionist future, sympathy with revolutionary France, and a strict reading of the Constitution. Neither was a modern mass party, but they fought hard.

Foreign affairs drove the division faster than any domestic question. When revolutionary France went to war with Britain in 1793, Washington issued a Neutrality Proclamation, and the French minister Edmond-Charles Genêt arrived in Charleston and began commissioning American privateers. The British, meanwhile, seized American ships and impressed American sailors. Washington sent John Jay to London in 1794; the resulting Jay's Treaty (ratified 1795) secured British evacuation of the northwestern posts but conceded little on neutral rights and was burned in effigy in many cities. By 1796 the lines were drawn, and the election of that year, in which Adams defeated Jefferson by 71 electoral votes to 68 and Jefferson became vice president, put two opponents in the two highest offices.

**The Whiskey Rebellion.** — The excise on distilled spirits that Hamilton designed to fund the debt fell hardest on the farmers west of the Appalachians, who turned their grain into whiskey because it was far cheaper to ship east than bulky grain. Resistance began as soon as the tax did: officials were tarred and feathered, stills were smashed, and in 1794 a federal marshal and the local inspector, General John Neville, were attacked at Neville's house near Pittsburgh. Washington saw in the resistance a test of whether the new government could enforce its laws. He called out the militia of four states, roughly 12,950 men, a larger force than any he had commanded during the Revolution, and rode west at its head. The rebellion collapsed before a shot was fired; about twenty men were arrested, two were convicted of treason, and Washington pardoned both. The episode cut both ways: it demonstrated that the federal government could project force into the interior, and it convinced Republicans that Hamilton's financial system was an engine of centralized coercion. Joseph J. Ellis reads the confrontation as the moment Washington established that the new government would enforce its own laws, however unpopular; critics read it as the first federal use of military power against its own citizens over a tax.

**Adams and the crisis of 1798.** — John Adams's single term was consumed by the threat of war with France. In 1797 French agents demanded a loan and a bribe before negotiations could begin, an episode the administration publicized as the XYZ Affair. The demand produced a wave of patriotic anger, captured in the phrase "Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute," and an undeclared naval conflict, the Quasi-War, in which American and French ships fought in the Caribbean. The Federalist Congress, seeing Republican editors as the voice of France, passed the Alien and Sedition Acts in 1798. The Naturalization Act lengthened the residency requirement for citizenship from five years to fourteen; the Alien Friends Act gave the president power to deport foreigners he judged dangerous; the Alien Enemies Act gave him power over the subjects of hostile powers in wartime; and the Sedition Act made it a crime to publish "false, scandalous, and malicious" attacks on the government or the president. The Sedition Act was enforced almost entirely against Republican newspapermen: Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was jailed and reelected from his cell, and Benjamin Franklin Bache of the Philadelphia Aurora died of yellow fever before trial. Of roughly twenty-five prosecutions, about ten ended in conviction.

**The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions.** — The Republican answer came from two state legislatures. Jefferson, writing secretly for Kentucky, and Madison, writing for Virginia, argued in 1798 that the states, not the federal courts, were the parties to the constitutional compact and had the right to judge when the federal government exceeded its delegated powers. Kentucky's first resolution declared that "whensoever the general government assumes undelegated powers, its acts are unauthoritative, void, and of no force." Virginia's resolution held that in cases of "deliberate, palpable, and dangerous" federal overreach the states "have the right, and are in duty bound, to interpose." The doctrine of state interposition, or nullification, would be revived by South Carolina in 1832 and by secessionists in 1860–61. At the time, no other state legislature endorsed the resolutions; several condemned them. The crisis passed not because the constitutional question was settled but because the election of 1800 removed the men who had made the laws.

**The election of 1800.** — The election of 1800 was the first in which power passed from one party to its opponent, and it was nearly a disaster. Jefferson and Aaron Burr, both Democratic-Republicans, received 73 electoral votes each; Adams received 65. The tie threw the election into the House of Representatives, where Federalists held the balance. For thirty-six ballots over a week in February 1801 the House deadlocked; Hamilton, who detested Burr more than he distrusted Jefferson, urged Federalists to allow Jefferson to win, and on the thirty-sixth ballot the deadlock broke. The episode exposed a flaw in the original design, since electors cast two votes without distinguishing president from vice president, and the Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, corrected it. Jefferson called his victory "the revolution of 1800" and said it was as real a revolution as that of 1776, achieved by the ballot rather than the sword. In his First Inaugural Address, delivered on 4 March 1801 in the new capital, he tried to lower the temperature: "We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists." Gordon S. Wood treats the transfer as proof that the American experiment had produced a genuine opposition politics; others note that the defeated Federalists never accepted the legitimacy of their defeat.

**Jefferson in power.** — Jefferson came to office determined to shrink the federal government: he reduced the army and navy, repealed the whiskey excise, let the Sedition Act expire, and applied the proceeds to paying down the debt, while keeping Hamilton's Bank because it worked.

**Marbury v. Madison.** — Before leaving office, the Federalist Congress had created new judgeships and Adams had filled them with Federalists, the "midnight judges." William Marbury had been appointed a justice of the peace in the District of Columbia, but his commission had not been delivered when Jefferson took office, and the new Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to deliver it. Marbury sued for a writ of mandamus, a court order commanding an official to perform a duty. In February 1803, Chief Justice John Marshall delivered an opinion that gave the Jefferson administration the case and the Court a power. Marshall held that Marbury had a legal right to the commission, but that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 giving the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to issue such writs was unconstitutional, because the Constitution limited the Court's original jurisdiction to cases involving ambassadors and states. The Court therefore could not order Madison to act. In reaching that conclusion, Marshall wrote the most consequential sentence in American constitutional law: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." A law repugnant to the Constitution, he held, is void. Judicial review was not invented from nothing, but Marbury gave it a practical footing and, by declining a fight it would have lost, established the Court as a co-equal branch. Some historians, including R. Kent Newmyer, emphasize Marshall's nationalism and his skill at building the Court's authority; others stress that judicial review remained contested for generations.

**The Louisiana Purchase.** — In 1800 Spain secretly ceded Louisiana back to France, and in 1802 the Spanish closed the port of New Orleans to American trade, alarming western farmers who depended on the Mississippi. Jefferson sent Robert Livingston and James Monroe to Paris to buy New Orleans and West Florida. Napoleon, whose plans for a North American empire had collapsed with the failure to reconquer Saint-Domingue (Haiti), offered the whole of Louisiana, some 828,000 square miles from the Mississippi to the Rockies, for about $15 million. The envoys signed on 30 April 1803; the Senate ratified in October; the transfer took place in December. Jefferson, who had made his name on strict construction, worried that the Constitution gave the federal government no power to acquire territory and drafted a constitutional amendment, then dropped it when his advisers warned of delay. The purchase doubled the size of the country and guaranteed that the question of slavery's expansion would follow it west. Joyce Appleby reads it as the fulfillment of the Republican vision of a republic of independent farmers; critics point out that the land belonged to Native nations who were not consulted, and that the purchase entrenched a slave empire in the lower Mississippi valley.

**Lewis and Clark.** — Even before the purchase was complete, Jefferson asked Congress to fund an expedition to find a water route to the Pacific. Meriwether Lewis, his private secretary, and William Clark, an army officer, led the Corps of Discovery up the Missouri River beginning in 1804, across the Rockies in 1805, to the Pacific coast that November, and back to St. Louis in September 1806. The party of roughly thirty-three included York, an enslaved man owned by Clark, and the Shoshone woman Sacagawea, whose presence and language skills helped the expedition obtain horses and guides. The expedition produced maps, journals, and collections of plants and animals and established American claims to the Oregon country. It also began a sustained American effort to draw the Native peoples of the West into trade, treaties, and eventually dispossession.

**The drift to war.** — Jefferson's second term was dominated by the failure of his attempt to keep the United States out of the European war by economic pressure. The British seized American ships and impressed American sailors, and in 1807 a British warship fired on the USS Chesapeake. Congress passed the Embargo Act in December 1807, forbidding American exports to all foreign ports; it damaged the American economy far more than it did Britain or France and was repealed in 1809. Meanwhile, in the West, the Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa built a confederacy to resist American expansion; in 1811 William Henry Harrison defeated the confederacy at Tippecanoe, and in 1812 Tecumseh allied with Britain. A group of young Republicans known as the War Hawks, including Henry Clay of Kentucky and John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, pressed for war, citing impressment, British support for Native resistance, and the desire for Canada and Florida. Congress declared war on 18 June 1812.

**The War of 1812.** — The war went badly at first. American invasions of Canada failed, and in August 1814 a British force entered Washington and burned the Capitol and the president's house. American fortunes revived on the water and on the frontier: Oliver Hazard Perry's victory on Lake Erie in 1813, Harrison's victory at the Thames that autumn in which Tecumseh was killed, and Thomas Macdonough's victory at Plattsburgh in 1814. In September 1814, as a British fleet bombarded Fort McHenry in Baltimore harbor, Francis Scott Key wrote the poem that became "The Star-Spangled Banner." New England Federalists, who had opposed the war, met at the Hartford Convention in December 1814 and drafted constitutional amendments, with secession in the air; the news of the Treaty of Ghent and Andrew Jackson's victory at New Orleans destroyed the party's reputation. The Treaty of Ghent, signed on 24 December 1814, ended the war with no change of territory and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights. Jackson's lopsided victory at New Orleans on 8 January 1815, won after the treaty was signed but before the news arrived, gave Americans the impression that they had won. The war's real results were a surge of national feeling, the collapse of the Federalist Party, and a generation of westward expansion and Native dispossession. Alan Taylor argues that it was also a civil war within North America, in which Native nations, Canadian settlers, and Americans fought over the continent's future.

**The Marshall Court.** — John Marshall served as Chief Justice from 1801 until 1835 and used the office to build the authority of the federal government and the Court. After Marbury, the Court struck down a state law for the first time in Fletcher v. Peck (1810), holding that Georgia could not revoke a land grant because the contract clause protected it. In Martin v. Hunter's Lessee (1816) it asserted appellate jurisdiction over state courts. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) it upheld the Second Bank of the United States and denied that a state could tax an instrument of the federal government, because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy." In Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819) it protected corporate charters under the contract clause, and in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) it read the commerce clause broadly. Together these decisions made the federal government, and the Court itself, the arbiters of a national market and a national constitution. Whether Marshall was a nationalist statesman or a partisan Federalist legislating from the bench remains a live argument among historians.

**The Era of Good Feelings.** — James Monroe, elected in 1816 and again in 1820, presided over one-party politics after the Federalists collapsed; a Boston newspaper, the Columbian Centinel, coined the phrase "Era of Good Feelings" in 1817 to describe his goodwill tour. The harmony was real but thin. The Panic of 1819, the first great peacetime financial crisis, brought bank failures, foreclosures, and unemployment. In 1819–20 the Missouri crisis forced Congress to confront slavery's expansion: Missouri applied for statehood as a slave state, and after a bitter fight Congress admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state and drew the line of 36°30' across the Louisiana Purchase territory, prohibiting slavery north of it except in Missouri. Jefferson called the crisis "a fire bell in the night." The compromise postponed the conflict for a generation.

**The Monroe Doctrine.** — In December 1823, in his annual message to Congress, Monroe declared that the American continents were "henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers," that the United States would not interfere in the internal affairs of European states or their existing colonies, and that any attempt by the European powers to extend their political system to the Western Hemisphere would be viewed as dangerous to American peace and safety. The occasion was the fear that the Holy Alliance of Russia, Prussia, and Austria might help Spain reconquer its former Latin American colonies; the doctrine was made credible by the British navy, which shared the goal of keeping other European powers out of the Americas. John Quincy Adams, Monroe's Secretary of State, was its chief architect. It was a statement of intent, not a treaty, and it had no force except the British fleet; but it announced that the United States claimed a hemisphere for itself, with consequences for Latin America and Native nations that lasted far beyond the period.

**The beginnings of the market revolution.** — Underneath the political history ran a slower transformation of how Americans worked and traded. The cotton gin, patented by Eli Whitney in 1794, made short-staple cotton profitable across the Deep South and tied the new southwestern states to the world market through New Orleans; it also revived and expanded slavery, and the domestic slave trade moved hundreds of thousands of people to the cotton frontier. In New England, Samuel Slater built the first successful water-powered cotton mill in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, in 1790, and in 1813 Francis Cabot Lowell and the Boston Manufacturing Company founded a mill at Waltham, Massachusetts, that combined spinning and weaving under one roof; by the 1820s the Lowell mills employed thousands of young farm women. The Second Bank of the United States, chartered in 1816, provided a national currency and credit; the National Road, begun in 1811, pushed west; and Robert Fulton's Clermont ran up the Hudson in 1807. State and private enterprise built the Erie Canal between 1817 and 1825, cutting the cost of moving goods from the Great Lakes to New York City by more than ninety percent. Henry Clay's "American System" — a protective tariff, a national bank, and federal internal improvements — was the political expression of this new economy. The market revolution created opportunities for some and insecurity for many and sharpened the divisions between a commercial North and a slaveholding South. Joyce Appleby argues that the Democratic-Republican vision of the 1790s was not a backward-looking agrarianism but a forward-looking liberalism of opportunity and exchange; Lance Banning and others reply that the Republicans' language of civic virtue was a genuine ideology, not a mask for capitalism.

**Why it held.** — The republic survived its first partisan conflicts for reasons partly structural and partly accidental. The Constitution's separation of powers made outright victory difficult, geography absorbed conflict and offered exit, and the deaths of Washington in 1799 and Hamilton in 1804 removed the most polarizing figures. A generation of politicians, however bitterly they disagreed, shared assumptions about representative government, property, and the rule of law. Ellis argues that the founders' personal bonds and their shared memory of the Revolution made compromise possible at the crucial moments; Wood argues that the deeper cause was the democratization of American society, which made partisan conflict normal and legitimate. Both arguments have limits: the republic's survival came at the expense of enslaved people and Native nations, and the conflicts postponed in 1820 would return with greater force.

## Chronology

- **1789** — The first Congress meets; Washington is inaugurated; the Judiciary Act and executive departments are created.
- **1790** — Hamilton's Report on Public Credit; the Compromise of 1790 moves the capital toward the Potomac.
- **1791** — The Bank of the United States is chartered; the whiskey excise is passed; the Bill of Rights is ratified.
- **1793** — Washington issues the Neutrality Proclamation; Genêt's mission to the United States.
- **1794** — The Whiskey Rebellion; Jay's Treaty; Eli Whitney patents the cotton gin.
- **1795** — Jay's Treaty is ratified; Pinckney's Treaty with Spain.
- **1796** — Washington's Farewell Address; Adams defeats Jefferson.
- **1798** — The XYZ Affair; the Alien and Sedition Acts; the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions.
- **1800** — The election ties Jefferson and Burr; the capital moves to Washington, D.C.; Gabriel's Rebellion in Richmond.
- **1801** — Jefferson is elected by the House; Marshall becomes Chief Justice; the Judiciary Act of 1801 creates the "midnight judges."
- **1803** — Marbury v. Madison; the Louisiana Purchase.
- **1804** — The Twelfth Amendment is ratified; the Lewis and Clark expedition sets out; Burr kills Hamilton in a duel.
- **1805** — Lewis and Clark reach the Pacific.
- **1807** — The Embargo Act; the Clermont runs up the Hudson; Burr is tried for treason and acquitted.
- **1811** — The Battle of Tippecanoe; the National Road begins.
- **1812** — The United States declares war on Britain; Madison is reelected.
- **1813** — Perry's victory on Lake Erie; the Battle of the Thames.
- **1814** — The British burn Washington; Fort McHenry; the Hartford Convention; the Treaty of Ghent.
- **1815** — The Battle of New Orleans; the Federalist Party collapses.
- **1816** — The Second Bank of the United States is chartered; Monroe is elected.
- **1819** — McCulloch v. Maryland; Dartmouth College v. Woodward; the Adams-Onís Treaty; the Panic of 1819.
- **1820** — The Missouri Compromise; Monroe is reelected without opposition.
- **1823** — The Monroe Doctrine is declared.
- **1824** — Gibbons v. Ogden; the presidential election is thrown to the House.

## Questions to carry

- The Constitution's framers feared faction. Why did organized parties appear anyway, and did they strengthen or weaken the republic?
- Hamilton and Jefferson disagreed about what the Constitution allowed. Which reading has history vindicated, and does the question have a right answer?
- The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions claimed that states could judge federal overreach. When does that doctrine become a threat to the Union?
- The War of 1812 settled none of the issues it was fought over, yet Americans remembered it as a victory. What does that tell us about how wars end?
- The market revolution and the expansion of slavery happened together. Can the early republic's survival be separated from what it cost?
