# America as a world power

**Period:** c. 1898–1920 · **Region:** The United States and the world

## The war with Spain

**A rebellion in Cuba.** — In February 1895 Cuban rebels rose against Spanish rule for the third time in thirty years. Spain sent General Valeriano Weyler, who forced rural Cubans into fortified towns — the reconcentrados — where hunger and disease killed perhaps 100,000 people by 1898. American newspapers, led by Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal, competed for readers with accounts of Spanish cruelty. In December 1897 a private letter from the Spanish minister in Washington, Enrique Dupuy de Lôme, was stolen and passed to Hearst; in it de Lôme called President William McKinley "weak and a bidder for the admiration of the crowd." The letter was printed on February 9, 1898.

**The Maine.** — On February 15, 1898, the armored cruiser USS Maine blew up in Havana harbour, where it had been sent to protect American lives and property; 266 of its 354 officers and crew died. A naval court of inquiry blamed a mine, though the cause was never settled. "Remember the Maine" became the cry. On April 11 McKinley asked Congress for authority to intervene; on April 20 Congress recognised Cuban independence and passed the Teller Amendment, which disclaimed "any disposition or intention to exercise sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control over said island except for the pacification thereof." War was declared on April 25, 1898.

**The "splendid little war."** — The fighting lasted less than four months. Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish squadron at Manila Bay in the Philippines on May 1. In Cuba an American army landed in June; on July 1 it took the heights above Santiago at El Caney and San Juan Hill, where Theodore Roosevelt's volunteer Rough Riders and the Black regulars of the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the 24th and 25th Infantry fought side by side. The Spanish fleet at Santiago was destroyed on July 3, and the city surrendered on July 17. An armistice followed on August 12. About 3,000 Americans died, but fewer than 400 in combat; the rest fell to yellow fever, typhoid, and malaria. John Hay, soon to be secretary of state, called it "a splendid little war."

**The Treaty of Paris.** — Peace was signed in Paris on December 10, 1898. Spain gave up Cuba, which became nominally independent, and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States; the United States paid Spain $20 million for the Philippines. Whether to take the Philippines at all became the question of the winter. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, by 57 to 27 — one vote more than the two-thirds required. The debate that followed turned on one question: should the United States act as an empire and a world power, and on what terms?

## The new empire

**Hawaii and the Pacific.** — In January 1893 a group of American planters and businessmen, with the help of the American minister and a landing party of sailors, overthrew Queen Liliuokalani of Hawaii. President Grover Cleveland, who thought the coup dishonourable, refused to annex the islands. McKinley revived the project and annexed Hawaii by joint resolution on July 7, 1898, in the middle of the war with Spain; it became a territory in 1900. The empire was a chain of coaling stations and naval bases across the Pacific as much as it was a set of colonies.

**The debate over empire.** — The annexations provoked the most serious argument about the nature of the republic since the fight over slavery. In November 1898 opponents founded the American Anti-Imperialist League in Boston. Its members were an odd coalition: the industrialist Andrew Carnegie, the philosopher William James, the writer Mark Twain, the reformer Jane Addams, the labour leader Samuel Gompers, and the German-born statesman Carl Schurz. Their platform of October 1899 called imperialism "hostile to liberty," insisted that "governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed," and demanded independence for the Filipinos. The anti-imperialists were not all humanitarians; some opposed annexation precisely because they did not want non-white peoples admitted to the Union or to citizenship. The Indiana orator Albert Beveridge, soon a senator, said in 1898 that God had marked the English-speaking peoples "as the master organizers of the world"; Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, the naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan, and Theodore Roosevelt argued that the nation needed overseas bases, a two-ocean navy, and markets. The debate was never settled by argument; the annexationists had the votes, and the treaty passed.

**Governing the possessions.** — The new possessions were governed without the consent of their inhabitants, and the Supreme Court supplied a doctrine. In the Insular Cases of 1901, above all Downes v. Bidwell, the Court held that the Constitution did not automatically follow the flag: full rights attached only to "incorporated" territory, while the new possessions were "unincorporated" and subject to whatever Congress chose. Puerto Rico got a civil government by the Foraker Act of 1900 and American citizenship by the Jones-Shafroth Act of 1917. The Philippines were governed from 1901 by a civil governor, William Howard Taft, under the Philippine Organic Act of 1902. Cuba was the special case. The Platt Amendment, passed on March 2, 1901 and written into the Cuban constitution the next year, forbade Cuba to make treaties with foreign powers, limited its debt, allowed the United States to intervene "for the preservation of Cuban independence," and required Cuba to lease land for naval stations — which became Guantánamo Bay. The United States withdrew in 1902 but returned in 1906, and again in 1912 and 1917. Cuba was independent in name and a protectorate in fact.

## The Philippine war

**A war of independence.** — The Filipinos had expected independence. Emilio Aguinaldo had proclaimed a republic in 1899 and been excluded from the peace at Paris. Fighting began on February 4, 1899, at San Juan Bridge outside Manila, and the Philippine-American War — the "Philippine insurrection" in American usage — lasted more than three years. After the Filipino army was beaten in conventional battle, its units broke into guerrilla bands, and the Americans responded with tactics copied from the war in Cuba: forced resettlement, destruction of villages and crops, and torture. At Balangiga on Samar in September 1901, Filipino fighters killed some forty-eight American soldiers; General Jacob H. Smith then ordered the island turned into "a howling wilderness." An American officer's report described the "water cure," a form of torture by forced ingestion of water. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901 and swore allegiance to the United States; Washington declared the war over on July 4, 1902, though resistance continued in the Muslim south until 1913. About 4,200 American soldiers died, and some 20,000 Filipino fighters. The civilian death toll is disputed and enormous: estimates range from 200,000 to well over 500,000, mostly from disease and hunger. The United States promised eventual independence in the Jones Act of 1916, and granted it in 1946.

## China, the canal, and the Caribbean

**The Open Door.** — American policy in East Asia had no colony to defend, so it argued for the rules that suited a trading nation. After Japan's defeat of China in 1895, the powers carved out spheres of influence along the Chinese coast. In September 1899 Secretary of State John Hay sent notes to Britain, Germany, Russia, France, Italy, and Japan asking them to keep the "open door": equal commercial access to China within their spheres. When the Boxer Uprising — an anti-foreign movement in northern China — broke out in 1900 and the foreign legations in Beijing were besieged, the United States sent troops as part of an international relief force. Hay sent a second round of notes in July 1900, adding a commitment to Chinese territorial and administrative integrity. The Open Door was less a law than an aspiration, but it became the central principle of American policy in Asia for four decades.

**The Panama Canal.** — The French had tried and failed to dig a canal across the isthmus of Panama in the 1880s; their company collapsed in scandal, and about 20,000 workers died of disease. The United States, after a long debate over a route through Nicaragua, chose Panama. The Spooner Act of 1902 authorised the purchase of the French assets, but the Colombian senate refused to ratify a treaty granting the United States the canal zone. In November 1903 Panamanian nationalists, encouraged by the French engineer Philippe Bunau-Varilla and assured of American support, declared independence from Colombia; President Roosevelt sent the warship USS Nashville, and Colombia could not land troops. The new republic was recognised within days, and the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty of November 18, 1903 gave the United States a ten-mile-wide canal zone in perpetuity. Roosevelt later said, "I took the Isthmus and let Congress debate." Construction ran from 1904 to 1914; the chief medical officer William Gorgas defeated yellow fever and malaria by draining breeding grounds. The canal opened on August 15, 1914, cutting the sea voyage from New York to San Francisco from about 13,000 miles around Cape Horn to about 5,200.

**The Roosevelt Corollary.** — In December 1904, in his annual message to Congress, Roosevelt added a corollary to the Monroe Doctrine of 1823. The Monroe Doctrine had warned European powers to stay out of the Americas. Roosevelt now declared that if a Latin American nation defaulted on its debts or failed to keep order, the United States itself would intervene to prevent European intervention — the "big stick." The occasion was the Dominican Republic, whose customs revenues the United States took over in 1905 to pay its creditors. The policy rested on Roosevelt's formula, first used at the Minnesota State Fair in 1901: "speak softly and carry a big stick." The United States intervened in Cuba in 1906 and ran the country for three years. Roosevelt also mediated the Russo-Japanese War in 1905, winning the Nobel Peace Prize, and sent the Great White Fleet around the world from 1907 to 1909.

## Dollar diplomacy and moral diplomacy

**Taft and Knox.** — Roosevelt's successor, William Howard Taft, and his secretary of state, Philander C. Knox, gave the corollary an economic form. "Dollar diplomacy" — the phrase was Knox's — meant encouraging American bankers and businessmen to invest abroad, especially in China and the Caribbean, as a substitute for armed intervention. In practice it often produced both: when Nicaragua's government refused a loan arrangement, Taft sent Marines in 1912 and kept them there, with a brief interval, until 1933. The policy assumed that American capital would stabilise weak states and that American influence would follow American dollars, an assumption that seldom held.

**Wilson and Mexico.** — Woodrow Wilson came to office in 1913 promising "moral diplomacy" and condemning dollar diplomacy as a policy that treated other peoples as instruments. At Mobile, Alabama, in October 1913, he said that the United States would help Latin America only by "the orderly processes of just government based upon law." Within months he sent the Navy to Veracruz. The Mexican Revolution had overthrown Porfirio Díaz in 1911 and brought Francisco Madero, then Victoriano Huerta, to power. Wilson refused to recognise Huerta, called his regime a government of "butchers," and in April 1914, after Mexican authorities arrested American sailors at Tampico, ordered the bombardment and occupation of Veracruz, which left some 200 dead, most of them Mexican. When the revolutionary Francisco "Pancho" Villa raided Columbus, New Mexico, in March 1916, Wilson sent General John J. Pershing with 10,000 troops into Mexico. The expedition was withdrawn in early 1917 as war with Germany neared. Wilson's moral language and his interventions were not easy to reconcile.

**Wilson's Caribbean.** — The same pattern held in the Caribbean. In 1915, after a bloody political crisis, Marines landed in Haiti and stayed nineteen years. In 1916 Marines occupied the Dominican Republic and established a military government that lasted until 1924. In 1917 the United States bought the Danish West Indies for $25 million, renaming them the Virgin Islands. Wilson had come into office saying that governments derived their powers from the consent of the governed; by 1917 he governed several million people in the Caribbean and the Pacific without it.

## Neutrality and the Great War

**The outbreak.** — The First World War began in Europe in the summer of 1914, after the assassination of the Austrian archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo on June 28. On August 4, 1914, Wilson issued a proclamation of neutrality and asked Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action." Neutrality in trade was another matter. Britain controlled the sea and imposed a blockade of the Central Powers; the United States, though technically a neutral, sold and lent overwhelmingly to Britain and France, because Britain could buy and transport and Germany could not. By 1917 American bankers had extended about $2.3 billion in credits to the Allies. Neutrality in law and partiality in economics lived side by side.

**The submarine and the Lusitania.** — Germany answered the British blockade with submarines. In February 1915 it declared the waters around Britain a war zone. On May 7, 1915, a German U-boat sank the British liner Lusitania off Ireland; 1,198 people died, including 128 Americans. Wilson protested and demanded that Germany protect non-combatants, and for a time the sinkings eased. The Arabic was sunk in August 1915 and the Sussex in March 1916; after the Sussex the Germans promised not to sink merchant ships without warning, and Wilson's diplomacy appeared to have kept the peace. He ran for reelection in 1916 on the record of having kept the country out of war, and won narrowly against Charles Evans Hughes.

**Peace without victory.** — In January 1917, with both sides exhausted, Wilson spoke to the Senate and called for "peace without victory" — a settlement between equals, not a victor's peace. The speech was an attempt to end the war, and it failed. Germany, facing starvation from the British blockade, decided that unrestricted submarine warfare could force Britain to its knees before American troops could arrive. On January 31, 1917, it announced that all shipping in the war zone would be sunk without warning. On February 3 the United States broke off diplomatic relations.

## The decision for war

**The Zimmermann telegram.** — In January 1917 British intelligence intercepted and decoded a telegram from the German foreign secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, to the German minister in Mexico. Zimmermann proposed that if the United States entered the war, Mexico should ally with Germany and would recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. The British held the telegram until they could release it without revealing that they had broken the German code; Wilson released it on March 1. Meanwhile German submarines began sinking American ships. Between March 16 and 18 alone, three American merchant vessels went down with American sailors.

**The war message.** — On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress to declare war. The Russian Revolution the previous month had removed the objection that the Allies included a tsar; Wilson could now describe the war as a struggle between democracy and autocracy. "The world must be made safe for democracy," he told Congress. "We have no selfish ends to serve. We desire no conquest, no dominion." Congress declared war on April 6, 1917, with six senators and fifty representatives voting no.

**Why the United States entered.** — The immediate triggers are not in doubt; what they meant is contested. William Appleman Williams and Walter LaFeber have read the entry as the culmination of a long economic expansion: the search for markets and the protection of Allied loans made intervention likely once the Allied cause was threatened. Ernest R. May and other strategic historians emphasise the failure of armed neutrality and the German decision to attack American shipping. Arthur S. Link stressed Wilson's own convictions — his belief that American power was needed to build a liberal peace.

## The home front

**The Committee on Public Information.** — Once in the war, the government mobilised opinion as well as men. Wilson created the Committee on Public Information in April 1917 under the journalist George Creel. The committee produced films, posters, pamphlets, and a corps of 75,000 "Four Minute Men" who spoke in theatres and halls. It portrayed the Germans as barbarians and the war as a crusade, and it was the first large-scale official propaganda campaign in American history.

**The Espionage and Sedition Acts.** — The Espionage Act of June 1917 made it a crime to obstruct recruiting or to cause insubordination in the armed forces, and gave the postmaster general power to exclude from the mail material he judged disloyal. The Sedition Act of May 1918 went further, punishing "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the government, the flag, or the uniform. The socialist leader Eugene V. Debs was sentenced to ten years in 1918 for a speech in Canton, Ohio, that praised men who resisted conscription; he ran for president from prison in 1920 and was pardoned in 1921. In Schenck v. United States (1919) the Supreme Court upheld the Espionage Act and Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote that speech could be punished when it created "a clear and present danger." In Abrams v. United States later that year Holmes dissented, arguing that the best test of truth was "the power of the thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market."

**The Great Migration.** — The war also moved people. Between about 1915 and 1930, roughly 1.5 million Black Southerners left the rural South for the cities of the North and Midwest — Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, New York — drawn by war-industry jobs and pushed by Jim Crow, debt peonage, and violence. The migration transformed Black life in the North. It also produced conflict: a pogrom in East St. Louis in 1917 killed dozens of Black residents, and the "Red Summer" of 1919 brought riots in more than two dozen cities, including a week of violence in Chicago that left thirty-eight dead. Black soldiers who had fought in France returned to a country that still denied them the vote and often their lives.

**Mobilisation and the flu.** — The war effort was run by boards: the War Industries Board under Bernard Baruch set production priorities; the Food Administration under Herbert Hoover managed supplies. Liberty Bonds and taxes paid for much of the war. The American Expeditionary Forces, eventually about two million men, were commanded by General John J. Pershing; they fought their largest battle in the Meuse-Argonne offensive from late September 1918 until the armistice on November 11. About 116,000 Americans died in the war, roughly half from disease. Among the diseases was influenza: the pandemic of 1918–1919 killed an estimated 675,000 people in the United States, far more than the fighting. In 1920 the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified that August, gave women the vote.

## The peace and the League

**The Fourteen Points.** — In January 1918, ten months before the armistice, Wilson went before Congress with a statement of war aims: the Fourteen Points. They called for open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, arms reduction, and an impartial adjustment of colonial claims. They promised self-determination in Europe — a Polish state, autonomous development for the peoples of Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire — and, in the fourteenth point, "a general association of nations" to guarantee the political independence and territorial integrity of great and small states alike. They gave the American war a programme and made Wilson the spokesman for a new kind of international order.

**Paris.** — The peace conference opened in Paris in January 1919. Wilson went in person, the first American president to leave the country while in office. But the conference was dominated by the victors' interests: Georges Clemenceau of France wanted security and reparations, David Lloyd George of Britain wanted the empire and the German fleet, and Vittorio Orlando of Italy wanted territory. Wilson got the League of Nations written into the Treaty of Versailles, signed on June 28, 1919, and Article X of the League Covenant committed members to preserve the independence of all members against external aggression. He gave up much else. The mandate system put former German and Ottoman colonies under Allied administration rather than independence, and Japan's proposal to affirm racial equality was rejected. The treaty imposed heavy reparations and war-guilt on Germany.

**The Senate fight.** — The Constitution requires the Senate to approve treaties by two-thirds. Wilson's opponents were of three kinds: a small group of "irreconcilables," led by William Borah and Hiram Johnson, who would not join a League under any terms; the "reservationists" around Henry Cabot Lodge, the chair of the Foreign Relations Committee, who wanted the treaty amended to protect American sovereignty and the Monroe Doctrine; and Democrats loyal to Wilson. Lodge's committee attached fourteen reservations, above all to Article X. Wilson refused to compromise. He took his case to the people in a speaking tour of the West in September 1919, collapsed in Colorado, and suffered a stroke on October 2 that left him partly paralysed. On November 19, 1919, the Senate rejected the treaty with reservations and then without them; a second vote on March 19, 1920, failed 49 to 35, seven short of two-thirds. The United States made a separate peace with Germany in 1921 and never joined the League it had done most to create.

**The reckoning.** — Should the United States act as an empire and a world power, and on what terms? The years from 1898 to 1920 gave two different answers at once. In the Pacific and the Caribbean, the country built an empire: it annexed Hawaii and the Philippines, made Cuba a protectorate, took Puerto Rico and Guam, and intervened repeatedly in the states around the Gulf. In Europe, it claimed a different role: it entered the war, Wilson said, not for conquest or dominion but to make the world safe for democracy, and it proposed to bind itself to a league of nations. The Senate refused the second commitment while keeping the first. The two halves of the story belong together, because they are the two faces of the same question — how far the United States would accept obligations abroad, and to whom it would acknowledge them. Historians disagree about which impulse was primary and about whether the rejection of the League was a decisive turn or a temporary one. What is not in dispute is that the country that came out of the war was a world power, and that it never settled what being one required.

## Chronology

- **February 15, 1898** — The USS Maine explodes in Havana harbour; 266 die.
- **April 25, 1898** — The United States declares war on Spain.
- **July 7, 1898** — Hawaii is annexed by joint resolution.
- **December 10, 1898** — The Treaty of Paris: Spain cedes Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines.
- **February 4, 1899** — Fighting begins between American and Filipino forces near Manila.
- **February 6, 1899** — The Senate ratifies the treaty of Paris, 57–27.
- **October 1899** — The American Anti-Imperialist League adopts its platform.
- **1900** — The Foraker Act gives Puerto Rico a civil government; the Boxer Uprising draws American troops to China.
- **March 2, 1901** — Congress passes the Platt Amendment.
- **September 1901** — The Balangiga attack on Samar, and the campaign that follows.
- **July 4, 1902** — Washington declares the Philippine-American War over.
- **November 3, 1903** — Panama declares independence; the canal treaty follows.
- **December 1904** — Roosevelt states the Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine.
- **1912** — Marines intervene in Nicaragua under Taft's dollar diplomacy.
- **April 1914** — American forces occupy Veracruz.
- **May 7, 1915** — A U-boat sinks the Lusitania; 128 Americans die.
- **March 1916** — Pershing's expedition crosses into Mexico.
- **January 31, 1917** — Germany announces unrestricted submarine warfare.
- **March 1, 1917** — The Zimmermann telegram is published.
- **April 6, 1917** — Congress declares war on Germany.
- **January 8, 1918** — Wilson delivers the Fourteen Points.
- **May 1918** — The Sedition Act becomes law.
- **June 28, 1919** — The Treaty of Versailles is signed.
- **October 2, 1919** — Wilson suffers a stroke.
- **November 19, 1919** — The Senate rejects the treaty; it fails again on March 19, 1920.
- **August 1920** — The Nineteenth Amendment is ratified.

## Questions to carry

- Was 1898 a break in American history or a continuation of westward expansion?
- The anti-imperialists quoted the Declaration of Independence. Does that make their position the consistent one, and their opponents the hypocrites?
- Who counted as "the governed" whose consent made a government just, and how did race shape the answers?
- Was the Senate's rejection of the League a principled defence of sovereignty or a failure of leadership?
- If the United States rejected the League in 1920, why did it go on to build so much of the order that followed?
