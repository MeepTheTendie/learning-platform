# The sixties, Vietnam, and upheaval

**Period:** c. 1960–1975 · **Region:** The United States

**The promise and the fracture.** — In the spring of 1964 Lyndon Johnson told a graduating class at the University of Michigan that the country could move "upward to the Great Society," a nation that would end poverty and racial injustice. Four years later the same president announced that he would not run again, driven from the race by a war that had split his party, and within months the country watched its two most prominent advocates of racial and economic hope, Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert Kennedy, shot dead ten weeks apart. By 1974 the president who had promised to end the war had himself resigned in disgrace. The organizing question is how a decade that opened in such confidence closed in such division and distrust. The country attempted two large things at once, a welfare state at home and a land war in Asia; the second consumed the first, while the gap between what officials said and what the evidence showed widened until many stopped believing them. But the result was never foreordained: at almost every turn, contemporaries believed they were choosing the cautious course.

## The New Frontier and the Great Society

**The election of 1960.** — John F. Kennedy, a Massachusetts Democrat and the first Catholic elected to the presidency, defeated Richard Nixon by one of the narrowest margins in American history: about 112,000 votes out of 68 million, though the electoral vote was comfortable at 303 to 219. His inaugural address in January 1961 asked Americans to "ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." The New Frontier mixed Cold War and domestic measures — the Peace Corps, the Alliance for Progress, a pledge to land a man on the moon — with bills on education, health, and the minimum wage that Congress largely stalled. The early crises were foreign: the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba in 1961, the Berlin Wall, and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962. The domestic New Frontier was still mostly a promise when Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963.

**Johnson takes up the cause.** — Kennedy's vice president, Lyndon Baines Johnson, a Texas Democrat and former Senate majority leader, used the shock of the assassination and his own legislative skill to pass more in two years than Kennedy had in three. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and discrimination in employment; the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, ratified that January, abolished the poll tax in federal elections. In May 1964, at Ann Arbor, Johnson gave the Great Society its name. In November he crushed the Republican Barry Goldwater, an Arizona senator who had voted against the Civil Rights Act and talked of using nuclear weapons in Vietnam, by 486 electoral votes to 52.

**What the Great Society did.** — The 89th Congress, elected with Johnson, passed an extraordinary body of law. Medicare, signed in July 1965, paid hospital and doctor bills for the elderly; Medicaid, passed the same month, did the same for the poor under state administration. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act sent federal money to schools with poor students; Head Start gave preschool to poor children; the Job Corps and VISTA trained and placed the young; food stamps also date from these years. The Office of Economic Opportunity, created in 1964, ran the War on Poverty that Johnson had declared in his State of the Union that January. The Immigration and Nationality Act of October 1965 abolished the national-origins quotas that had favored northern and western Europeans since the 1920s, opening the way to large immigration from Asia and Latin America. The results were real: the official poverty rate fell from about 22 percent in 1959 to about 12 percent in 1969. The programs were expensive and vulnerable to the charge that they created dependency.

**The Warren Court.** — The Supreme Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren, who served from 1953 to 1969, expanded individual rights further and faster than any court before it. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) had already declared segregated schools unconstitutional; the Warren Court went on to require that state legislative districts be roughly equal in population (Reynolds v. Sims, 1964), to require counsel for poor defendants (Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963), to require police to warn suspects of their rights (Miranda v. Arizona, 1966), and to strike down a state ban on contraceptives as an invasion of marital privacy (Griswold v. Connecticut, 1965). The decisions enraged many conservatives, who put "Impeach Earl Warren" on billboards and made the Court a permanent issue in presidential politics.

## The rights revolution and the women's movement

**The movement for Black freedom.** — The sit-ins that began at a Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, in February 1960 spread across the South; the Freedom Rides of 1961 tested interstate bus desegregation; the March on Washington in August 1963 brought 250,000 people to the Lincoln Memorial, where King described his dream. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, passed after the beating of marchers at Selma, Alabama, ended legal segregation and gave the federal government tools to protect the vote. The laws did not end inequality. Riots in Watts in August 1965, in Newark and Detroit in 1967, and across more than a hundred cities after King's assassination in 1968 exposed a gulf between legal equality and daily life; the Kerner Commission reported in 1968 that the country was "moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal." A younger generation turned toward Black Power, a term popularized by Stokely Carmichael in 1966.

**The Feminine Mystique.** — In 1963 Betty Friedan, a suburban mother and former journalist, published The Feminine Mystique, which gave a name to what she called "the problem that has no name": the dissatisfaction of educated women whose lives had been defined entirely by housework, marriage, and children. The book sold millions of copies and helped revive a feminist movement quiet since the winning of the vote in 1920. The ground had been prepared by the President's Commission on the Status of Women (1961) and the Equal Pay Act of 1963. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned employment discrimination by sex as well as race — the word "sex" was added by a segregationist congressman, Howard Smith of Virginia, who may have meant to kill the bill but instead supplied the legal basis for a generation of complaints. In 1966 Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women. The movement of the early 1970s was broader: the National Women's Political Caucus (1971), Gloria Steinem's Ms. magazine (1972), the anti-discrimination law known as Title IX (1972), and Roe v. Wade (1973), which legalized abortion in the first trimester.

## Vietnam: from advisers to war

**A war inherited.** — Vietnam had been part of French Indochina until the Second World War, when Japan occupied it; in September 1945 the communist leader Ho Chi Minh declared independence, and France fought to reclaim the colony for eight years. The French garrison at Dien Bien Phu fell in May 1954, and the Geneva Accords that July divided Vietnam at the seventeenth parallel, with elections promised for 1956. Those elections were never held in the south. President Dwight Eisenhower, who had spoken in April 1954 of a "falling domino" principle — that the loss of one country to communism would topple its neighbors — backed the anti-communist government of Ngo Dinh Diem in Saigon, sent military advisers, and made South Vietnam a client state. By the end of 1963 there were about 16,300 American advisers in the country. Diem's repression of Buddhists turned much of the population against him; a military coup, encouraged by American officials, killed him on 2 November 1963. Three weeks later Kennedy was dead, and Johnson inherited a deteriorating war.

**The Gulf of Tonkin and the blank check.** — The decisive turn came in August 1964. On 2 August the destroyer USS Maddox, on an intelligence patrol in the Gulf of Tonkin, was attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats; on the night of 4 August, in bad weather and on the basis of radar and intercepted radio signals, the Maddox and the USS C. Turner Joy reported a second attack that later evidence made doubtful. Johnson ordered retaliatory air strikes and asked Congress for a resolution of support. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, passed on 7 August, approved the President's determination to take "all necessary steps, including the use of armed force," and to "prevent further aggression." It was not a declaration of war, and it passed the House 416 to 0 and the Senate 88 to 2, with only Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening dissenting. Johnson campaigned that autumn as the peace candidate, saying that "we are not about to send American boys nine or ten thousand miles away from home to do what Asian boys ought to be doing for themselves." He won 61 percent of the popular vote. The resolution became the legal foundation for everything that followed.

**Escalation and the credibility gap.** — In February 1965, after a Viet Cong attack on an American base at Pleiku killed eight soldiers, Johnson ordered the sustained bombing campaign called Operation Rolling Thunder, and in March the first American combat troops, 3,500 Marines, landed at Da Nang. General William Westmoreland, the commander in Saigon, pursued a strategy of attrition: large-unit "search and destroy" missions intended to kill more of the enemy than he could replace, measured by body counts that were often inflated. American troop strength rose from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 536,000 by early 1968, and the tonnage of bombs dropped on Indochina eventually exceeded that of the Second World War. As the war expanded, the gap between official optimism and the evidence on the ground became a subject of daily journalism, and reporters gave it a name: the credibility gap. Officials spoke of the "light at the end of the tunnel" while casualty figures rose and South Vietnamese governments changed every few months. The phrase captured something new in American politics: the suspicion that the government was not merely wrong but managing the truth.

**Tet and the unraveling.** — On 30 January 1968, during the truce of the lunar new year holiday called Tet, some 84,000 communist troops attacked more than a hundred cities and towns across South Vietnam, including the American embassy in Saigon and the old imperial capital of Hue. The offensive failed militarily: the Viet Cong were badly mauled and never recovered as a fighting force, and the South Vietnamese government did not fall. But it succeeded politically. The scale of the attacks contradicted the administration's assurances that the enemy was fading, and the televised battle for Hue made a lie of the progress reports. On 27 February the CBS anchor Walter Cronkite told his audience that the war was "mired in stalemate," and Johnson is reported to have said that if he had lost Cronkite he had lost the country. On 31 March 1968 Johnson announced that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination, and that he was halting most bombing of North Vietnam and opening peace talks.

## The movement against the war, and the counterculture

**The antiwar movement.** — Organized opposition began on the left. Students for a Democratic Society, founded in 1960, issued the Port Huron Statement in 1962, a manifesto of "participatory democracy" written largely by Tom Hayden. The first national teach-in against the war was held at the University of Michigan in March 1965; a march on Washington in April 1965 drew perhaps 25,000 people; by October 1967 a march on the Pentagon drew 100,000, and the 1969 Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands into the streets. The movement was never united: some demonstrators sought negotiation, some immediate withdrawal, some revolution, and a small faction, the Weather Underground, turned to bombings in 1969. Resistance to the draft grew as well, from counseling and conscientious objection to draft-card burning and the flight of tens of thousands to Canada.

**The counterculture.** — Beside the political movement grew a cultural one. The singer Bob Dylan and the Beatles changed the sound of popular music; the birth-control pill, approved in 1960, helped separate sex from procreation and fed a sexual revolution; Timothy Leary urged the young to "turn on, tune in, drop out." In 1967 the Haight-Ashbury district of San Francisco drew thousands for the "Summer of Love," and in August 1969 some 400,000 people gathered on a farm in Bethel, New York, for the Woodstock music festival. The counterculture celebrated spontaneity, communal living, and the rejection of what it called the establishment. A smaller and angrier fringe, the Yippies of Abbie Hoffman and Jerry Rubin, made theater of protest at the 1968 Democratic convention.

**1968: the year that broke.** — The events of 1968 are usually treated as a single catastrophe. In January the Tet Offensive began. In March, Senator Eugene McCarthy's strong showing in the New Hampshire primary on an antiwar platform drove Robert Kennedy into the race and then drove Johnson out of it. On 4 April Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis; riots broke out in more than a hundred cities and killed dozens. On 5 June, moments after winning the California primary, Robert Kennedy was shot in a Los Angeles hotel and died the next day. In August the Democrats met in Chicago, where Mayor Richard Daley's police fought antiwar demonstrators in the streets and on television, chanting "the whole world is watching," while the party nominated Vice President Hubert Humphrey. The segregationist George Wallace ran as an independent and won 13.5 percent of the popular vote and five states in the South. In November Richard Nixon, who had promised to restore "law and order" and to end the war honorably, defeated Humphrey by 301 electoral votes to 191, with Wallace taking 46. The election did not settle the country's divisions; it registered them.

## Nixon, Cambodia, and the end of the war

**Vietnamization and the secret war.** — Nixon took office in January 1969 with a plan he called Vietnamization: to build up the South Vietnamese army and withdraw American troops while continuing to bomb and to negotiate, in pursuit of what he called "peace with honor." Between 1969 and 1972 American troop strength fell from more than 540,000 to about 24,000, even as the bombing widened. In March 1969 Nixon secretly ordered the bombing of communist sanctuaries in neutral Cambodia, a campaign code-named Operation Menu that was concealed from Congress and the public for more than a year. In a televised address on 3 November 1969 he appealed to the "great silent majority" for support and claimed that the protesters did not represent the country. In April 1970, after a coup in Cambodia, Nixon announced that American and South Vietnamese forces were invading that country to destroy enemy bases. The announcement set off the largest wave of campus protest of the war.

**Kent State.** — On 4 May 1970, at Kent State University in Ohio, National Guardsmen called out to quell demonstrations fired into a crowd of students, killing four and wounding nine. Ten days later, at Jackson State College in Mississippi, police fired into a dormitory and killed two more students. Hundreds of campuses shut down. The killings at Kent State, photographed and televised, became for many Americans the image of a government turning its weapons on its own young people. The war's moral standing had already been damaged by the revelation, in November 1969, of the My Lai massacre: in March 1968 an American platoon had killed more than 500 unarmed villagers in a hamlet in South Vietnam, and the officer in charge, Lieutenant William Calley, was convicted of murder in 1971.

**The Pentagon Papers and Watergate.** — In June 1971 the New York Times began publishing a classified Defense Department history of the war, the Pentagon Papers, which the analyst Daniel Ellsberg had copied and given to the press. The documents showed that successive administrations had planned and widened the war while telling the public something narrower, and that the Johnson administration had resolved on escalation months before the Gulf of Tonkin incident. The Nixon administration tried to block publication, but the Supreme Court in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971) refused to impose prior restraint. Nixon then set up a secret team, the "Plumbers," to stop leaks; their methods led directly to the June 1972 burglary of the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate office building. Nixon won re-election that November in a landslide, but the burglary, the cover-up, and the tapes of his own conversations unraveled his presidency. Televised Senate hearings and the Supreme Court's order to surrender the tapes in United States v. Nixon (1974) destroyed his support. On 9 August 1974 Nixon resigned, the only American president to do so, and Gerald Ford, appointed vice president after Spiro Agnew resigned, pardoned him a month later.

**The fall of Saigon.** — The Paris Peace Accords, signed in January 1973, provided for the withdrawal of American forces and the return of prisoners of war, but left the political future of South Vietnam unresolved and North Vietnamese troops in place. Congress, tired of the war and determined to check the president, passed the War Powers Resolution in November 1973 over Nixon's veto, requiring the president to report and then obtain congressional approval for any sustained use of force. In the spring of 1975, North Vietnamese forces advanced across the south; the last Americans were evacuated from the roof of the embassy in Saigon on 30 April 1975, and the city fell the same day. The war cost more than 58,000 American lives and over 300,000 wounded, and Vietnamese deaths are estimated in the millions. The Great Society had promised to end poverty at home; the war, its critics charged, had spent that promise on bombs.

## The movements of the late sixties

**The Chicano movement.** — Mexican Americans, particularly in the Southwest, built their own movement in these years. In 1962 Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta founded what became the United Farm Workers, and in September 1965 the union began a strike of grape pickers in Delano, California, that lasted five years and was carried to victory in 1970 by a national boycott, fasting, and the slogan "Sí, se puede" — "Yes, it can be done." In Denver, Rodolfo "Corky" Gonzales founded the Crusade for Justice in 1966 and wrote the epic poem "Yo Soy Joaquín"; in Los Angeles, the Brown Berets and high-school students who walked out of classes in the "blowouts" of March 1968 demanded better schools and an end to discrimination. In 1970 activists formed La Raza Unida, a third party that won local offices in south Texas.

**The American Indian Movement.** — Native activists challenged a longer history of dispossession. The American Indian Movement, founded in Minneapolis in July 1968 by Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt, and others, grew into a national movement. In November 1969 a group calling itself Indians of All Tribes occupied the abandoned federal prison island of Alcatraz in San Francisco Bay, demanding that the land be returned; the occupation lasted until June 1971. In 1972 the Trail of Broken Treaties caravan occupied the Bureau of Indian Affairs in Washington, and in 1973, at Wounded Knee, South Dakota — the site of the 1890 massacre — Oglala Lakota and AIM members occupied the town for seventy-one days in protest against tribal corruption and federal policy. The confrontations were followed by legislation giving tribes more control over federal programs.

**Gay rights.** — On the night of 28 June 1969, police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York's Greenwich Village, and the patrons fought back, touching off three nights of rioting. The event is conventionally taken as the birth of the modern gay rights movement. Within a year, gay liberation groups had formed in several cities, and the first Pride marches were held in June 1970. The movement won an important early victory in 1973, when the American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its list of mental disorders.

## The ledger

**What the decade did.** — The sixties left durable achievements and durable wounds. Medicare, Medicaid, federal aid to schools, food stamps, and the legal machinery of the civil rights acts outlived the era and became part of the ordinary expectations of American life; the Voting Rights Act transformed Southern politics within a generation. The decade also left the war, the deaths, the distrust, and the polarization that Nixon had learned to use. Public confidence in the federal government, measured by the National Election Studies, fell from about three-quarters of Americans in 1964 to about one-third by 1976. Historians disagree about where to place the blame. George C. Herring treats the war as the product of a series of small, cautious decisions, each justified by the need to preserve American credibility, until no one could see a way out without humiliation; David Halberstam places more weight on the character and confidence of the men who made the decisions; Rick Perlstein finds in the backlash against the decade the beginning of a new conservative politics. The question the decade leaves is whether the promise and the war were ever separable, or whether the same confidence that built the Great Society also carried the country into Vietnam.

## Chronology

- **1960** — John F. Kennedy narrowly defeats Richard Nixon; the Greensboro sit-ins begin; the birth-control pill is approved.
- **1961** — The Bay of Pigs invasion fails; the Berlin Wall is built; the Peace Corps and the President's Commission on the Status of Women are created.
- **1962** — The Cuban Missile Crisis; Students for a Democratic Society issues the Port Huron Statement.
- **1963** — Betty Friedan publishes The Feminine Mystique; the Equal Pay Act; the March on Washington; the Diem coup; Kennedy is assassinated.
- **1964** — The Civil Rights Act and the tax cut; the Great Society speech at Ann Arbor; the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution; Johnson defeats Goldwater.
- **1965** — Medicare and Medicaid; the Elementary and Secondary Education Act; the Immigration and Nationality Act; Operation Rolling Thunder and the first combat troops; the Watts riot.
- **1966** — The National Organization for Women is founded; Stokely Carmichael popularizes "Black Power"; Miranda v. Arizona.
- **1967** — The march on the Pentagon; the "Summer of Love"; the Detroit and Newark riots; Loving v. Virginia.
- **1968** — The Tet Offensive; Johnson withdraws; King and Kennedy are assassinated; riots; the Chicago convention; Nixon is elected.
- **1969** — Nixon takes office; Vietnamization; the secret bombing of Cambodia; the "silent majority" speech; Stonewall; Woodstock; the Alcatraz occupation begins.
- **1970** — The invasion of Cambodia; Kent State and Jackson State; the first Pride march.
- **1971** — The Pentagon Papers are published; New York Times Co. v. United States; Calley is convicted for My Lai.
- **1972** — Nixon visits China and the Soviet Union; the Watergate break-in; the Equal Rights Amendment passes Congress; Nixon is re-elected.
- **1973** — The Paris Peace Accords; the Watergate hearings and the Saturday Night Massacre; Agnew resigns; Roe v. Wade; the Wounded Knee occupation; the War Powers Resolution.
- **1974** — United States v. Nixon; the House Judiciary Committee approves impeachment articles; Nixon resigns; Ford pardons him.
- **1975** — North Vietnamese forces capture Saigon; the last Americans are evacuated on 30 April.

## Questions to carry

- The Great Society promised to end poverty and racial injustice. Which of its programs lasted, and what does their survival say about the promise?
- Johnson won in 1964 as the peace candidate and escalated the war in 1965. How could both be true, and what does that say about how the public learns what its government is doing?
- The Warren Court expanded rights that many Americans did not want expanded. Can a democracy both protect minority rights and answer to majority opinion?
- The movements of the decade — Black, feminist, Chicano, Native, gay — each demanded recognition as well as equality. Did they strengthen one another, or compete for a hearing?
- Halberstam blames the men, Herring blames the system, Perlstein blames the backlash. Which account best explains how the promise of 1964 gave way to division and distrust?
