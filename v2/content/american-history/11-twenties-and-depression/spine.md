# The twenties and the Great Depression

**Period:** c. 1920–1939 · **Region:** The United States

**The question.** — On the afternoon of 28 July 1932, some fifteen thousand unemployed veterans of the First World War were camped in shacks and tents along the Anacostia River, within sight of the Capitol dome. They had come to Washington to demand early payment of a bonus Congress had promised for wartime service. That afternoon cavalry and infantry under General Douglas MacArthur, with tanks and tear gas, drove them out and burned the camp. The Bonus Army stood at the end of a decade that had begun with the Palmer raids and the Eighteenth Amendment. Between the two moments lies the problem that organizes this unit: what caused the Great Depression, and did the New Deal end it?

## The Red Scare and the politics of fear

**Revolution abroad, bombs at home.** — In November 1917 a small Bolshevik party seized power in Russia, and radicals elsewhere took heart from it. The spring of 1919 brought a wave of strikes and a series of anarchist bombings. In April mail bombs went to officials including Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer; in June a bomb damaged his house; in September 1920 a blast on Wall Street killed thirty-eight people. The bombings were the work of a handful of militants, but the response treated them as the edge of a mass conspiracy.

**The Palmer raids.** — Palmer made the suppression of radicalism his program, appointing J. Edgar Hoover to run a new Radical Division in the Bureau of Investigation. On 7 November 1919 federal agents and local police raided meeting halls in a dozen cities and arrested some 250 people. In January 1920 the raids widened to thirty-three cities and seized between four and six thousand people, many without warrants. In December 1919 the government put 249 deportees, among them the anarchists Emma Goldman and Alexander Berkman, aboard the transport *Buford* for Soviet Russia — the "Soviet Ark." Palmer predicted a revolutionary uprising for May Day 1920; when it did not come, his standing collapsed. The most enduring case was that of Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, Italian anarchists executed in 1927 after a murder trial that many judged unfair.

**Closing the gate.** — The fear of the foreigner produced the most restrictive immigration law in American history to that point. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 capped annual immigration from any country at 3 percent of its foreign-born residents counted in the 1910 census. The Immigration Act of 1924, known for its sponsors Representative Albert Johnson and Senator David Reed, cut the quota to 2 percent and moved the base year back to 1890. The choice of 1890 was not neutral: it was the last census before the great wave of immigration from southern and eastern Europe, so the quotas favored Britain, Germany, and Scandinavia and sharply reduced Italy, Poland, and Russia. From 1927 the law set a total ceiling of 150,000 a year, allotted by "national origins." Section 13(c) barred any "alien ineligible to citizenship," a category that included the Japanese. Immigration fell from roughly 800,000 in 1920–21 to about 150,000 a year.

**The second Ku Klux Klan.** — The Klan was refounded in 1915 on Stone Mountain, Georgia, by William Joseph Simmons, the same year that D. W. Griffith's film *The Birth of a Nation* romanticized the Klan of Reconstruction. It grew slowly until 1920, when Simmons hired professional organizers and the order spread far beyond the South. At its peak in the mid-1920s it claimed between three and five million members, with strength in Indiana, Oregon, Maine, and the cities of the Midwest as well as the South. Its targets were Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and black Americans, and it supported Prohibition. In August 1925 tens of thousands of Klansmen marched down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington. The order then fell apart quickly, undone by feuds and by the 1925 conviction of D. C. Stephenson, the Indiana leader, for kidnapping and rape. Membership had never been as deep as its numbers suggested, but the Klan showed how large a politics of racial and religious grievance could become.

## Prohibition

**The dry crusade.** — The Eighteenth Amendment, ratified in January 1919 and effective in January 1920, prohibited the manufacture, sale, and transport of intoxicating liquors. The Volstead Act, passed over President Wilson's veto in October 1919, defined "intoxicating" as anything above 0.5 percent alcohol, which made beer and wine illegal too. The amendment was the work of a long campaign by the Anti-Saloon League, founded in 1893 and led by Wayne Wheeler, and the Woman's Christian Temperance Union; by 1916 twenty-three states had already gone dry. Prohibitionists argued that drink destroyed families and that saloons corrupted city politics, and wartime rationing of grain added a practical reason. The Sixteenth Amendment of 1913, which created the federal income tax, had removed the government's dependence on the liquor excise.

**What Prohibition did.** — Prohibition never came close to stopping drinking. It moved it into speakeasies — New York City alone may have had tens of thousands — and created a vast illegal trade. Bootleggers smuggled liquor from Canada and the Caribbean, and gangs fought over the profits; in Chicago Al Capone built an organization that took in tens of millions a year and culminated in the St. Valentine's Day Massacre of February 1929, when seven men of a rival gang were lined up and shot. The government never had enough agents — the Prohibition Bureau had roughly fifteen hundred for the whole country — and enforcement was uneven and often corrupt. Prohibition divided the country along lines of region, class, and religion, and it made a drink into a badge of modern independence. It was repealed by the Twenty-first Amendment, ratified in December 1933, the only amendment ever approved by state conventions rather than state legislatures.

## The New Era

**Mass culture.** — In the 1920s the United States acquired, for the first time, a shared national culture delivered by machine. Radio station KDKA in Pittsburgh began commercial broadcasting in 1920; by 1929 about twelve million receivers were in American homes, and the networks NBC (1926) and CBS (1927) knit the country together with the same programs and advertisements. The movies grew from storefront nickelodeons into picture palaces, selling some eighty to a hundred million tickets a week by the late 1920s; *The Jazz Singer* (1927) brought synchronized sound. Advertising became a large industry, and radio carried the same heroes — Babe Ruth, Jack Dempsey, Red Grange — and the same celebrity, the aviator Charles Lindbergh, whose solo flight across the Atlantic in May 1927 made him the most famous man in the world.

**The consumer economy.** — Prosperity rested on buying. Automobile registrations rose from about eight million in 1920 to more than twenty-three million in 1929, a car for roughly every five people. Henry Ford's moving assembly line and his five-dollar day made the Model T cheap enough for ordinary families, and General Motors under Alfred Sloan answered Ford with annual model changes and installment financing. Credit was the key: a buyer could put a little down and pay the rest monthly, and by 1929 consumers owed several billion dollars on cars, furniture, radios, and appliances. The whole structure assumed that spending would keep rising; when it stopped, the debt remained.

**The Harlem Renaissance.** — Between 1910 and 1930 roughly a million and a half black southerners moved north and west in the Great Migration, and Harlem became the capital of black America. Alain Locke announced a "New Negro" in a 1925 anthology of that name, and the decade produced the poetry of Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen, and Claude McKay; the fiction of Zora Neale Hurston, whose *Their Eyes Were Watching God* appeared in 1937; and the jazz of Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, and Bessie Smith. Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association, brought to Harlem in 1916, drew huge crowds with a "Back to Africa" program before Garvey was convicted of mail fraud and deported. The movement was also shaped and limited by white patrons who funded writers and often expected a particular kind of "primitive" art; the Cotton Club, where Ellington played, admitted only white customers.

**The Scopes trial.** — In March 1925 Tennessee passed the Butler Act, which made it unlawful for a public school teacher to teach that humans descended from a lower order of animals. The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend anyone charged, and businessmen in the town of Dayton, hoping for publicity, recruited a substitute teacher named John T. Scopes to be the test case. The trial in July 1925 became a national spectacle: Clarence Darrow for Scopes and William Jennings Bryan, three times the Democratic candidate for president and a leader of Protestant fundamentalism, for the prosecution, with the journalist H. L. Mencken sending mocking dispatches north. The judge refused to let scientists testify, so Darrow put Bryan himself on the stand and questioned him about the literal truth of the Bible. Scopes was convicted and fined $100, and the conviction was later thrown out on a technicality; Bryan died days later. The case was less a referendum on biology than a contest over who had authority over the public schools and modern knowledge.

## Boom and crash

**The bull market.** — The stock market rose almost without interruption from 1924 to 1929, the Dow Jones industrial average climbing from about 100 to 381 by 3 September 1929. Much of the buying was on margin: a buyer could put down as little as 10 percent and borrow the rest from a broker, so a small fall in price could wipe out the buyer's stake and force a sale. Investment trusts and holding companies multiplied, sometimes built on little more than debt and paper. President Coolidge said in 1925 that "the chief business of the American people is business," and his treasury secretary, Andrew Mellon, cut taxes on the wealthy on the theory that investment would follow. The gains were concentrated: by 1929 the richest 1 percent of Americans received about a quarter of all income, while perhaps 60 percent of families lived below what was considered a subsistence level.

**The crash.** — The break came in the last week of October 1929. On Thursday the 24th, a record 12.9 million shares changed hands and a group of bankers briefly steadied prices. On Monday the 28th the Dow fell nearly 13 percent. On Tuesday the 29th — "Black Tuesday" — some 16 million shares were traded and the market lost roughly another 12 percent. By the middle of November the value of listed stocks had fallen by tens of billions of dollars. The crash destroyed paper wealth and confidence, but it did not by itself create the Depression; the economy was already weakening in 1929, and the collapse of the banks and the money supply did far more damage over the following years.

**What caused the Depression?** — This is a genuine historical argument. One set of explanations points to weaknesses inside the American economy: an unequal distribution of income that left too little purchasing power, overproduction, a fragile banking system of thousands of small uninsured banks, and heavy private debt. Another points outward, to the international order: war debts and reparations, the rigid gold standard, and the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of June 1930, which raised American duties and provoked retaliation. A third, associated with Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz in *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963), blames the Federal Reserve, which raised interest rates and stood by while banks failed, allowing the money supply to fall by about a third between 1929 and 1933. John Kenneth Galbraith, in *The Great Crash, 1929* (1955), emphasized speculative excess; Barry Eichengreen, in *Golden Fetters* (1992), argued that the gold standard transmitted the crisis and that recovery came when nations abandoned it. The causes were several, and their relative weight remains contested; the Depression was not a natural disaster but the result of choices.

## Hoover

**Hoover's response.** — Herbert Hoover, elected in 1928, was a mining engineer who believed in what he called "rugged individualism" and in voluntary cooperation rather than federal relief. He was not idle: he signed the Agricultural Marketing Act of 1929, which created a Federal Farm Board to buy surplus crops; he supported public works, including the beginning of Hoover Dam; in January 1932 he established the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to lend federal money to banks and railroads. But he refused direct federal payments to the unemployed, fearing that relief would sap their independence, and he accepted the Smoot-Hawley Tariff over the objections of economists. The measures came too late and reached too few. Between 1930 and 1933 some five thousand banks failed; by 1932 unemployment had reached roughly a quarter of the labor force, and shantytowns of the homeless were nicknamed "Hoovervilles." Hoover told the country that recovery was near, and the phrase "prosperity is just around the corner," though not his own, was hung on him.

**The Bonus Army.** — In the spring of 1932 some fifteen to twenty thousand veterans and their families came to Washington to demand that Congress pay immediately the bonus certificates issued for wartime service, which were not due until 1945. They camped on the Anacostia Flats. The House passed a bonus bill in June; the Senate defeated it, and most of the veterans went home. A few thousand stayed. On 28 July 1932 the government ordered the camps cleared. MacArthur, assisted by officers including Dwight Eisenhower and George Patton, used cavalry, infantry, tanks, and tear gas, then burned the shacks. Two veterans died, one of a gunshot, one of injuries from tear gas. The president had not ordered the assault on the main camp, but he defended it, and the images of soldiers driving veterans from the capital did him lasting political damage.

## The New Deal

**Roosevelt and the Hundred Days.** — In November 1932 Franklin D. Roosevelt, the governor of New York, defeated Hoover by 472 electoral votes to 59. By the time Roosevelt took the oath on 4 March 1933 the banking system was close to collapse: thousands of banks had failed since 1930, and the governors of most states had suspended banking by declaring holidays. Roosevelt's inaugural address promised "action, and action now" and told the country that "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself." Two days later he proclaimed a national bank holiday from 6 to 13 March, and Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act on 9 March; on 12 March Roosevelt gave the first of his radio "fireside chats." In June the Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial from investment banking and created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Roosevelt also took the country off the gold standard in April 1933, allowing the dollar to fall and prices to rise.

**The first New Deal.** — The early program tried many things at once. The Civilian Conservation Corps put young men to work planting trees and building trails. The Federal Emergency Relief Administration, run by Harry Hopkins, gave money to the states for direct relief. The Tennessee Valley Authority built dams and brought electricity to a poor region. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of May 1933 paid farmers to reduce their acreage in order to raise prices. The National Industrial Recovery Act of June 1933 created the National Recovery Administration under Hugh Johnson, which wrote codes for industries setting prices, wages, and hours and gave workers the right to organize; businesses that complied displayed a blue eagle. The Supreme Court struck down the NRA in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* in May 1935 and the AAA in *United States v. Butler* in January 1936. The first New Deal relieved distress and restored some confidence, but it had not produced recovery.

**The second New Deal.** — In 1935, with the Depression still deep, Roosevelt turned to a broader program. The Works Progress Administration, again under Hopkins, employed about eight and a half million people over eight years on roads, buildings, and, controversially, the arts. The Social Security Act, signed on 14 August 1935, created old-age insurance financed by a tax on wages, a federal-state system of unemployment insurance, and grants to the states for old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, and aid to the blind. The National Labor Relations Act, known as the Wagner Act, became law on 5 July 1935; it guaranteed private-sector workers the right to organize and bargain collectively and created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce it. The decade also produced demagogic alternatives: Huey Long of Louisiana, whose "Share Our Wealth" plan promised to cap fortunes and give every family a homestead, was assassinated in September 1935; Father Coughlin broadcast to millions. Under the Wagner Act, the Committee for Industrial Organization organized mass-production workers under John L. Lewis; the sit-down strike in Flint, Michigan, in the winter of 1936–37 won recognition for the United Auto Workers.

**Social Security and the Wagner Act.** — The two great laws of 1935 built a floor under many working Americans, but they were drawn with limits. Social Security's old-age insurance covered workers in commerce and industry and was funded by a payroll tax on workers and their bosses; the Act's definition of "employment" excluded agricultural labor and domestic service, the work of most black southerners and of many women, and the state-administered assistance programs left room for local discrimination. The Wagner Act protected workers who could organize under federal law but likewise left farm and domestic workers outside its reach. The result was a system that secured many white industrial workers while leaving others exposed, a distinction that would shape American politics for generations.

**The Dust Bowl and the Okies.** — The southern plains — western Kansas, eastern Colorado, the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, and eastern New Mexico — had been plowed up during the wheat boom of the 1910s and 1920s, tearing out the deep-rooted grass that held the soil. When drought began in 1931, the wind lifted the exposed earth into enormous clouds that darkened the sky and came to be called "black blizzards"; the worst, "Black Sunday," fell on 14 April 1935. The Soil Conservation Service, created that year, taught contour plowing and planted shelterbelts of trees to hold the ground. Roughly two and a half million people left the plains during the 1930s, many toward California; those from Oklahoma were called "Okies," though the migrants came from several states. The Farm Security Administration sent photographers, including Dorothea Lange, to record the migration; Lange's "Migrant Mother" of 1936 became its most famous image, and John Steinbeck's novel *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) carried the story further. The New Deal's farm program, by paying landowners to take land out of production, often helped the owners more than the tenants and sharecroppers, black and white, who were pushed off the land.

**The court-packing plan.** — Roosevelt won a second landslide in 1936, taking every state but Maine and Vermont. The Supreme Court, however, had been striking down his programs, and in February 1937 he proposed the Judicial Procedures Reform Bill, which would have let him appoint one new justice for every sitting justice over the age of seventy, up to six. He presented it as a workload measure, but everyone understood it as a plan to pack the Court, and even many allies objected. In March 1937 the Court upheld a Washington State minimum-wage law in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*, and in April it upheld the Wagner Act; Justice Owen Roberts, who had voted with the conservative bloc, changed sides, in what wags called "the switch in time that saved nine." The bill died in the Senate in July. Roosevelt lost political capital, and an alliance of Southern Democrats and Republicans that would check further reform began to take shape.

## The limits of the New Deal

**Recovery incomplete.** — The New Deal relieved suffering and reformed the banks, but it did not restore full employment. When Roosevelt cut spending in 1937 to balance the budget, the economy fell into a sharp recession in 1937–38 and unemployment climbed again, to about 19 percent. As late as 1940 unemployment was still around 15 percent. Full employment returned only with the Second World War and the enormous government spending it brought. Whether the New Deal "ended" the Depression therefore depends on the measure: it stabilized the economy and changed the role of the federal government, but it did not by itself produce recovery.

**For black Americans.** — The New Deal's benefits were unevenly shared. National Recovery Administration codes sometimes set lower wages for black workers than for white. Agricultural Adjustment Act payments went to landowners and displaced tenants and sharecroppers, many of them black. Social Security and the Wagner Act excluded farm and domestic labor. The Federal Housing Administration and the Home Owners' Loan Corporation marked black neighborhoods as poor credit risks — the practice known as redlining — and public housing was segregated. At the same time, black voters, most of whom had been loyal to the party of Lincoln since the Civil War, moved toward the Democrats in the 1930s, and a majority of northern black voters supported Roosevelt by 1936. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph, the head of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, threatened a mass march on Washington unless the defense industries stopped discriminating; Roosevelt responded with Executive Order 8802, which barred discrimination in defense employment and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee.

**Did the New Deal end the Depression?** — The classic interpretation, argued at length by Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr. in *The Age of Roosevelt* (1957–1960), treats the New Deal as a pragmatic middle way that saved both capitalism and democracy by taming the market and giving the federal government a permanent role in welfare. A generation of economic historians has been more skeptical: Friedman and Schwartz held that the New Deal's monetary and regulatory choices prolonged the slump, and others have noted that recovery waited on the war. The debate is not only about economics. Ira Katznelson's *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005) argues that the New Deal's programs were deliberately built to exclude the black South, so that the benefits flowed to white workers and a white middle class. The question the unit began with thus has more than one honest answer.

## Chronology

- **1919** — The Eighteenth Amendment is ratified and the Volstead Act defines intoxicating liquor; a wave of strikes and bombings sets off the first Red Scare.
- **November 1919 – January 1920** — The Palmer raids arrest thousands of suspected radicals; 249 deportees sail on the *Buford* in December.
- **1921** — The Emergency Quota Act sets the first numerical limits on immigration.
- **May 1924** — The Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed) cuts quotas, uses the 1890 census, and bars aliens ineligible to citizenship.
- **July 1925** — The Scopes trial in Dayton, Tennessee, pits Clarence Darrow against William Jennings Bryan.
- **1925** — Alain Locke publishes *The New Negro*; the Klan marches in Washington and then declines after D. C. Stephenson's conviction.
- **May 1927** — Charles Lindbergh flies solo across the Atlantic; *The Jazz Singer* later that year brings sound to the movies.
- **3 September 1929** — The Dow Jones average peaks at 381.
- **24–29 October 1929** — The crash: record selling on Black Thursday, Black Monday, and Black Tuesday.
- **June 1930** — The Smoot-Hawley Tariff raises duties and deepens the slump in world trade.
- **1930–1933** — Roughly five thousand banks fail; unemployment reaches about a quarter of the labor force.
- **January 1932** — The Reconstruction Finance Corporation is created.
- **28 July 1932** — The army drives the Bonus Army from Washington.
- **November 1932** — Roosevelt defeats Hoover; he takes office on 4 March 1933 and declares a bank holiday.
- **May–June 1933** — The AAA, the TVA, and the NRA are created in the Hundred Days.
- **May 1935** — *Schechter* strikes down the NRA; **July 1935** the Wagner Act; **August 1935** the Social Security Act.
- **April 1935** — "Black Sunday" dust storm; the Soil Conservation Service is created.
- **February–July 1937** — Roosevelt's court-packing plan fails; the Court upholds the Wagner Act and a minimum-wage law.
- **1937–1938** — A sharp recession follows cuts in federal spending.
- **June 1941** — Executive Order 8802 bars discrimination in defense employment.

## Questions to carry

- What did Americans in the 1920s fear, and how did that fear shape the laws they passed?
- Prohibition and the Immigration Act of 1924 both restricted personal liberty in the name of reform. What did their supporters believe they were protecting?
- The crash of 1929 is the date everyone remembers. How much of the Depression does it actually explain?
- Hoover and Roosevelt disagreed about direct federal relief. What did each assume about the obligations of government and the character of the unemployed?
- The New Deal changed the federal government permanently. Whom did it help, whom did it leave out, and how was that decided?
