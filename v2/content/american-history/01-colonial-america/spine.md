# Colonial America

**Period:** c. 1607–1763 · **Region:** British North America

**Two landings.** — On 13 May 1607 three ships of the Virginia Company of London — the *Susan Constant*, the *Godspeed*, and the *Discovery* — tied up on a low, swampy peninsula in the river the English called the James, some sixty kilometres from Chesapeake Bay. About a hundred men and boys went ashore, threw up a wooden fort, and named the place Jamestown. Thirteen years later, in November 1620, a single ship, the *Mayflower*, anchored off Cape Cod with 102 passengers, most of them religious dissenters who had left England for the Netherlands and now meant to plant a colony farther south. Half of them were dead within a year. These two settlements are where the English history of what became the United States is usually said to begin, but they were not one society, and for most of the next century the people who lived in them had little reason to think of themselves as a single people. The question this unit returns to is how, by the middle of the eighteenth century, thirteen colonies with different economies, churches, and governments had come to share a sense of common cause.

## The first footholds

**A company's colony.** — Virginia was a business. The Virginia Company of London was a joint-stock venture — investors pooled capital in return for shares in whatever the colony produced — and its charter of 1606 granted land between the thirty-fourth and forty-first parallels. The investors expected gold, a passage to the Pacific, or at least a profitable trade, and they expected it quickly. What the first settlers found was a tidewater country of forests, marshes, and a paramount chiefdom the English called Powhatan, which may have held fourteen thousand people in some thirty towns along the rivers. The colony's first years were a catastrophe: the site was chosen for its defensibility against Spanish ships, but its water was brackish and its mosquitoes carried malaria; gentlemen among the colonists refused to farm; and the company's communal store gave every man an incentive to shirk. Captain John Smith, who took charge of the colony's affairs in 1608, imposed a blunt rule: "he that will not worke shall not eate." The winter of 1609–10, the "starving time," reduced perhaps five hundred colonists to about sixty.

**Tobacco rescues Virginia.** — The crop that made Virginia viable was tobacco. John Rolfe, who arrived in 1610, began experimenting with a West Indian variety, and by 1612 he was curing a leaf that Europeans would pay for. Tobacco exhausted the soil, which meant planters constantly needed fresh land, and fresh land meant a labor supply. The company answered with the **headright** — a grant of fifty acres to anyone who paid his own passage, and another fifty for each servant he imported. The result was a rush of migration and a plantation economy strung along the rivers, where ships could load at a planter's own wharf. In July 1619 the colony's governor convened an assembly of elected burgesses at Jamestown, the first representative legislature in English America. In August of that same year a Dutch vessel sold the colony "twenty and odd Negroes," the first Africans known to have been landed in Virginia; their legal status was not yet fixed, and some of them seem to have been treated much like the white servants around them. In 1622 a coordinated assault by Powhatan's people killed some 347 colonists, about a quarter of the population, and in 1624 the Crown revoked the company's charter and made Virginia a royal colony.

**Plymouth and the compact.** — The settlers of Plymouth were not a company. They were Separatists — Protestants who had concluded that the Church of England was beyond reform and had formed their own congregations, first at Scrooby in Nottinghamshire and then in Leiden. Having failed to secure a patent for their own land, they sailed in 1620 with a mixed company that included non-Separatist "strangers" hired by the investors. Before going ashore, forty-one of the adult men signed an agreement that became known as the Mayflower Compact. It is short, and its language is legal rather than philosophical: the signers "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick," promising to "enact, constitute, and frame, such just and equal Laws ... as shall be thought most meet and convenient for the general Good of the Colony." Its purpose was immediate and prudential — the patent was for Virginia, not New England, and without some acknowledged authority the company might have dissolved into quarrels — but the principle it recorded, that a government derives its just powers from the consent of the governed, would be quoted for centuries. The first winter killed about half the settlers. The survivors were helped by Squanto, a Patuxet who had been carried to England and back, and by Massasoit, the Wampanoag sachem with whom they concluded a treaty in 1621. Plymouth never received a royal charter of its own; it was absorbed into Massachusetts in 1691.

**The "city upon a hill."** — The largest and most influential New England colony was Massachusetts Bay, chartered in 1629. Its first governor, John Winthrop, sailed on the *Arbella* in 1630 with a fleet of eleven ships, and before landing he wrote out a lay sermon, "A Model of Christian Charity." Winthrop's argument was social rather than political: God had "soe disposed of the condition of mankind, as in all times some must be rich, some poore," and the rich and the poor were bound to one another by "Justice and Mercy." The colonists, he wrote, had "entered into Covenant with Him for this worke"; if they kept it, God would dwell among them, and "men shall say of succeeding plantations, 'the Lord make it likely that of New England.' For wee must consider that wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us." The phrase was a warning, not a boast. Between 1630 and 1640 some twenty thousand Puritans crossed to New England in what historians call the Great Migration, settling Boston and the towns around the bay. Dissenters were not tolerated for long: Roger Williams, who argued for the separation of church and magistrate, was banished in 1635 and founded Providence, in what became Rhode Island; Anne Hutchinson, who held that salvation came from an inward experience of grace rather than outward obedience, was tried and banished in 1638.

**The other colonies.** — Massachusetts was only the beginning. Connecticut's towns, founded from Massachusetts in the 1630s, adopted the Fundamental Orders in 1639. Maryland was settled in 1634 by Cecil Calvert, Lord Baltimore, as a haven for English Catholics; its assembly passed an Act Concerning Religion in 1649. The Dutch had planted New Netherland on the Hudson in the 1620s; the English took it in 1664 and renamed it New York, granting New Jersey to two proprietors. Pennsylvania was granted in 1681 to William Penn, a Quaker, who advertised it across Europe as a place of religious liberty; Philadelphia became the largest city in the colonies. Carolina, chartered in 1663, split into North and South, and Charleston became the seat of a rice-planting elite. Georgia, the last of the thirteen, was founded in 1733 as a refuge for debtors and a buffer against Spanish Florida, and banned slavery until 1750. By 1760 the thirteen colonies held about 1.6 million people, roughly a fifth of them of African descent, nearly all enslaved.

## Three regions

**New England.** — New England's soil was thin and rocky, its winters long, and its farms small; a family might work forty or fifty acres and grow only what it needed, with a little to sell. What the region did have was the sea: cod, mackerel, and whales; timber for ships and barrels; and a merchant marine that carried fish to the West Indies and brought back sugar, molasses, and rum. Because the land could not support a plantation economy, New England developed towns: compact settlements around a meetinghouse, with fields and woodlots apportioned by the town and a militia company. Harvard College was founded in 1636, and a 1647 Massachusetts law required every town of fifty households to hire a teacher. The region's churches were Congregational — each congregation governed itself — and its politics were conducted in town meetings and, after 1631, by a franchise limited to church members.

**The middle colonies.** — Between New England and the Chesapeake lay New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware, the most fertile and the most mixed region. The soil grew wheat, rye, oats, and barley in quantity, and the region became the breadbasket of the British Atlantic, shipping flour to the West Indies and southern Europe. It was also the most religiously and ethnically plural: Dutch Reformed, Quaker, Anglican, Presbyterian, Lutheran, Mennonite, German Baptist, and Jewish communities lived within a few days' ride of one another, and the middle colonies practiced a degree of toleration that New England and the Chesapeake did not.

**The Chesapeake and the South.** — The southern colonies — Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas, later joined by Georgia — were built on staple crops grown for export. Tobacco dominated the Chesapeake; rice and indigo dominated the Carolina low country after the 1690s. These were plantation economies, worked by bound labour, and they produced a society very different from New England's: settlement scattered along rivers rather than clustered in towns, a small planter elite at the top, a thin professional class, an Anglican establishment, county courts rather than town meetings, and a demography of early death. South Carolina's rice fields were so deadly that planters often lived in Charleston and left the work to overseers; by the 1720s enslaved Africans and their descendants outnumbered whites in the colony.

## Labour, servitude, and slavery

**Indentured servitude.** — Most white immigrants to the Chesapeake before 1680 came not as free settlers but as **indentured servants**: people who sold four to seven years of their labour in exchange for passage, and who were legally bound to their masters until their term expired. Servants could be bought and sold, could not marry without permission, could be whipped for running away, and received at the end of their term a set of "freedom dues," usually including a small grant of land. It was the labour system that built the tobacco colony, but it was temporary, which limited its usefulness to planters who wanted a permanent, inheritable workforce.

**The turn to African slavery.** — The first Africans in Virginia, in 1619, did not arrive as chattel slaves in the modern sense; they appear in early records in ways that resemble servitude. Over the next half-century the law changed. A Virginia statute of 1662 declared that children born in the colony would follow the status of their mother — *partus sequitur ventrem* — which made slavery heritable and attached it to race rather than to religious or national origin. A law of 1667 provided that baptism did not free a slave, and laws of 1691 and 1705 restricted the movement, marriage, assembly, and property-holding of Black Virginians and made slavery a permanent condition. By the early eighteenth century the English colonies had built a system of **chattel slavery** — human beings treated as movable property, bought and sold, and passed by inheritance — justified in law and sermon by the claim that Africans and their descendants were a distinct and inferior people.

**Bacon's Rebellion and the turn.** — The event most often credited with accelerating that turn is Bacon's Rebellion. In 1676 Nathaniel Bacon, a young planter on the frontier, raised an army of small farmers, servants, and even some enslaved men against the governor, Sir William Berkeley, after Berkeley refused to authorize a campaign against the Susquehannock and other Native peoples. Bacon's forces attacked friendly and hostile Indians alike, marched on Jamestown, and burned it. Bacon died of dysentery that autumn, and Berkeley hanged some twenty-three of the rebels, but the underlying grievance — a laboring class of servants and freedmen with little land and no voice, allied across lines of colour — frightened the planter elite. In the interpretation of the historian Edmund S. Morgan, the planters responded by replacing white indentured servants with enslaved Africans, dividing the labouring population by race and offering poor whites a stake in a hierarchy that placed them above Africans. The chronology is more complicated than the thesis: the shift began before 1676 and continued for decades, driven by the falling price of enslaved people, the rising cost of English servants, and the growth of the Atlantic slave trade. But Morgan's argument remains the most influential account of why freedom and slavery grew up together.

**Resistance.** — Enslaved people resisted. They broke tools, ran away, preserved African languages and religious practices, and occasionally rebelled. In September 1739, a group of about twenty enslaved men near the Stono River in South Carolina seized weapons, killed more than twenty whites, and marched toward Spanish Florida, where they had been promised freedom; the militia caught and killed most of them. The Stono Rebellion, the largest slave uprising in the British mainland colonies, was followed by a harsh new slave code. In New York in 1741 a supposed conspiracy led to the execution of some thirty people, most of them Black, on evidence that later historians have found deeply suspect. Fear of rebellion shaped colonial law as much as the demand for labour did.

## Family, religion, and daily life

**Households.** — In New England, the household was the basic unit of production and of religious instruction. A married couple might raise seven or eight children, and a woman's life was organized around childbearing, cooking, spinning, weaving, dairying, and the garden. Because so many children survived to adulthood, New England's population grew rapidly without immigration after 1640. In the Chesapeake, by contrast, the household was less stable: high mortality meant frequent remarriage, stepchildren, and orphans, and a widow might inherit and manage a plantation, at least until she remarried.

**The Puritan churches.** — New England's religious order rested on the idea of the covenant. A congregation was a body of "visible saints" who had given an account of their conversion and bound themselves to one another and to God; the minister was chosen by the congregation, and the civil government was expected to support the church without controlling it. Because the first generation's children could not always testify to an experience of grace, the churches adopted a "Half-Way Covenant" in 1662, allowing the grandchildren of the founding members to be baptized even if their parents had not become full members. Some historians have read the seventeenth century as a story of "declension," of declining piety and rising worldliness; others have found the churches adapting and persisting, and have warned that the declension narrative was itself a Puritan rhetorical device.

**Salem, 1692.** — The most notorious episode of colonial religion was the witchcraft panic at Salem, Massachusetts. In 1692 a group of young girls accused neighbours of afflicting them; the courts admitted "spectral evidence" — testimony that the accused's spirit had tormented the victim — and within a year some two hundred people were accused, nineteen were hanged, and one man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death under stones for refusing to plead. The panic ended only when the governor's wife was accused and the clergy, including Increase Mather, condemned spectral evidence. Historians have offered many explanations: the trauma of recent Indian wars on the northern frontier; a factional feud in Salem Village over its minister and its independence from Salem Town; and the economic tensions of a growing, unequal community. In *Salem Possessed* (1974), Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum argued that the accusers and the accused lined up on opposite sides of a village divided by geography, wealth, and family.

## The eighteenth century

**The Great Awakening.** — In the 1730s and 1740s a wave of religious revival swept the colonies. Jonathan Edwards, a minister at Northampton, Massachusetts, preached a Calvinist theology of the "new birth," and his sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," delivered at Enfield, Connecticut, in 1741, became the most famous sermon in American history. George Whitefield, an English itinerant, toured the colonies from Georgia to New England in 1739 and 1740, preaching in fields and town squares to crowds of thousands. The revival split churches into "New Lights," who welcomed the emotional preaching and the itinerants, and "Old Lights," who distrusted both, and it produced new colleges — Princeton, Brown, Rutgers, and Dartmouth all grew out of the awakening. It also created a shared religious experience and a common set of evangelical heroes that crossed colonial boundaries. Some historians, notably Jon Butler, have argued that the awakening's unity has been exaggerated and that it was a series of local revivals rather than a single movement; either way, it was the first popular movement to reach across the colonies.

**The Enlightenment.** — Alongside the revivals came the ideas of the European Enlightenment. Benjamin Franklin, a Boston printer's apprentice who ran away to Philadelphia, embodied the new learning: he founded a debating club called the Junto in 1727 and a subscription library in 1731, published *Poor Richard's Almanack* from 1732 to 1758, helped found the American Philosophical Society in 1743, and conducted experiments with electricity that made him famous on both sides of the Atlantic. The colleges of the eighteenth century taught natural philosophy and the natural rights theory of John Locke, whose *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) argued that legitimate government rests on consent and that people retain the right to resist tyranny. This was not a secular society — most colonists remained devout — but reason, observation, and print had become instruments of public argument.

**Print, post, and a common conversation.** — The first continuous newspaper in the colonies, the *Boston News-Letter*, appeared in 1704; by the 1760s there were more than twenty, and they reprinted one another's stories, so that an argument begun in a Boston paper could be answered in a Philadelphia one. Franklin, appointed deputy postmaster general for the colonies in 1753, improved the mail routes, and the post carried newspapers free of charge. By the middle of the century a colonist in Massachusetts and a colonist in Georgia might read the same news, sermon, and political essay, and might think of themselves as participants in a single conversation — even as their churches, crops, and governments remained different.

## Empire, war, and the question of union

**How the colonies were governed.** — By 1763 the thirteen colonies had three kinds of government. Eight were royal colonies, governed by a governor appointed by the Crown and a legislature elected by property-holding men; three — Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Delaware — were proprietary, still owned by a proprietor; and two — Connecticut and Rhode Island — were charter colonies that elected their own governors. In every colony the elected assembly controlled the purse and used that control to bargain with the governor, and the franchise was limited to men of property. The Crown governed trade through the Navigation Acts, a series of laws beginning in 1651 that required colonial exports to travel in English or colonial ships and "enumerated" goods such as tobacco, sugar, and rice to be shipped first to England. For long stretches the laws were loosely enforced, a policy later called "salutary neglect," and the colonies grew used to managing their own affairs.

**The French and Indian War.** — The last of the great imperial wars began in 1754 in the Ohio Valley, where the French built forts to link Canada to Louisiana and Virginians claimed the same land. A young Virginia colonel, George Washington, was defeated at Fort Necessity that July; the next year General Edward Braddock's army was ambushed and destroyed near the Monongahela. The war went badly for Britain until William Pitt took charge and poured money and troops into North America. In September 1759 the British general James Wolfe defeated the French at Quebec on the Plains of Abraham, and Montreal fell in 1760. The Treaty of Paris, signed in February 1763, expelled France from mainland North America: Britain gained Canada and all French territory east of the Mississippi, and Spain ceded Florida in exchange for the return of Cuba. In the same year, an uprising of Native peoples under the Ottawa leader Pontiac alarmed the British, and the Crown issued the Proclamation of 1763, forbidding colonial settlement west of the Appalachian divide.

**The Albany Plan and the first idea of union.** — In June 1754, as the war began, delegates from seven colonies met at Albany, New York, to coordinate relations with the Iroquois Confederacy. Benjamin Franklin proposed a "Plan of Union" that would have created a grand council of delegates from each colony and a president-general appointed by the Crown, with power over defence, Indian relations, and western settlement. The plan was rejected by the colonial assemblies, which feared losing their powers, and by the Crown, which feared creating a rival government; but it was the first serious proposal for a continental union, and Franklin's woodcut of a snake cut into pieces, captioned "Join, or Die," became the most famous political image of the period.
## One people, or thirteen?

**What held them together.** — The colonies that Britain governed in 1763 were not a nation. They had no common church, economy, or government; a New England town and a Carolina rice plantation had less in common than either had with parts of Britain. What they shared was a language, a legal inheritance, a mostly Protestant religious culture, and a set of British liberties they believed made them the freest people in the world. They were connected by trade, the post, newspapers, the great revivals, and the shared experience of the French and Indian War, in which colonists from several colonies fought side by side under British commanders. When the war ended, Britain faced an enormous debt and looked to the colonies to help pay it. Within two years Parliament passed the Stamp Act, and colonists discovered that the habits of self-government they had built over a century and a half were not something the empire meant to leave alone. The question of how thirteen separate colonies came to think of themselves as one people would be answered less by the century of settlement than by the decade of conflict that followed.

## Chronology

- **1606** — James I charters the Virginia Company of London.
- **13 May 1607** — Colonists land at Jamestown and build a fort.
- **1609–10** — The "starving time" reduces the Virginia colony to about sixty people.
- **1612** — John Rolfe begins to cure a marketable tobacco crop.
- **1619** — The first House of Burgesses meets at Jamestown; the first Africans are landed in Virginia.
- **11 November 1620** — The Mayflower Compact is signed off Cape Cod; Plymouth is founded.
- **1622** — A Powhatan assault kills some 347 Virginia colonists.
- **1624** — Virginia becomes a royal colony.
- **1629–40** — The Great Migration carries about twenty thousand Puritans to Massachusetts Bay.
- **1630** — John Winthrop writes "A Model of Christian Charity" and founds Boston.
- **1634** — Maryland is settled as a Catholic haven.
- **1636** — Roger Williams founds Providence; Harvard College is established.
- **1649** — Maryland passes its Act Concerning Religion.
- **1662** — Virginia law makes slavery hereditary through the mother.
- **1664** — England takes New Netherland and renames it New York.
- **1675–76** — King Philip's War devastates New England.
- **1676** — Bacon's Rebellion burns Jamestown.
- **1681** — William Penn receives Pennsylvania.
- **1691–92** — The Salem witchcraft trials; nineteen are hanged.
- **1704** — The *Boston News-Letter*, the first continuous colonial newspaper, begins publication.
- **1739** — The Stono Rebellion in South Carolina; George Whitefield tours the colonies.
- **1741** — Jonathan Edwards preaches "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God."
- **1754** — Washington is defeated at Fort Necessity; the Albany Congress proposes a plan of union.
- **1759** — Wolfe takes Quebec.
- **1763** — The Treaty of Paris ends the French and Indian War; Pontiac's Rebellion and the Proclamation of 1763 follow.

## Questions to carry

- The Mayflower Compact and the Virginia House of Burgesses both created self-government. What did each assume about who was entitled to govern, and who was left out?
- Winthrop's "city upon a hill" is often quoted as a promise. Why did he frame it as a warning, and what does that change about how the colonists understood their mission?
- Morgan argued that Bacon's Rebellion pushed Virginia toward racial slavery. What evidence would confirm that argument, and what evidence would count against it?
- The Great Awakening is sometimes described as the first shared American experience. How much did it actually unite the colonies, and how much did it divide them?
- By 1763 the colonies shared a language, a press, and a war. Was that enough to make them one people, or did union only become real in response to British policy after the war?
