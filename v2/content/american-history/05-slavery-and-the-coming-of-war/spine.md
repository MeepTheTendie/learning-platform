# Slavery and the coming of the Civil War

**Period:** c. 1820–1860 · **Region:** The United States

## King Cotton

**The gin and the cotton kingdom.** — In 1793 Eli Whitney built a machine that pulled the sticky green seeds out of short-staple upland cotton. Before the gin, a worker cleaned about a pound of cotton a day by hand; with it, fifty. The machine made cotton profitable across the interior South, where the soil and the long growing season suited the short-staple variety. The American crop rose from roughly 3,000 bales in 1790 to nearly four million in 1860, moving by river and rail to New Orleans, Mobile, Savannah, and Charleston, and from there to the mills of Lancashire and New England. By 1860 cotton made up well over half the value of all exports from the United States, and the country supplied about two-thirds of the cotton used in the world. On 4 March 1858 Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina gave the crop its famous title: "Cotton is king."

**The plantation and the gang.** — Cotton was worked by enslaved people in gangs, from first light until dark, under a driver drawn from their own ranks and an overseer paid to make them move. A planter with fifty or a hundred people lived at the centre of a small forced community: field hands, house servants, a blacksmith, a carpenter, a cook, and children too young to work full days. Most slaveholding families held fewer than ten people and worked beside them; a minority of about 46,000 planters held twenty or more, and that minority held roughly half of all the enslaved. In the rice country of the Carolina and Georgia lowlands planters used the task system, assigning each worker a measured daily job; in the cotton belt the gang system prevailed. The whip was the sanction behind both, and enslaved people were fed largely on cornmeal and salt pork and housed in dirt-floored cabins.

**The domestic slave trade.** — Congress banned the Atlantic slave trade effective 1 January 1808, but the ban did nothing to stop the sale of people already in the country. As the tobacco and wheat economy of the Upper South weakened and the cotton economy of the Deep South boomed, a vast internal trade carried people south and west. Historians estimate that between 1790 and 1860 roughly one million enslaved people were moved from the Upper South to the Lower South — the largest forced migration in American history. The traffic ran through markets and pens at Richmond, Alexandria, Baltimore, Charleston, Montgomery, Natchez, and above all New Orleans. Solomon Northup, a free Black man from New York kidnapped in Washington in 1841 and sold in Louisiana, described being shipped in a coffle and renamed "Platt." The trade broke families deliberately: a husband sold to a cotton state might never see his wife or children again, and the fear of sale disciplined everyone who remained.

**Slavery as an economic system.** — Enslaved people were the largest single form of private property in the South, worth more than the region's land and its railroads and factories combined. The price of a prime field hand rose from a few hundred dollars in the early republic to roughly $1,200 to $1,800 by the late 1850s. Because they could be mortgaged and seized for debt, enslaved people served as the South's credit system. The defence of slavery was therefore a matter of money as well as racial belief, and even Southerners who owned no slaves had reason to fear a world in which such property could be taken away.

## The world the slaves made

**Work, food, and the lash.** — On a cotton plantation the hands were in the field before sunrise and left it after sunset, with a short break at noon; during picking season every pair of hands was counted and weighed, and a short weight brought the whip. Women worked the same hours as men and were whipped the same way, while also bearing and nursing children. Punishment was public, because its purpose was to be seen. But the record also shows how much of life the system did not directly control. Enslaved people hunted and fished, kept gardens and chickens, and traded in town on Sundays; they made their own clothes and quilts and passed down songs, recipes, and stories. Frederick Douglass, born into slavery in Talbot County, Maryland, in 1818, wrote that he never knew his birthday, that his mother was taken from him before he could know her, and that she walked twelve miles at night to lie beside him.

**Family and kin.** — Marriage between enslaved people had no legal standing; a slaveholder could sell husband and wife apart at will. Even so, enslaved people married, named their children for kin, and built networks that stretched across farms. A child born to an enslaved woman belonged to her owner, not her husband. The threat of sale was constant, and the ache of separation runs through the era's testimony.

**The invisible church.** — Christianity reached enslaved people through the Second Great Awakening, but planters preached a version that told servants to obey their masters. Enslaved people read the same book differently. They met in secret "hush harbors" in the woods, and they made the Exodus — the escape of Israel from bondage in Egypt — the central story of their faith. From those meetings came the spirituals: "Go Down, Moses," "Steal Away," "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot." The songs carried double meanings, at once prayers and coded signals, and they passed orally because teaching an enslaved person to read was forbidden across most of the South. Douglass recalled the songs of the people walking to the Great House Farm and insisted that Northerners who took them as proof of contentment had it backwards: "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy."

**Resistance and rebellion.** — Enslaved people resisted in ways small and large: working slowly, breaking tools, feigning illness, stealing food, running away for days or weeks, and occasionally setting fire to a barn. A few rose in open rebellion. In August 1831 Nat Turner, a preacher in Southampton County, Virginia, led about seventy enslaved and free Black people in a rising that killed some fifty-five white men, women, and children over two days before it was crushed. Turner was captured, tried, and hanged in November. Virginia's legislature debated gradual emancipation in the winter of 1831–32 and rejected it, choosing instead to tighten the slave codes, ban the teaching of enslaved people to read, and restrict free Black assembly. The largest consequence of Turner's rebellion was not freedom but a harsher law.

## The antislavery movement

**Colonization and immediatism.** — The first organized American answer to slavery was not abolition but removal. The American Colonization Society, founded in 1816–17, raised money to send free Black Americans to a colony in West Africa, which became Liberia in 1822; its supporters included slaveholders who wanted free Black people gone and clergymen who thought emancipation had to be gradual. Most Black Americans rejected the scheme. A younger generation of white reformers concluded that slavery was a sin to be repented at once, and from about 1830 the movement turned to immediate abolition.

**Garrison and The Liberator.** — On 1 January 1831 William Lloyd Garrison published the first issue of The Liberator in Boston. His language was uncompromising: he demanded immediate emancipation, denounced slaveholders as criminals, and refused to accept that the Constitution protected slavery, calling it "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell." In 1833 he helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society, which sent speakers and pamphlets across the North. Abolitionists were a small minority even in the North, and they were attacked for it: mobs broke up meetings, and in 1837 a pro-slavery crowd killed the printer Elijah Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois. Congress, flooded with petitions, adopted a "gag rule" from 1836 to 1844 that set them aside without discussion.

**Douglass and the slave narrative.** — The most powerful voice of the movement was a man who had escaped it. Frederick Douglass fled Baltimore in September 1838, disguised in sailor's clothes, and reached New Bedford, Massachusetts. In 1845 he published the Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave, written by himself — a book that put lie to the claim that enslaved people were content or incapable of letters. Because the book named his former owner and his route, it endangered him; he sailed for Britain and returned in 1847 after supporters bought his freedom. That year he began publishing The North Star in Rochester, New York. His account of learning to read, of his 1833 fight with the overseer Edward Covey, and of the songs in the pine woods made the enslaved person an author rather than an object of pity.

**The underground railroad.** — Escape was the most direct resistance and the hardest to sustain. A loose network of free Black people, Quakers, and white abolitionists helped fugitives move north by night and by stages, from one safe house to the next; the name came from the language of trains, with "conductors" and "stations." Harriet Tubman, who escaped from Maryland in 1849, returned about thirteen times and guided some seventy people to freedom, carrying a pistol and, by her own account, never losing a passenger. The numbers who escaped were probably in the tens of thousands, and the routes led increasingly to Canada after 1850. But the network's political weight was out of proportion to its size, because every successful escape advertised that slavery was neither natural nor secure.

## The political crisis, 1846–1852

**The Wilmot Proviso and the territories.** — The Mexican War, which ended in 1848 with the United States taking vast new land in the West, reopened the question the Missouri Compromise of 1820 had been meant to settle: whether slavery would be allowed to expand. In August 1846 Representative David Wilmot of Pennsylvania moved that slavery be forbidden in any territory acquired from Mexico. The proviso failed in the Senate, but the vote revealed a new alignment — Southerners of both parties against it, most Northerners for it — and from that point the parties could no longer contain the issue. Some Northern Democrats, including Senator Lewis Cass of Michigan, proposed "popular sovereignty": let the settlers of each territory decide. The formula sounded democratic and left the timing of any decision unclear.

**The Compromise of 1850.** — When California applied for statehood as a free state, the Union seemed to come apart. Henry Clay of Kentucky assembled a package of measures; Daniel Webster of Massachusetts spoke on 7 March 1850 for compromise in the name of the Union; John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, too ill to read his own speech, had it delivered against any restriction on slavery; and William Seward of New York answered with a "higher law" than the Constitution. Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois broke Clay's package into separate bills and drove them through Congress that September. California entered as a free state; Utah and New Mexico were organized under popular sovereignty; Texas gave up its claim to New Mexico for federal assumption of its debt; the slave trade, though not slavery, was abolished in the District of Columbia; and a far harsher Fugitive Slave Act was passed. The compromise saved the Union for a decade, but it bought the time with a law the North could not stomach.

**The Fugitive Slave Act.** — The Act of 18 September 1850 put the recovery of fugitives in federal hands. A claimant could seize an alleged fugitive without a warrant and bring the person before a federal commissioner, who decided the case in a summary hearing. The alleged fugitive could not testify; no jury was required; and the commissioner received ten dollars if he granted the certificate of removal but only five if he refused. Section 5 commanded all "good citizens" to aid in enforcement and made marshals liable for a fugitive's value if one escaped. Section 7 punished anyone who harbored or helped a fugitive with a fine of up to $1,000 and six months in prison, plus $1,000 in damages to the owner for each person lost. In 1851 Thomas Sims was marched out of Boston under guard; in 1854 Anthony Burns was returned to Virginia escorted by two thousand soldiers, while a crowd in Milwaukee rescued Joshua Glover. The Act radicalized the North more than any abolitionist pamphlet had.

**Uncle Tom's Cabin.** — In 1851 Harriet Beecher Stowe, a clergyman's daughter from Connecticut who had lived in Cincinnati across the river from a slave state, began publishing a serial in the National Era. The book appeared in March 1852 and sold 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year and more than two million worldwide within five years. Its scenes of Eliza crossing the Ohio River on the ice and of the brutal overseer Simon Legree entered the common speech. Stowe's South was sentimental and her Black characters often saintly, and Southerners answered with novels of their own. But the book did what the Fugitive Slave Act had begun: it made slavery vivid to readers who had never seen it, and it convinced many Northerners that a system capable of such cruelty could not be left alone.

## The unraveling, 1854–1857

**The Kansas-Nebraska Act.** — In 1854 Stephen Douglas, wanting a transcontinental railroad through Chicago, organized the Nebraska country into two territories, Kansas and Nebraska, and applied popular sovereignty. Because both lay north of the 36°30′ line that the Missouri Compromise had closed to slavery, Douglas had to repeal that line, and he did. President Franklin Pierce signed the bill on 30 May 1854. The North reacted with fury: the old Whig Party collapsed, and out of the wreckage came the Republican Party, founded in 1854 on the principle that Congress should forbid slavery in the territories and that the West should be reserved for free labour. The Act destroyed the last national party that straddled the sections and made a purely sectional party — the Republicans — the second-largest in the country.

**Bleeding Kansas.** — Kansas became the testing ground. The New England Emigrant Aid Company sent antislavery settlers; Missouri "border ruffians" crossed over to vote, and a fraudulently elected pro-slavery legislature met at Lecompton in 1855. Free-state settlers formed a rival government at Topeka. On 21 May 1856 a pro-slavery posse sacked the free-state town of Lawrence, burning the hotel and the newspaper office. Two days later John Brown, a stern Calvinist who had come to Kansas with several of his sons, led a small party to Pottawatomie Creek and killed five pro-slavery settlers with swords. In the months that followed, raids and reprisals killed perhaps fifty-five people. The territory earned the name Bleeding Kansas, and the violence there taught both sections that the question might be settled by force.

**The caning of Sumner.** — On 19–20 May 1856 Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts delivered a speech titled "The Crime against Kansas," in which he mocked Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina. Two days later Butler's nephew, Representative Preston Brooks, entered the nearly empty Senate chamber and beat Sumner over the head with a cane until the cane broke and Sumner lay unconscious. The House censured Brooks but did not expel him, and South Carolina voters returned him to office; the near-murder was celebrated in one section and mourned in the other.

**Dred Scott.** — Dred Scott was an enslaved man born in Virginia about 1795 and taken by his owner to Missouri, then to the free state of Illinois and the free territory of Wisconsin, before being brought back. In 1846 he and his wife Harriet sued for freedom on the ground that residence in free soil had made them free. After eleven years the case reached the Supreme Court, which decided it on 6 March 1857. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney held that Black people, whether enslaved or free, were not citizens and could not sue in federal court; that the Missouri Compromise's ban on slavery north of 36°30′ had always been unconstitutional because Congress had no power to exclude slavery from a territory; and that a slaveholder's property in an enslaved person was protected everywhere. Only Justices John McLean and Benjamin Curtis dissented. The decision seemed to make slavery national and freedom local.

## The last years, 1858–1861

**The Lincoln-Douglas debates.** — In June 1858 the Illinois Republicans nominated Abraham Lincoln, a Springfield lawyer and former one-term congressman, to run against Stephen Douglas for the Senate. Accepting the nomination on 16 June, Lincoln said the country could not remain half slave and half free: "A house divided against itself cannot stand." Douglas agreed to seven joint debates that summer and autumn, before crowds of thousands. Lincoln pressed Douglas to say how the people of a territory could exclude slavery if the Dred Scott decision said they could not; at Freeport on 27 August Douglas answered that slavery would die for want of local police laws whatever the Court said, a reply known as the Freeport Doctrine. It satisfied Illinois voters enough that the legislature re-elected Douglas, but it cost him the South. Lincoln lost the seat and won a national reputation.

**John Brown at Harpers Ferry.** — On the night of 16 October 1859, John Brown and twenty-one men, five of them Black, seized the federal arsenal and rifle works at Harpers Ferry, in what is now West Virginia, hoping to arm a rising of enslaved people. No rising came. Colonel Robert E. Lee and Lieutenant J. E. B. Stuart led a company of Marines that stormed the engine house on 18 October; ten of Brown's men were killed, including two of his sons, and Brown was wounded and captured. Tried in Charles Town for treason against Virginia, he was hanged on 2 December 1859. Before he died he handed over a note: "I, John Brown, am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood." Brown's raid convinced many Southerners that the North meant to arm their slaves.

**The election of 1860.** — The parties met in 1860 and split. The Democrats divided into a Northern wing that nominated Douglas and a Southern wing that nominated Vice President John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky on a platform demanding federal protection of slavery in the territories. Former Whigs and Know-Nothings formed the Constitutional Union Party and nominated John Bell of Tennessee, campaigning on the Union alone, while the Republicans nominated Lincoln on a platform of no extension of slavery. On 6 November 1860 Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and about 39.8 per cent of the popular vote; Douglas took 12 electoral votes, Breckinridge 72, and Bell 39. Lincoln's name did not appear on the ballot in ten Southern states. The result was a purely sectional victory: a president chosen entirely by the free states, on a platform the slave states read as a threat to their existence.

**Secession.** — South Carolina moved first, seceding on 20 December 1860. Its "Declaration of the Immediate Causes" charged that Northern states had refused to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act and had elected a president "whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery." Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed by 1 February 1861. Delegates met at Montgomery, Alabama, formed the Confederate States of America on 8 February, and inaugurated Jefferson Davis as president on 18 February. In his "Cornerstone" speech of 21 March 1861, Vice President Alexander Stephens said the new government rested on "the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery… is his natural and normal condition." Congress passed a proposed amendment, the Corwin Amendment, protecting slavery where it already existed, and Lincoln endorsed it in his inaugural address on 4 March 1861. It was not enough. On 12 April Confederate batteries opened fire on Fort Sumter, and on 15 April Lincoln called for 75,000 militia. Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina then left the Union, and the war began.

## The question of causes

**Irrepressible conflict or blundering generation?** — Historians have long divided over why the war came. One school, the "blundering generation" school, held that the conflict was avoidable and was produced by fanaticism, political miscalculation, and a failure of leaders to compromise; its leading figures were James G. Randall, in an essay of 1940, and Avery Craven, in The Coming of the Civil War (1942). The opposing school treated the conflict as fundamental, even irrepressible: two societies, one built on free labour and one on slavery, had grown incompatible, and no compromise could have lasted. James M. McPherson's Battle Cry of Freedom (1988) is the fullest modern statement of that view, and Eric Foner's Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men (1970) showed how deeply the free-labour ideal ran in Republican thought. The debate is not closed, but the weight of the evidence runs against the idea that the war was an accident.

**Was slavery the cause?** — On the Southern side the answer is unusually clear, because secessionists said so themselves. South Carolina's declaration named slavery; the Confederate constitution protected it; and Stephens called it the "cornerstone" of the new nation. The war began because seven states chose to leave rather than accept a government they believed would eventually end slavery, and the other four left only when asked to furnish troops against their neighbours. On the Northern side the answer is more complicated. Lincoln went to war to preserve the Union, not to abolish slavery, and said so repeatedly; the Emancipation Proclamation came only in 1863, and it freed the enslaved in rebel-held areas, not in the loyal border states. Yet the Union could not be preserved without addressing slavery, because slavery was the thing that had split it. Slavery was the cause of secession and the underlying cause of the war, while emancipation became a Union aim during the war rather than before it.

**Was the war avoidable?** — Contingency is not the same as accident. The crisis had many forks. If the Compromise of 1850 had failed, if Douglas had not repealed the Missouri Compromise, or if Douglas had won the presidency in 1860, the war might have come later or not at all. But each of those choices was made by people who could see the risk, and the underlying conflict did not go away. The secession winter of 1860–61 failed because the two sections wanted incompatible things: the South wanted guarantees that slavery could expand and would be protected, and the Republicans would not grant them. The war may not have been inevitable in 1861, but a durable peace required either the end of slavery or its permanent national protection.

## Chronology

- **1793** — Eli Whitney builds the cotton gin; the cotton kingdom begins to spread.
- **1808** — The Atlantic slave trade is banned by federal law; the domestic trade grows in its place.
- **1816–17** — The American Colonization Society is founded to send free Black Americans to Africa.
- **1820** — The Missouri Compromise admits Missouri as a slave state and bars slavery north of 36°30′.
- **1831** — Garrison begins The Liberator; Nat Turner's rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, is crushed.
- **1833** — The American Anti-Slavery Society is founded; Britain abolishes slavery in its colonies.
- **1838** — Frederick Douglass escapes from Baltimore; he publishes his Narrative in 1845.
- **1846** — The Wilmot Proviso seeks to bar slavery from the Mexican cession.
- **1850** — The Compromise of 1850 admits California free and enacts the Fugitive Slave Act.
- **1851–52** — Uncle Tom's Cabin is serialized and then published as a book.
- **1854** — The Kansas-Nebraska Act repeals the Missouri Compromise; the Republican Party is founded.
- **1856** — Lawrence is sacked and John Brown strikes at Pottawatomie; Preston Brooks canes Charles Sumner.
- **1857** — The Supreme Court decides Dred Scott v. Sandford.
- **1858** — Lincoln delivers the "House Divided" speech and debates Douglas seven times.
- **1859** — John Brown raids Harpers Ferry in October and is hanged in December.
- **1860** — Lincoln is elected president on 6 November; South Carolina secedes on 20 December.
- **1861** — Six more states secede; the Confederacy is formed in February; Fort Sumter is fired on 12 April.

## Questions to carry

- Cotton made the Southern planters rich and gave them political power. Did it also make them prisoners of a system they could not safely abandon?
- The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was meant to settle the sectional quarrel. Why did it inflame the North instead?
- Douglass wrote that "slaves sing most when they are most unhappy." What does that tell us about reading a source against its surface?
- The Supreme Court in Dred Scott claimed to settle the territorial question. Why did a decision by the highest court make the crisis worse rather than better?
- If the secessionists said plainly that slavery was their cause, why do historians still argue about whether the war was "about" slavery?
