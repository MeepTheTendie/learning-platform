# Pronouns and case

Pronouns are the small, closed set of words that English still inflects for case. A noun keeps only one case ending, the possessive *-'s*; a pronoun keeps three, and often keeps a distinct form for each. That is why pronouns are where most of the surviving case grammar of English is taught, and why so many of the standing disputes in English usage — *It is I* against *It is me*, *who* against *whom*, *everyone* with *his* or *their* — gather around these few words.

**What a pronoun is.** — A pronoun is a word that stands in the place of a noun or noun phrase. Robert Lowth's definition of 1762 is as good as any: "A Pronoun is a word standing instead of a Noun, as its Substitute or Representative." The point of the class is economy. Without pronouns, a sentence about one man becomes a chant: *A man came in, and the man took off the man's hat, and the man sat down.* With pronouns: *A man came in, and he took off his hat, and he sat down.* Lindley Murray's example makes the saving plain: "The man is happy; he is benevolent; he is useful."

Pronouns form a closed class: new ones are not coined, and the list barely changes across centuries. English has personal pronouns, possessive pronouns, reflexive and intensive pronouns, demonstrative pronouns, interrogative pronouns, relative pronouns, and indefinite pronouns. The same word often belongs to more than one class. *Who* is interrogative in *Who called?* and relative in *the man who called*. *That* can be a demonstrative (*That is mine*), a relative (*the book that I read*), or a conjunction (*I know that he came*). Part of learning the pronoun is learning to tell these functions apart, because the label depends on the job the word is doing in the sentence, not on its spelling.

**The antecedent.** — The noun or noun phrase a pronoun refers to is its antecedent. The word comes from Latin, "going before," and the antecedent usually does go before: *The woman took her coat.* The antecedent can be a whole clause: *He arrived an hour late, which annoyed everyone.* It can be implied rather than stated: in *Who steals my purse steals trash*, the antecedent of *who* is "the man who." Occasionally a pronoun points forward to its antecedent, a construction called cataphora: *When she got home, Mary went straight to bed.* And sometimes a pronoun has no antecedent at all. The *it* of *It is raining* is called impersonal or expletive; the *there* of *There is a man at the door* is an expletive too. A pronoun should agree with its antecedent in person, number, and gender — the rule Murray set out in 1795 as "Pronouns must always agree with their antecedents, and the nouns for which they stand, in gender and number." The hard cases are exactly where that rule collides with the way people speak, as the last sections show.

**Person, number, and gender.** — A pronoun has person (first, second, third), number (singular, plural), and — in the third person singular only — gender. The first person is the speaker (*I, we*), the second the person addressed (*you*), the third the person or thing spoken of (*he, she, it, they*). English marks gender only in the third-person singular, and chiefly in the pronouns: *he* is masculine, *she* feminine, *it* neuter. The plural *they* makes no gender distinction. The second person once had *thou* for the singular and *ye* for the plural, with *thee* and *you* as their objective forms; *you* spread to all uses, and *thou* survives only in poetry, prayer, and a few dialects. This is why *you* has the same form as subject and object — a fact that matters for case, because it means the second person gives no visible signal of the distinction the other persons keep.

**The three cases.** — Case is the form a word takes to show its grammatical job. English nouns have only the possessive (*the king's crown*, *the kings' crowns*); the subject and object forms of a noun are identical, and word order does the work. *The dog bit the man* and *The man bit the dog* are distinguished by position, not by endings. Pronouns preserve a fuller system. Lowth names the three cases the nominative, "the Genitive, or Possessive," and the objective, "a Case, which follows the Verb Active, or the Preposition, expressing the Object of an Action, or of a Relation."

| | Nominative | Objective | Possessive (determiner) | Possessive (independent) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First singular | I | me | my | mine |
| Second singular | you (thou) | you (thee) | your (thy) | yours (thine) |
| Third singular, masc. | he | him | his | his |
| Third singular, fem. | she | her | her | hers |
| Third singular, neut. | it | it | its | its |
| First plural | we | us | our | ours |
| Second plural | you | you | your | yours |
| Third plural | they | them | their | theirs |

The possessive has two shapes for most pronouns: one that comes before a noun (*my, your, his, her, its, our, their*) and one that stands alone (*mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs*). Grammarians call the first a possessive determiner, or pronominal adjective, and the second an independent possessive. *His* and *its* do double duty and do not change form. The older singular forms *thou, thee, thy, thine* behave like the others: *thy* before a noun, *thine* standing alone, and *mine* and *thine* used before a vowel in older English (*mine eyes*, *thine ear*).

**Case in the sentence.** — The case of a pronoun is fixed by its grammatical function, not by its position. That is the key difference from nouns. A pronoun is nominative when it is the subject of a verb (*She left*), a predicate nominative after a linking verb (*It is she*), or in apposition with a nominative (*We, the people, ...*). It is objective when it is the direct object (*I saw him*), the indirect object (*I gave her the book*), the object of a preposition (*with them*), or the subject of an infinitive in the older grammar (*I took him to be honest*). Because the form itself carries the case, a pronoun can move to the front of a sentence without losing its function: *Whom ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you* — *whom* is the object of *worship* and *him* the object of *declare*, even though both stand before their verbs. Nouns cannot do this, because a noun's case is shown by its place. That single fact explains why grammarians care so much about pronoun case: it is the one place where the old inflectional machinery of English is still visible in ordinary sentences.

**It is I or It is me.** — The predicate nominative is the most disputed point in English pronoun case. After the verb *to be*, the traditional rule requires the nominative, because the complement names the same person as the subject: *It is I*, *It was she*, *It is they*. Lowth states it flatly: "The Verb to Be has always a Nominative Case after it; as, 'it was I, and not He, that did it.'" The rule is Latin in origin — in Latin the complement of the copula takes the nominative — and it has never matched speech. In ordinary English, *It's me* is the normal form, and *It is I* sounds formal or theatrical. Modern grammarians point out that *be* is not a transitive verb and does not "govern" a case at all; the complement of *be* is simply a complement, and the choice between *I* and *me* there is a matter of register and idiom rather than of logic. When a clause follows, the nominative often returns: *It is I who am to blame* — but even here, *It's me who's to blame* is common, and the verb may then agree with the pronoun nearest it. The practical advice is to write *It is I* in formal prose and to expect *It's me* in speech, and not to treat the second as a grammatical error. The same choice appears in short answers: *Who is it? — It's me.*

**Comparisons with than and as.** — Comparisons hide a clause, and the hidden words decide the case. In *She is taller than I*, the full sentence is *She is taller than I am*; the *I* is the subject of the omitted *am*. In *She likes him more than me*, the full sentence is *She likes him more than [she likes] me*; the *me* is the object. Lowth's rule is to supply the missing words and let them fix the case: "Thou art wiser than I [am.]" and "You think him handsomer than [you think] me." William Cobbett made the same point with a warning that the choice can change the meaning:

> "I love you as well as him" means that I love you as well as I love him; but "I love you as well as he" means that I love you as well as he loves you.

Because the understood clause can be read in two ways, the bare comparison is genuinely ambiguous, and the ambiguity — not the case form — is the real problem. Modern grammar treats *than* and *as* as either conjunctions, which take a clause (often with words left out), or prepositions, which take the objective. On that analysis both *taller than I* and *taller than me* are grammatical, the first more formal and the second more common; the same holds for *as tall as I* and *as tall as me*. When a pronoun follows *than* or *as* and a verb is expressed, use the nominative: *She runs faster than he does.* When the pronoun is clearly the object, use the objective: *They invited you rather than me.* When the sentence is ambiguous, rewrite it: *She likes him more than she likes me* or *She likes him more than I do.*

**Who and whom.** — *Who* is the nominative form and *whom* the objective, and the choice follows the same test as *he* and *him*. Ask what the pronoun does inside its own clause. In *Who called?*, *who* is the subject, so nominative. In *Whom did you call?*, *whom* is the object of *call*, so objective. In *the man whom I saw*, *whom* is the object of *saw*; in *the man who saw me*, *who* is the subject. The same rule governs the compounds: *whoever* is nominative, *whomever* objective — *Whoever calls will be admitted*, but *I will speak to whomever I choose*. A relative pronoun that is the subject of its own clause is nominative even when the clause as a whole is the object of something else: *I know who did it.* Prepositions keep the objective: *to whom*, *with whom*, *from whom*. *Whom* is fading from informal English, where *Who did you see?* is normal, but it survives after a preposition and in formal writing, and it is never wrong to use it correctly. Note the trap in *Whom shall I say is calling?*: *who* is the subject of *is calling*, so the correct form is *Who shall I say is calling?*

**Relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that.** — A relative pronoun joins a clause to a noun and refers back to it. *Who* (with *whom* and *whose*) is used of persons; *which* of things; *that* of either, though many writers reserve it for things. The choice between *that* and *which* is a matter of clause type, not of correctness. A restrictive, or defining, clause narrows the noun and takes *that* or *which* without a comma: *The house that Jack built fell down.* A non-restrictive, or commenting, clause adds information and takes *which* with a comma: *The house, which Jack built in 1900, fell down.* The comma does real work: in the first sentence there may be several houses and only Jack's fell; in the second there is one house, already identified, and the clause merely adds a fact. *That* is usual after a superlative or after *all*, *only*, and *first*: *the best book that I have read*, *all that remains*. Fowler argued that *whose* may be used of things as well as persons (*the book whose cover is torn*) and that avoiding it by writing *of which the cover* is often clumsy. The relative can be left out when it is objective: *The man [whom] I love* — but not when it is the subject: *The man who came*, never *The man came* (in that sense). *What* combines antecedent and relative: *What he said was true* means *the thing that he said was true.* *Which* can also refer to a whole clause: *He was late, which annoyed her.*

**Interrogative pronouns.** — The interrogative pronouns are *who*, *whom*, *whose*, *which*, and *what*. *Who* asks about persons and takes the case its function requires: *Who is there?* (subject), *Whom did you meet?* (object), *Whose is this?* (possessive). *Which* asks about a limited set and can be a pronoun or a determiner: *Which do you want?*, *Which train?* *What* asks about things or about an unlimited range: *What happened?*, *What did she say?* The contrast between *which* and *what* is the contrast between a known set and an open one: *Which of the two?* but *What kind of music?* In indirect questions the same forms appear: *I asked who had come*, *Tell me whom you saw.* The interrogative *who* can also introduce a clause that is not a question: *I know who you are.* And *who* can be an indefinite pronoun meaning "the person who": *Who dares wins.*

**Demonstrative pronouns.** — *This* and *these* point to what is near; *that* and *those* to what is farther away in space, time, or thought. They are pronouns when they stand alone (*This is mine*, *Those are ripe*) and determiners when they precede a noun (*this book*, *those apples*). *That* and *those* often refer back to avoid repeating a noun: *The climate of Italy is milder than that of England* — *that* stands for *the climate*. *Those* can mean "the people who": *Those who arrived late missed the speech.* *This* commonly refers to a whole statement just made: *She resigned without notice, and this surprised no one.* The demonstratives also carry the near/far contrast in discourse: *this* for what is coming or close at hand, *that* for what is already past or at a distance. *That* has still other lives — as a relative pronoun, as a conjunction, and as an adverb of degree (*I was that tired*) — which is why it is one of the hardest words in English to parse by itself.

**Reflexive and intensive pronouns.** — The *-self* pronouns — *myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves* — have two jobs. A reflexive pronoun is the object of a verb or preposition when the object is the same person as the subject: *I hurt myself*, *She talked to herself*, *They blamed themselves.* An intensive pronoun adds emphasis and stands in apposition to a noun or pronoun: *I myself will go*, *The king himself came.* The two uses take the same form; the difference is function. *Himself* and *themselves* are the standard forms; *hisself* and *theirselves* are nonstandard. *Yourself* is singular and *yourselves* plural, and the royal *we* takes *ourself*, not *ourselves*. A reflexive is not a substitute for a personal pronoun: standard English has *He was kind to Mary and me*, not *He was kind to Mary and myself*. The reflexive also has a reciprocal cousin: *each other* for two, *one another* for more than two, though the distinction is often ignored. In older English the simple pronouns could be reflexive (*I held me still*), and a trace survives in *I bought me a hat* and *Look about you.*

**Possessive pronouns and contractions.** — The possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe: *mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs.* The reason is historical. The apostrophe in possessives began as a mark of elision, standing for the *e* of an old genitive ending *-es*; it belongs to nouns (*the king's*), not to pronouns, which had already lost that ending. The independent possessives are therefore written without it, and the determiner *its* — the possessive of *it* — is written without it too. This creates the commonest spelling trap in English: *its* is the possessive (*The dog wagged its tail*), while *it's* is the contraction of *it is* or *it has* (*It's raining*, *It's been a long day*). The same trap appears in three more pairs:

- *whose* (possessive of *who*) against *who's* (who is, who has): *Whose book is this?* / *Who's coming?*
- *their* (possessive of *they*) against *they're* (they are) and *there* (place): *Their house is there; they're home.*
- *your* (possessive of *you*) against *you're* (you are): *Your coat is wet; you're late.*

The test is to expand the contraction: if *it is*, *who is*, *they are*, or *you are* fits, use the apostrophe; if not, use the possessive. The apostrophe is not a mark of the possessive as such; it is a mark of something left out. That is why the possessive pronoun, which leaves nothing out, has none.

**Indefinite pronouns and agreement.** — Indefinite pronouns refer to people or things without naming them. Some are always singular: *everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, anyone, anybody, anything, no one, nobody, nothing, each, either, neither, one.* Some are always plural: *both, few, many, several.* Others vary with what they refer to: *all, some, any, none, most, more.* The traditional rule is that the singular indefinites take singular verbs and singular pronouns: *Everyone is here*, *Each of the boys has a book*, *Neither of them knows.* In practice the pronouns often go plural, because the meaning is plural even when the grammar is singular: *Everyone should bring their book.* This clash between formal agreement (singular) and notional agreement (plural) is the source of most of the trouble. *None* is a special case: it can be singular (*None of the money is missing*) or plural (*None of the guests have arrived*), and both are defensible, since *none* means "not one" but also stands for a plural noun. *Either* and *neither* are singular when they stand alone. *Every one* written as two words means each individual; *everyone* as one word means everybody. *No one* is always two words. The verb in *one of those who ...* is a further snare: *He is one of those people who believe in luck* — *who* refers to *people*, so the plural verb is right.

**The singular they.** — *They*, *them*, and *their* are used with a singular antecedent when the person's gender is unknown, unspecified, or irrelevant: *Someone left their umbrella.* This is not a recent error. The *Oxford English Dictionary* traces singular *they* to the fourteenth century, and it appears in the King James Bible: "if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses" (Matthew 18:35). Shakespeare uses it — "There's not a man I meet but doth salute me / As if I were their well-acquainted friend" (*The Comedy of Errors*) — and Jane Austen uses it: "everybody has their failing, you know, and everybody has a right to do..." (*Northanger Abbey*). What changed was not the language but the rule. In the eighteenth century, grammarians including Ann Fisher (1745) and John Kirkby (1746) began to insist that *he* do duty for both sexes, and Lowth and Murray fixed the agreement rule that made singular *they* look wrong. In 1850 Parliament enacted that "words importing the masculine gender shall be deemed and taken to include females," which gave the generic *he* a legal footing. For two centuries schoolbooks taught *he* as the neutral pronoun, and many writers avoided singular *they* in formal prose even while using it in speech. The picture has changed again: major style guides now accept singular *they*, dictionaries record it, and it is the usual pronoun for a person whose gender is unknown and for many people who use it of themselves. Where a sentence allows it, singular *they* is often the cleanest choice; where a formal context demands it, *he or she* remains available, though it is clumsy and excludes those who use *they*. The disputed point is not whether singular *they* exists — it is centuries old — but how far formal writing should admit it.

**Practice.** — Identify the case of each italicized pronoun, and correct any error. (1) *Him* and *me* went to the fair. (2) The prize went to *she* and *I*. (3) *Who* did you give it to? (4) She is taller than *him*. (5) Everyone should do *their* best. (6) *Its* too late to change *it's* color. (7) Between you and *I*, the plan is risky. Answers: (1) nominative *He and I*; (2) objective *her and me*; (3) objective *Whom*; (4) *than he* if the clause is *than he is*, though *than him* is acceptable as a prepositional comparison; (5) *their* is standard; (6) *It's* too late to change *its* color; (7) objective *me*.

## Chronology

- **c. 1000** — Old English pronouns are inflected for four main cases: nominative, accusative, genitive, and dative.
- **c. 1150–1400** — Middle English loses most case endings; the pronoun system survives, and singular *they* appears in the fourteenth century.
- **1586** — William Bullokar publishes the first grammar of English written in English.
- **1611** — The King James Bible prints "every one his brother their trespasses" (Matthew 18:35).
- **1623** — Shakespeare's First Folio prints "As if I were their well-acquainted friend" in *The Comedy of Errors*.
- **1712** — Jonathan Swift calls for an English academy to fix the language.
- **1745–1746** — Ann Fisher and John Kirkby prescribe *he* as the pronoun for both sexes.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, sets out the three cases and the nominative after *to be*.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, makes pronoun-antecedent agreement a rule: "in gender and number."
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, explains the case after *than* and *as*.
- **1850** — An Act of Parliament rules that the masculine gender includes females in legal language.
- **1891** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar*, treats usage rather than correctness as the standard.
- **1913** — Kittredge and Farley, *An Advanced English Grammar*, teach case by function and warn against *myself* for *me*.
- **1924** — Otto Jespersen, *The Philosophy of Grammar*, describes the decay of case and the drift from *whom* to *who*.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, defends *whose* for things.
- **1975** — Anne Bodine documents how the ban on singular *they* was invented.
- **1986** — Dennis Baron, *Grammar and Gender*, traces the history of the generic *he*.
- **2017–2019** — Major style guides and dictionaries accept singular *they*.

## Questions to carry

- Why does English keep case in its pronouns but not in its nouns?
- Is *It is me* an error, a different construction, or simply the way the language has gone?
- When *than* or *as* is followed by a pronoun, what decides the case — the missing verb, the preposition, or the writer's meaning?
- Does *everyone ... their* break agreement, or does it show that agreement follows meaning as well as form?
- Singular *they* is older than the rule against it. What does that say about where grammar rules come from?
