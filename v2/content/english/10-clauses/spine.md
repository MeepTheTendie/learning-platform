# Clauses: main and subordinate

**Period:** English grammar, sixteenth to twenty-first centuries

## The clause

**A clause, defined.** — A clause is a group of words built around a subject and a finite verb. *The rain stopped* is a clause: *rain* is the subject, *stopped* the finite verb. *Birds sing*, *Alexander conquered the Persians*, *that she lied* — each contains a subject and a verb, and each is therefore a clause. The definition is the one the school tradition has used since Lowth, who wrote in 1762 that a "Simple Sentence hath in it but one Subject, and one Finite Verb." Lowth's simple sentence is what modern grammar calls an independent clause; the word *clause* came to name the unit because a sentence may contain more than one of them.

**Clause and phrase.** — A **phrase** is a group of words that works as a single part of speech and has no subject–verb pair. *The last bus*, *very fond of cake*, *under the stairs*, *to finish the work*, *having eaten* — none of these is a clause. A phrase can be long and a clause short. *The man in the blue coat with the umbrella* is a noun phrase, however many words it carries; *it rained* is a clause, however few. The test is structural, not a matter of length: look for the subject and the finite verb. Lowth defined a phrase as "two or more words rightly put together in order to make a part of a Sentence," but modern grammar keeps the terms apart because clauses can be coordinated, embedded, and moved in ways that phrases cannot.

**Finite and non-finite.** — The verb at the centre of a clause is usually **finite**: it is marked for tense (*stopped*, *stops*) and agrees with its subject (*the rain stops*, *the rains stop*). English also has **non-finite** verb forms — the infinitive (*to stop*), the present participle (*stopping*), the past participle (*stopped*) — which carry no tense and no agreement. A group of words built on a non-finite verb is not a full clause: *to finish the work* is an infinitive phrase, and *having eaten* is a participial phrase. When such a group has its own understood subject and does the work of a clause, modern grammarians call it a **reduced clause** or **non-finite clause**; the traditional grammars called it a phrase. The distinction matters because only finite clauses can stand as sentences.

**Counting clauses.** — A practical rule is to count the finite verbs. *The rain stopped and we went out* contains two finite verbs (*stopped*, *went*) and therefore two clauses. *When the rain stopped, we went out* also has two. *The man who came to dinner stayed* has two: *came* and *stayed*. Two finite verbs can also belong to a single clause if they are coordinated under a shared subject — *She came and sat down* is analysed either as two clauses sharing the subject *she* or as one clause with a compound predicate. The counting rule is a guide, not an algorithm.

**Independent and dependent.** — An **independent** (or **main**) clause can stand alone as a sentence. A **dependent** (or **subordinate**) clause cannot: it is marked by a subordinating word and does the work of a noun, an adjective, or an adverb inside a larger sentence. *She left* is independent. *Because she left* is dependent — the subordinator *because* holds it open. Henry Sweet drew the line in 1891 by form rather than meaning: an independent sentence "is one whose grammatical structure allows it to stand alone," while a dependent sentence "cannot stand alone, but makes us expect another — generally an independent — sentence to complete its meaning." That expectation is created by the first word: *that*, *whether*, *who*, *which*, *when*, *because*, *although*, *if* all announce that a clause is coming and that a main clause is still owed. Meaning alone will not decide the matter. *Because the rain stopped* has a complete subject and predicate and a perfectly clear meaning, but it is not a sentence in edited prose; the subordinator leaves the grammar hanging.

**The markers of subordination.** — Three families of words introduce dependent clauses. **Subordinating conjunctions** (*because, although, when, while, if, unless, since, until, after, before, so that, as if*) introduce adverbial clauses. **Relative pronouns** (*who, whom, whose, which, that*) and **relative adverbs** (*where, when, why*) introduce relative clauses and refer back to an antecedent. **Complementizers** (*that, whether, if*) and **interrogative words** (*what, who, when, where, why, how*) introduce noun clauses. A few words do double duty: *when* is a relative adverb in *the day when we met* and a subordinating conjunction in *when we met, it rained*. The label depends on the work the word does, not on a fixed list.

## Main clauses and how sentences combine

**Coordination and subordination.** — Two clauses may be joined as equals or as unequals. **Coordination** joins two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*) or a semicolon: *Life is short, and art is long.* **Subordination** puts one clause inside the other as a dependent part: *When the rain stopped, we went out.* Lowth saw the two mechanisms and named the words that perform them: "There are two sorts of words which connect Sentences: 1. Relatives; 2. Conjunctions." Sweet separated them more sharply, distinguishing a **co-clause** (coordinate) from a **sub-clause** (subordinate), and treating the whole as a **complex**. A sentence with one clause is **simple**; one with two or more independent clauses is **compound**; one with a main clause and at least one dependent clause is **complex**; one with both is **compound-complex**. The labels are traditional, but the underlying distinction — equal rank versus embedding — is real.

**Combining short sentences.** — Complex sentences are built by choosing one clause as the main statement and demoting the others. Take three short sentences: *The rain stopped. We went out. The sun was setting.* Any of the three can be the main clause, and the others attached by subordination: *When the rain stopped, we went out while the sun was setting.* *Although the rain had stopped, we stayed in, because the sun was setting.* The choice of main clause decides which fact the sentence is *about*; the subordinators decide the logical relation between the facts. This is the practical value of the clause: it lets a writer rank ideas instead of merely listing them.

**The "And" myth.** — The rule that a sentence may not begin with *And* or *But* is not a rule of English grammar. It is absent from Lowth and Murray, and the King James Bible begins hundreds of sentences with *And*. What the rule tries to prevent — a string of loose coordinate clauses with no subordination — is a real fault of style, but the remedy is not to ban the word. The prohibition is a schoolroom convention of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and modern style guides treat it as a preference, not a law.

## Noun clauses

**What a noun clause does.** — A **noun clause** is a dependent clause that fills a slot where a noun or noun phrase could stand. It can be the **subject** (*That she lied surprised no one*; *What he said was true*), the **direct object** (*I know what you did*; *He said that he was tired*), the **subject complement** (*The truth is that we are late*), the **object of a preposition** (*It depends on whether it rains*), or an **appositive** (*the fact that the money was gone*). Modern grammar calls *that*, *whether*, and *if* **complementizers**, because their job is to turn a whole clause into a complement of the verb. The term belongs to the generative tradition in linguistics; the older grammars simply called these noun clauses or substantive clauses.

**The three introducers.** — A declarative noun clause takes *that*: *She said that the door was locked.* An interrogative noun clause takes *whether* or *if*: *I wonder whether he came*; *I wonder if he came.* A clause introduced by an interrogative word — *what, who, whom, whose, which, when, where, why, how* — keeps the word order of a statement, not a question: *I asked where he was going*, not *I asked where was he going*. The embedded question does not invert: *I wondered what time it was* is right; *I wondered what time was it* is wrong.

**That and whether: the fine points.** — *That* may be omitted after many verbs, especially *say, think, know, believe, hope*: *She said the door was locked.* It may not normally be omitted when the noun clause is the subject: *That she lied surprised no one* cannot lose its *that* (*She lied surprised no one* is not English). Extraposition rescues the subject clause by moving it to the end behind a dummy *it*: *It surprised no one that she lied.* *Whether* is required after a preposition (*It depends on whether it rains*), where *if* is not used; *whether* also handles the *or not* pair (*whether or not he comes*). *If* is the more casual of the two and is common in speech and in object clauses.

**Noun clauses and fragments.** — Because a noun clause cannot stand alone, a writer who punctuates one as a sentence produces a **fragment**. *That she had forgotten the key.* is not a sentence; attach it (*I realized that she had forgotten the key*) or drop the complementizer (*She had forgotten the key*). The same is true of a *wh*-clause used alone in exposition: *What the committee decided.* needs a main clause to complete it.

## Relative clauses

**What a relative clause does.** — A **relative clause** (also called an **adjective clause**) modifies a noun or pronoun, its **antecedent**. It is introduced by a **relative pronoun** (*who, whom, whose, which, that*) or a **relative adverb** (*where, when, why*), or by nothing at all. *The book that I read was dull* — the clause *that I read* tells which book. *The man who came to dinner stayed a week.* *This is the house where I was born.* Like an adjective, the relative clause narrows or describes the noun; unlike an adjective, it carries its own subject and verb.

**Restrictive and non-restrictive.** — A **restrictive** (or **defining**) relative clause identifies which person or thing is meant; it is essential to the meaning and is not set off by commas. A **non-restrictive** (or **non-defining**) relative clause adds information that could be removed without changing which thing is meant; it is set off by commas. Compare *The students who had studied passed* (only that group passed) with *The students, who had studied, passed* (all the students passed, and they happened to have studied). H. W. Fowler gave the test in *The King's English* (1906; 2nd ed. 1924): "the removal of a defining clause destroys the meaning of the antecedent," while a non-defining clause "can always ... be detached without disturbing the truth of the main predication." Remove *who had studied* from the restrictive sentence and the claim changes from some students to all; remove it from the non-restrictive sentence and the main fact — that they passed — stands.

**Punctuation is a signal, not a proof.** — Commas mark the non-restrictive clause, and their absence marks the restrictive one, but the punctuation follows the meaning rather than creating it. A writer who writes *My brother, who lives in Chicago, is a doctor* says that the brother is identified already and merely adds his city; *My brother who lives in Chicago is a doctor* implies more than one brother. The difference is not decorative: misplaced commas can invert a sentence's sense, which is why the removal test comes before the comma.

**That, which, and who.** — Traditional grammar assigns *who* to persons, *which* to things, and *that* to either. The usage is older and looser than the rule suggests. Lowth noted in 1762 that *who* and *which* "were both indifferently used of persons" in earlier English — the Lord's Prayer has *Our Father, which art in heaven* — and that *that* "is used indifferently both of persons and things." The sharp modern rule, that *which* may introduce only a non-restrictive clause and *that* only a restrictive one, is largely a twentieth-century editorial convention, strongest in American publishing. It is not a fact about English syntax. British edited prose has always used restrictive *which* — *the book which I read* — and the King James Bible, Shakespeare, and Dickens do so freely. Fowler, the supposed source of the rule, was more cautious than his followers: he wrote that "no distinction can be authoritatively drawn between the two relatives," then recommended *that* for defining clauses as a useful tendency, not a law. Modern corpus linguists, including Geoffrey K. Pullum, have shown that restrictive *which* is standard in edited prose on both sides of the Atlantic. A writer may follow the *that/which* convention for consistency, but calling restrictive *which* an error is prescription masquerading as description.

**Who and whom.** — *Who* is the subject form and *whom* the object form: *the man who called* (subject of *called*), *the man whom I called* (object of *called*), *the man to whom I spoke*. In speech and much modern prose, *who* has largely displaced *whom* except after a preposition, and even there the preposition is often stranded: *the man who I spoke to*. The pied-piping construction (*to whom*) is formal; the stranded one (*who ... to*) is normal. Lowth and his successors preferred the fronted preposition, but Lowth himself admitted that stranding "is an Idiom which our language is strongly inclined to; it prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with the familiar style in writing."

**Omitting the relative pronoun.** — When the relative pronoun is the object of its clause and the clause is restrictive, the pronoun may be left out: *the book I read*, *the man I love*, *the friend I was travelling with*. When the relative is the subject, it cannot be omitted: *the man who called* cannot become *the man called*. These **contact clauses**, as Otto Jespersen called them, are not careless ellipsis; they are a normal construction of the language, and English has used them for centuries. Lowth took the opposite view: "the omission of the Relative seems to be too much indulged in the familiar style; it is ungraceful in the serious; and ... it is apt to be attended with obscurity and ambiguity." His objection is a matter of taste and clarity, not of grammar. What can go wrong is ambiguity: *the man I saw in the park* is fine, but a long separation between antecedent and verb can leave the reader unsure where the clause begins.

**Which referring to a whole clause.** — A relative pronoun normally refers to a noun, but *which* can refer to the entire preceding clause: *He arrived late, which annoyed her.* Here *which* means "the fact that he arrived late," and the clause is always non-restrictive, so the comma is required. Some writers dislike the construction and call it loose; it is nonetheless standard, and the alternative — *and that annoyed her* — is not always clearer. *That* cannot be used this way: *He arrived late, that annoyed her* is not English.

**Relative adverbs.** — *Where, when,* and *why* introduce relative clauses that modify place, time, and reason nouns: *the house where I was born*, *the day when we met*, *the reason why she left*. Each can be replaced by a preposition plus *which* (*the house in which I was born*), and *when* is often omitted in speech (*the day we met*).

## Adverbial clauses

**What an adverbial clause does.** — An **adverbial clause** is a dependent clause that modifies the verb or the whole main clause, answering the questions an adverb answers: when, where, why, how, under what condition. It is introduced by a **subordinating conjunction**. *When the rain stopped, we went out.* *We stayed because it was raining.* *If you finish, call me.* *Although it was late, she kept working.* Like an adverb phrase, the clause can usually move: *We went out when the rain stopped* means the same as the fronted version, though the fronted clause takes a comma.

**The main kinds.** — Grammars group adverbial clauses by meaning, and the labels are convenient even though a single conjunction can carry several senses. **Time**: *when, whenever, while, before, after, since, until, till, as, once, as soon as*. **Place**: *where, wherever*. **Reason or cause**: *because, since, as, now that*. **Purpose**: *so that, in order that, lest*. **Result**: *so ... that, such ... that*. **Condition**: *if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case*. **Concession**: *although, though, even though, while, whereas, even if*. **Comparison**: *than, as ... as*. **Manner**: *as, as if, as though*. *Since* is the classic ambiguity: *Since you left, I have been lonely* (time) versus *Since you are here, you may as well stay* (reason). The context decides.

**Position and punctuation.** — A fronted adverbial clause is followed by a comma: *When the rain stopped, we went out.* A trailing one normally takes no comma: *We went out when the rain stopped.* The comma is not free variation; it marks the boundary between the subordinate clause and the main one, and omitting it after a long fronted clause can make the reader misparse the subject. A comma before a trailing clause usually signals that the clause is parenthetical or that it gives a reason already known: *We went out, because the rain had stopped* emphasizes the reason in a way that the unpunctuated version does not.

**Because, since, as, and for.** — The four words that give a reason are not interchangeable. *Because* is the plainest and the most emphatic. *Since* and *as* are lighter and more formal, and *as* is easily confused with its temporal sense. *For* is a **coordinating** conjunction, not a subordinating one: it joins two independent clauses, cannot be fronted, and is largely literary (*He stayed, for it was late*). A subordinate clause introduced by *because* can stand as the answer to a *why* question (*Why did you go? Because it was late*); a *for*-clause cannot.

**Like and as.** — Traditional grammar insists on *as* to introduce a clause (*Do as I say*) and *like* to introduce a phrase (*He swims like a fish*). *Like* followed by a clause — *He swims like I do* — is widespread in speech and increasingly in edited prose, and the prohibition is a style preference of the last century rather than a rule of syntax. Where the distinction prevents ambiguity, it is worth keeping.

## Reduced clauses

**Reduction.** — A dependent clause may lose its finite verb and its subject and survive as a phrase that still carries clause-like meaning. The result is a **reduced clause**, also called a **non-finite clause**. The most common type is the **participial clause**. A present participle reduces a relative clause with an active verb: *the man who is sitting there* becomes *the man sitting there*; *the train which was leaving the station* becomes *the train leaving the station*. A past participle reduces a passive relative clause: *the book which was written last year* becomes *the book written last year*. Modern grammarians describe these as **reduced relative clauses**; the school tradition called the participle an adjective phrase.

**Reduced adverbial clauses.** — Adverbial clauses reduce in the same way. *Though he was tired, he kept working* becomes *Though tired, he kept working.* *When you are in Rome, do as the Romans do* becomes *When in Rome, do as the Romans do.* *If it is possible, come early* becomes *If possible, come early.* The subordinator is kept and the subject and form of *be* are dropped, because the subject of the reduced clause is understood to be the same as the subject of the main clause. The **infinitive** provides another kind of reduction: *the first who arrived* becomes *the first to arrive*; *I was glad when I heard the news* becomes *I was glad to hear the news*.

**The dangling modifier.** — Reduction is safe only when the understood subject of the reduced clause is the same as the subject of the main clause. When it is not, the participle **dangles**: *Walking to the station, the rain began* suggests that the rain was walking. The repair is to name the right subject (*Walking to the station, I felt the rain begin*) or to expand the clause (*As I was walking to the station, the rain began*). The rule is not about the participle but about reference: the reader attaches a subjectless phrase to the nearest available subject, and the writer must make that subject the right one.

## Combining clauses without fragments

**A procedure.** — To turn several short sentences into one complex sentence: (1) decide which fact is the main statement; (2) make that an independent clause; (3) mark the logical relation of each other fact — time, cause, condition, concession, definition — and give it the matching subordinator; (4) put the more important clause last unless the sentence is deliberately periodic; (5) punctuate the boundaries, using commas for non-restrictive and fronted clauses. Following the steps on *The bell rang. The students left. The lesson was over.* might give *When the bell rang, the students left, because the lesson was over* — one main clause (*the students left*) and two subordinate ones.

**Fragments.** — A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but without an independent clause. The commonest kind in student writing is the **dependent-clause fragment**: *Because the rain stopped.* or *Which she had forgotten.* The fixes are to attach the clause to a main clause, to remove the subordinator, or to supply the missing main clause. The **missing-subject** and **missing-verb** fragments (*Went to the store*; *The man in the blue coat*) are repaired by adding what is missing. Fragments are not always errors: dialogue, advertising, and poetry use them for effect, and a writer who knows the rule may break it deliberately.

**Run-ons and comma splices.** — Two independent clauses need more than a comma between them. A **run-on** joins them with nothing (*I came I saw I conquered*); a **comma splice** joins them with only a comma (*The rain stopped, we went out*). Four repairs are standard: a full stop, a semicolon, a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, or subordination. The comma splice is common in earlier English — Addison and Swift use it — and modern edited prose treats it as an error.

**Why the clause matters.** — The clause is the unit of predication, and predication is how a language says that something is so. A phrase names; a clause asserts. The difference between a list of facts and an argument is largely the difference between coordinate clauses strung together and subordinate clauses that rank one fact under another. Learning to see clauses is learning to see the joints of a sentence.

## Chronology

- **c. 1586** — William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar* adapts the Latin sentence model to English.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines the simple and compounded sentence, the relatives, and the omission of the relative.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, popularises the terms for the schoolroom and repeats Lowth's account of relatives and conjunctions.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, teaches sentence-making to ordinary readers and treats the relative pronoun as a practical tool.
- **1891** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar*, distinguishes independent from dependent sentences and co-clauses from sub-clauses, and defines the complex.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, names the "contact clause" and treats the omitted relative as normal English.
- **1906/1924** — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English*, distinguish defining from non-defining relative clauses and discuss *that* and *which*.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, enters the *that/which* question in the reference book that fixed the American editorial convention.
- **1957** — Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures*, turns grammatical analysis toward the rules that generate embedded clauses.
- **1985** — Randolph Quirk and others, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, classifies finite and non-finite clauses and the full range of adverbial subordinators.
- **2002** — Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey K. Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, treats relative and reduced clauses within a phrase-structure account and rejects the schoolroom *that/which* rule as a rule of grammar.

## Questions to carry

- Why does *Because the rain stopped* have a subject and a verb but fail as a sentence, and what does that show about the difference between form and meaning?
- In *The students who had studied passed*, what exactly does the relative clause restrict, and how does a pair of commas change the claim?
- Fowler called the *that/which* distinction useful but not authoritative. When is following it helpful, and when does enforcing it misdescribe English?
- What is the understood subject of *Though tired, he kept working*, and how does a dangling participle break the same rule?
- Given three short sentences, what decides which one becomes the main clause and which become subordinate?
