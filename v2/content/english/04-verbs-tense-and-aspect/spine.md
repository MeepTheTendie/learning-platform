# Verbs: tense and aspect

**Period:** English grammar

**Two questions, two systems.** — A verb is the word that makes a sentence an assertion: it says that something is, happens, or is done. To use a verb well, a speaker has to answer two separate questions. The first is *when* the event is located — present, past, or future. The second is *how* the event is viewed — as a whole, as still going on, or as finished with a bearing on the moment of speaking. The first is a matter of **time**; the second is a matter of **aspect**. English keeps the two partly separate, and the separation is the key to the whole system. Compare "I have finished" with "I finished." Both put the finishing before now, but the first presents the result as still relevant to the present, while the second places the event in a past that is closed. The same event, differently viewed, takes a different form.

**Time is not tense.** — Time is a property of the world: an event happened before, during, or after the moment of speaking. Tense is a property of the language: a change in the shape of the verb. The two usually line up, but not always, and confusing them causes most of the difficulty. English marks tense on the verb itself in only two ways: the **present** ("I walk, he walks") and the **past**, or **preterite** ("I walked"). Everything else that looks like a tense is built from auxiliaries. The future, in particular, is not shown by any ending on the verb; English has no inflection for it. "I will walk" and "I shall walk" are the present-tense forms *will* and *shall* followed by the plain verb. Grammarians have argued for two centuries about whether that makes a future *tense*. Otto Jespersen held that English has only two tenses, present and preterite, and that *will* and *shall* remain modal auxiliaries; many teaching grammars, by contrast, list a future tense as a matter of convenience. The forms are the same; the classification is the dispute.

**A note on terminology.** — The word *tense* is used in two ways, and the double use causes trouble. In the strict sense it means a form of the verb that locates an event in time, and English has two: present and past. In the loose sense, used by most textbooks and language courses, it means any of the twelve combinations of time and aspect, so that "the present perfect tense" and "the future progressive tense" are spoken of as tenses. Both uses are established, and a careful writer says which one is meant. The older grammars also use names that have largely dropped out of speech: the **imperfect** for the simple past, the **pluperfect** for the past perfect, and the **preterite** for the past generally. *Aspect*, by contrast, names the viewpoint rather than the time: **simple** (the event as a whole), **progressive** (in progress), **perfect** (completed with present relevance), and **perfect progressive** (continuing up to a reference point). Tense and aspect are independent, which is why the twelve cells exist.

**The principal parts.** — Traditionally a verb is learned through its **principal parts**: the **base** (also called the plain form, the form listed in a dictionary, and the form used for the infinitive and for most of the present), the **past tense**, and the **past participle**. A fourth form, the **present participle** or *-ing* form, is needed for the progressive and is sometimes counted with them. The base is the form that follows *to* and *I*: *to walk, I walk; to sing, I sing*. The past tense is the form used for the simple past: *walked, sang*. The past participle is the form used after *have* and after *be* in the passive: *have walked, have sung; is written*. For regular verbs the past tense and the past participle are identical (*walked, walked*), so the two are easy to confuse; for irregular verbs they often differ (*sang, sung*), and the difference matters.

Examples of principal parts:

- walk, walked, walked, walking
- love, loved, loved, loving
- sing, sang, sung, singing
- write, wrote, written, writing
- cut, cut, cut, cutting
- go, went, gone, going
- be, was (were), been, being

**Regular verbs.** — A regular verb forms its past tense and past participle by adding *-ed* to the base. The ending is pronounced three ways, according to the sound before it: as /t/ after a voiceless sound (*walked, hoped, passed*), as /d/ after a voiced sound (*played, lived, opened*), and as a separate syllable /ɪd/ after *t* or *d* (*wanted, needed, landed*). The spelling follows the sound: a final silent *e* is dropped (*love, loved*), a final *y* after a consonant becomes *i* (*carry, carried*), and a single final consonant after a single vowel is doubled in a stressed syllable (*stop, stopped; prefer, preferred*). Lowth already observed that the language "inclines us to contract even all our Regular Verbs," so that *loved* and *turned* are "commonly pronounced in one syllable"; the written *-ed* is often a syllable only in careful speech or before a pause.

**Irregular verbs and their patterns.** — An irregular verb is one that does not simply add *-ed*. The older terminology called the regular verbs **weak** and the irregular ones **strong**, because the strong verbs formed their past by changing the vowel rather than by adding a suffix. The irregular verbs are a closed list — English has long since stopped adding to it — and most of them are native words of one syllable. Lowth made the point when he wrote that "the Irregular Verbs in English are all Monosyllables, unless Compounded; and they are for the most part the same words which are Irregular Verbs in the Saxon." They fall into a few recognisable patterns.

- **Invariant.** Base, past, and participle are all the same: *cut, cut, cut; hit, hit, hit; put, put, put; cost, cost, cost; let, let, let*. Lowth grouped these as verbs ending in *d* or *t* that "have the Present, the Past Time, and the Participle Perfect and Passive, all alike, without any variation."
- **Past and participle alike.** The past tense doubles as the participle, and only the base differs: *buy, bought, bought; keep, kept, kept; sell, sold, sold; tell, told, told; make, made, made; have, had, had*. Some of these also change the final consonant: *lend, lent, lent; build, built, built*.
- **Three distinct forms.** Base, past, and participle each differ: *sing, sang, sung; drink, drank, drunk; drive, drove, driven; write, wrote, written; speak, spoke, spoken; know, knew, known; take, took, taken; break, broke, broken*. The participle vowel is often close to the base vowel, which is why *sang* and *sung* must be kept apart in writing and speech.
- **Past equals base, participle distinct.** *come, came, come; run, ran, run; become, became, become*.
- **Suppletion.** A few verbs take their past from an unrelated word: *go, went, gone*. The most irregular of all is *be*, whose forms (*am, is, are, was, were, been*) are drawn from several roots. The old paradigm *I am, thou art, he is, we are, I was, thou wast, he was, we were* is a survival of a much older system, and only *thou wast* and *thou hadst* keep a person ending in the past tense.

**The simple tenses.** — The **simple present** is the base, with *-s* added in the third person singular: *I walk, you walk, he walks*. The **simple past** uses the past-tense form, with no person ending except the archaic *thou* forms. The **future** is formed with *will* or *shall* plus the base: *I will walk, we shall walk*. The simple forms present an event as a whole, without saying whether it is complete or continuing. That is why they are called "simple": they name the action and leave the internal view to context or to another aspect. Shakespeare's Sonnet 116 uses the simple present for a statement meant to hold always: "Love alters not with his brief hours and weeks, / But bears it out even to the edge of doom." The same form states a habit (*he walks to work*) or a general truth (*water boils at 100°C*).

**The verb phrase and its order.** — A finite verb phrase can contain up to four verbs before the main verb, and their order is fixed: a **modal** (*will, shall, can, must, may, might, could, would, should*), then the **perfect** auxiliary *have*, then the **progressive** auxiliary *be*, then the **passive** auxiliary *be*, and finally the main verb. Each auxiliary determines the form of what follows it: a modal takes the base (*will walk*), *have* takes the past participle (*have walked*), and *be* takes either the present participle (*is walking*) or the past participle (*is walked*). The full sequence is rare but perfectly regular: *the report will have been being checked* stacks modal, perfect, passive, and progressive in that order. Knowing the order is what lets a speaker build a form never seen before and still be understood.

**Do-support.** — The auxiliary *do* has a special job. When a sentence has no other auxiliary, *do* is called in to carry tense and agreement in three situations: **negation** (*I do not walk; he does not walk; I did not walk*), **questions** (*do you walk? does he walk? did you walk?*), and **emphasis** (*I do walk; he did walk*). The main verb then stays in the base form. This is why the negative of *I walk* is *I do not walk* rather than *I walk not*, and why the question is *did you walk?* rather than *walked you?* Lowth noted that *do* and *did* "are also of frequent and almost necessary use in Interrogative and Negative Sentences." Sweet traced the construction back to Old English and showed how it spread through the modern period. When another auxiliary is present, *do* is not used: *I will not walk; will you walk?*

**The progressive aspect.** — The **progressive** (also called the **continuous**) is formed with a part of *be* and the present participle in *-ing*: *I am walking, he was walking, they will be walking*. It presents an event as in progress at a reference time, and usually as temporary or limited. The nineteenth-century grammarian Henry Sweet called these the **definite tenses**, because they use duration to pin down the time of another event: "the characteristic of these tenses is that they use duration to define the time of a point-tense, as in *when he came, I was writing a letter*." The progressive therefore typically implies incompletion: *he is writing a letter* suggests the letter is not finished, and *what have you been doing?* asks about an activity, whereas *what have you done?* asks about a completed result. It also resists **stative** verbs — verbs of knowing, believing, belonging, and the like. Sweet noticed that verbs "which express feelings, physical and mental perceptions" occur chiefly in the simple forms: *I feel ill; he likes being here; I think so*. When the verb takes on an active sense, the progressive returns: *it hurts* beside *he is hurting him*; *I hear a noise* beside *I am hearing lectures*. The restriction is a strong tendency, not an absolute law: *I'm loving this* and *she's being difficult* are ordinary in speech, the first because the feeling is presented as an experience, the second because *be* here means "behave."

**The perfect aspect.** — The **perfect** is formed with a part of *have* and the past participle: *I have walked, he had walked, they will have walked*. It does not simply locate an event in the past; it presents a past event as relevant to a later reference time. The **present perfect** relates the event to now: *I have lost my keys* (and so I cannot get in). The **past perfect**, or **pluperfect**, relates it to a past moment: *the train had left before we arrived*. The **future perfect** relates it to a future moment: *by June I will have finished*. The perfect is thus an aspect — a way of viewing the event — not a tense in the narrow sense. Shakespeare's Macbeth says, just after the murder, "I have done the deed": the deed is over, but its consequence is standing in the room. The older grammars, which listed "perfect" and "pluperfect" among the tenses, are clearest when they explain the meaning rather than the label.

**The perfect progressive.** — The **perfect progressive** combines the two: *have* + *been* + the present participle, as in *I have been waiting, he had been working, they will have been travelling*. It presents an activity as continuing up to a reference time, and often explains a present state: *I have been running* (and so I am out of breath). It differs from the simple perfect in focus. *I have painted the room* reports a completed job; *I have been painting the room* reports the activity and its recent duration, without saying the job is finished. Like the simple progressive, it avoids stative verbs; *I have been knowing him* is not English.

**Modals and the perfect.** — The modal auxiliaries combine with the perfect to look back from a point in time and pass judgement on it: *you should have told me; he might have known; they must have left already; I would have helped*. The pattern is modal + *have* + past participle, and it is the same *have* as in the perfect, not the main verb "to have." These forms are often called the **modal perfect**, and they express possibility, obligation, or regret about something that did not happen or may have happened. The *have* is often reduced to *'ve* in speech and can be misheard as *of*: *should have* becomes *should've*, and the nonstandard spelling *should of* is a common error. Notice that the modal itself does not change for past time; *could* and *might* are the past forms of *can* and *may*, but *must* and *should* keep one shape, and the past meaning is carried by the perfect after them.

**The full grid.** — Combining the four aspectual views with the three times yields twelve combinations, the set that many textbooks call the twelve tenses. The table below uses *walk*.

| | Simple | Progressive | Perfect | Perfect progressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | I walk | I am walking | I have walked | I have been walking |
| **Past** | I walked | I was walking | I had walked | I had been walking |
| **Future** | I will walk | I will be walking | I will have walked | I will have been walking |

Not every cell is equally common or equally easy. The perfect progressive passive — *the house has been being painted* — is grammatical in principle but so awkward that writers avoid it. The future perfect progressive (*I will have been waiting for two hours*) is rarer than its present and past counterparts. The grid is a map of the system, not a list of forms that occur with equal frequency.

**The passive and the tenses.** — The **passive voice** is formed with a part of *be* and the past participle, and it combines with every tense and aspect in the grid. The same event can be reported in the active (*the committee wrote the report*) or the passive (*the report was written*), and the passive shifts attention from the doer to what is acted upon. Run through the forms and the pattern is clear: *the letter is written* (simple present passive), *the letter was written* (simple past), *the letter is being written* (present progressive), *the letter was being written* (past progressive), *the letter has been written* (present perfect), *the letter had been written* (past perfect), *the letter will have been written* (future perfect). The passive is not a tense but a **voice**, a separate choice that sits alongside tense and aspect; the same sentence can be passive in any of the twelve cells, though the perfect progressive passive is so clumsy that it is usually rewritten.

**The present with non-present meaning.** — Tense forms do not always mean what their names suggest, and the present is the most flexible of them. It can refer to the future when the future is scheduled or when a subordinate clause marks it: *the train leaves at six; when he arrives, he will hear the news; if it rains, we will stay in*. Murray noted that the present "preceded by the words *when, before, after, as soon as*, &c. is sometimes used to point out the relative time of a future action." It can state a general truth or a habit: *the sun rises in the east; he goes to Germany twice a year*. It can perform an action as it is spoken, in what are called **performatives**: *I name this ship the Endeavour; I promise to pay*. And it can narrate past events as if they were happening now — the **historic present**. Murray gives an example from historical writing: "He enters the territory of the peaceable inhabitants; he fights and conquers, takes an immense booty, which he divides amongst his soldiers, and returns home to enjoy an empty triumph." The historic present is common in conversation (*so he walks in and says...*), in summaries and reviews, and in some kinds of fiction, where it quickens the narrative. It is a stylistic choice, not an error; but in formal academic writing it can confuse, because it blurs the line between the time of the telling and the time of the tale.

**"I have done" and "I did."** — The most useful practical distinction in the whole system is between the present perfect and the simple past. Both describe a past event, but they view it differently, and the choice is often decided by the time words in the sentence. The simple past is used when the event is placed in a finished period and the time is specified or understood: *I saw him yesterday; I finished the work last week; Shakespeare wrote Hamlet about 1600*. The present perfect is used when the period in which the event occurred is still open, or when the result is still current: *I have seen him this morning* (if it is still morning); *I have finished the work* (and so it is done); *Shakespeare has written plays that still hold the stage* (viewing his work as part of a continuing present). Murray put the contrast exactly: "the former denotes it in such a manner, that there is still actually remaining some part of the time to slide away, wherein we declare the thing has been done; whereas the imperfect denotes the thing or action past, in such a manner, that nothing remains of that time in which it was done." A past-time adverb such as *yesterday, last week, in 1995, then* therefore forces the simple past: *I have seen him yesterday* is not standard English. Conversely, *since, for, ever, never, just, already, yet* and *so far* go naturally with the present perfect: *I have lived here since 2010; have you ever been to Rome?* American English uses the simple past in some contexts where British English prefers the present perfect (*Did you eat yet?* beside *Have you eaten yet?*), a difference of idiom rather than of grammar. Note too that the past perfect is not simply "more past" than the simple past; it is past relative to another past point, and a sentence such as *I had finished before he came* needs that second reference point to make sense.

**Prescription and its limits.** — Several "rules" attached to this material are really preferences or myths. The **shall/will** rule is the clearest case. Lowth taught that *will* in the first person "promises or threatens" and *shall* "simply foretells," while in the second and third persons the values reverse. Murray repeated the rule, and it was drilled for two centuries. Yet careful writers have never observed it consistently, and in ordinary speech *will* has largely displaced *shall* for the simple future in all persons; *shall* survives in offers and suggestions (*Shall I open the window?*), in legal and formal prose, and in some British usage. The rule is a prescription, not a description. Two other disputed points deserve mention. The claim that the perfect is "the past tense" is wrong: the perfect is an aspect, and the present perfect is anchored in the present. And the claim that a stative verb can never be progressive is an overstatement; the tendency is real, but usage is more flexible than the rule. The same caution applies to the famous prohibitions that surround verb forms — against splitting an infinitive (*to boldly go*), against ending a sentence with a preposition, and against opening one with *And*. Each describes a habit of formal prose rather than a law of the language, and each has been violated by writers whose English nobody questions. What is genuinely fixed is the morphology: the principal parts, the auxiliary patterns, and the constraints on which form follows which.

## Chronology

- **1762** — Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* sets out the "Indefinite" and "Definite" times of the verb and the rule for *shall* and *will*.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray's *English Grammar* classifies six tenses and explains the present perfect against the imperfect.
- **1818** — William Cobbett's *A Grammar of the English Language* argues that English has only three times and that the extra distinctions "only tend to bewilder."
- **1891–1898** — Henry Sweet's *A New English Grammar* distinguishes the "indefinite" tenses from the "definite" (progressive) forms and describes their aspectual meaning.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen's *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles* treats tense and aspect historically; his *The Philosophy of Grammar* (1924) argues that English has only two tenses.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* comments on *shall* and *will*, on the perfect, and on disputed points of idiom.
- **1985** — Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech and Svartvik, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, describe tense and aspect in English and retain a future-tense category.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, analyse English as having primary tense (present and past) and no future tense, treating *will* as a modal.

## Questions to carry

- If English marks only present and past on the verb, is it accurate to call *will walk* a future tense?
- Why does the present perfect resist *yesterday* but accept *since 2010*?
- What does the progressive add to a sentence that the simple form does not?
- Is the historic present a legitimate narrative choice, or does it blur the time of the telling?
- When a usage guide states a rule that good writers ignore, what is the rule actually doing?
