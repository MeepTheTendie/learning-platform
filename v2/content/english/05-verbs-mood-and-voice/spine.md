# Verbs: mood, voice, and auxiliaries

**Period:** English grammar

**Why the verb carries the clause.** — Every English clause has a verb at its centre, and the verb changes when the clause's job changes. Turn a statement into a command and the verb changes shape (*you close the door* becomes *close the door*); turn the action around so that the receiver leads, and it changes again (*the dog bit the postman* becomes *the postman was bitten by the dog*). Mood, voice, and the auxiliary system describe that machinery.

## The work a verb does

**Finite and non-finite verbs.** — A finite verb carries tense and agrees with a subject: *she walks*, *they walked*, *he is*. A non-finite verb has no subject agreement and no tense of its own: the infinitive (*to walk*), the present participle and gerund (*walking*), and the past participle (*walked*). Only a finite verb has mood; the verb in *Walking is good exercise* is a gerund with no mood at all.

**The five categories.** — A finite verb carries up to five kinds of information: person, number, tense, mood, and voice. Mood and voice are the subjects of this unit.

**Mood defined.** — Mood (older grammars say *mode*) is the form of the verb that shows how the speaker presents the action: as a fact, as a command, or as something wished, demanded, or supposed. Lowth, in *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762), wrote: "The Mode is the *Manner* of representing the Action or Passion." English has three moods that matter — the indicative, the imperative, and the subjunctive — and the non-finite forms, which stand outside mood altogether. Some older grammars add a separate conditional or optative mood; most modern descriptions either fold those uses into the subjunctive or treat them as uses of the modal auxiliaries.

**The indicative.** — The indicative is the mood of statement, belief, and question, the unmarked default: *The sun rises in the east. Did you lock the door? Shakespeare wrote thirty-seven plays.* It also carries exclamations (*What a mess this is!*) and the ordinary narration of events.

**The imperative.** — The imperative is the mood of command, request, advice, and instruction. It uses the base form of the verb and normally has no subject: *Close the door. Please sit down. Mind the gap.* Shakespeare's Mark Antony cries, *Cry 'Havoc!' and let slip the dogs of war* (Julius Caesar). The negative is made with *do* (*Don't touch it*), the subject may be expressed for emphasis (*You sit here*), and first- and third-person imperatives are made with *let* (*Let's go. Let him speak. Let there be light*). The imperative of *be* is simply *be* (*Be quiet*), and it has no tense; it always points at action still to come.

**The subjunctive.** — The subjunctive is the mood of what is not asserted as fact: wishes, demands, hypotheses, and concessions. English once marked it with endings, but those were lost, so the subjunctive is now shown either by the base form of the verb or by the special form *were*. Three uses survive.

First, the mandative (or *be*-) subjunctive: a base-form verb in a *that*-clause after verbs and expressions of demanding, insisting, requiring, proposing, or recommending, and after adjectives and nouns of necessity. *I insist that he be present. The committee demands that the report be made public. It is essential that she go. It is important that every applicant be interviewed.* In British English a *should*-construction often replaces the bare subjunctive (*I insist that he should be present*), while in American English the bare subjunctive is standard.

Second, the *were*-subjunctive, also called the irrealis or past subjunctive: the special form *were* used with any subject in a hypothetical or contrary-to-fact clause. *If I were you, I would apologize. She talks as if she were the manager. I wish I were taller.* It appears after *wish*, *as if*, *as though*, *suppose*, *would rather*, *if only*, and in *if I were to*. Only *be* keeps a distinct subjunctive form here; every other verb uses its ordinary past tense, so *If I knew the answer, I would tell you*, not *If I know the answer*.

Third, fossilised subjunctives survive in fixed phrases: *Long live the King. God save the Queen. Be that as it may. Come what may. Suffice it to say. If need be.* *Lest* still takes the base form: *Lest we forget. Take heed, lest he fall* — Lowth's example, from 1 Corinthians 10:12. After *if* and *though*, the subjunctive has mostly given way to the indicative.

**Was or were?** — Lowth and Murray insisted that hypothetical clauses take the subjunctive (*if it were so*). Modern English preserves *were* in *if I were you* and in formal writing, but *if I was you* is common in speech and increasingly accepted in print. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) call *were* an irrealis form, surviving mainly with *be*, since other verbs have no separate irrealis form. The mandative subjunctive, by contrast, has grown more common in American English and is spreading in British English. The old rule that every *if* clause requires the subjunctive is a preference of the eighteenth-century grammarians, not a fact about English, and even Lowth admitted that "by use they often admit of the Indicative."

## Auxiliaries: be, have, do

**What an auxiliary is.** — An auxiliary (from Latin *auxilium*, help) is a verb that appears with another verb to form a tense, a question, a negative, a passive, or a mood. Cobbett, in *A Grammar of the English Language* (1818), put it plainly: "the word auxiliary means helper." English has two sets: the primary auxiliaries *be*, *have*, and *do*, and the modal auxiliaries *can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *shall*, *should*, *will*, *would*, *must*, *ought*, with the marginal *need* and *dare*.

**Be.** — *Be* has three jobs: as a linking verb it joins a subject to a complement (*She is a doctor*); as the progressive auxiliary it is followed by the present participle (*She is running*); as the passive auxiliary it is followed by the past participle (*The window was broken*). It also carries the imperative (*Be quiet*), the subjunctive (*If I were you*), and the perfect progressive (*She has been waiting*). It is the most irregular verb in the language: *am, are, is, was, were, been, being*.

**Have.** — *Have* forms the perfect with the past participle: *I have eaten. She had left before we arrived. They will have finished by noon.* It also forms the perfect progressive (*have been* + *-ing*: *She has been waiting*) and the perfect passive (*have been* + past participle: *The letter has been sent*). *Have* is also a full verb meaning "possess": *I have a cold.*

**Do.** — *Do* does three things as an auxiliary. It supports questions, negatives, emphatic affirmatives, and negative imperatives: *Do you know her? I don't know her. I do know her. Don't go.* It provides code and ellipsis, standing in for an understood verb phrase: *She sings better than he does. You promised, and so did I.* And it works as a pro-verb standing for another verb: *He washed the car and she did the dishes.* Cobbett distinguished *do* as a principal verb (*I do my work*) from *do* as a helper: "Do and did, as helpers, are used with neuter as well as with active verbs; for here it is not their business to supply the place of other verbs, but merely to add strength to affirmations and negations, or to mark time." *Do* is not used with *be* (*Are you ready?*, not *Do you be ready?*) or with the modals (*Can you swim?*, not *Do you can swim?*).

**The NICE properties.** — Huddleston and Pullum (2002) list four tests that separate auxiliaries from ordinary verbs, remembered as NICE. Negation: *not* attaches directly after the auxiliary (*He will not go*, not *He goes not*). Inversion: the auxiliary inverts with the subject in a question (*Will he go?*). Code: it can stand alone for an understood verb phrase (*He will go, and so will she*). Emphasis: it can carry contrastive stress (*He will go, whatever you say*). A modal auxiliary also lacks the third-person *-s* and the non-finite forms: there is no *musts*, no *to must*, no *musting*, which is why *must*, *can*, and *will* are called defective.

## Voice: active and passive

**The active voice.** — In the active voice the subject is the doer: *The dog bit the postman. Shakespeare wrote Hamlet.*

**The passive voice.** — In the passive voice the subject is the receiver of the action, and the verb is *be* plus the past participle: *The postman was bitten by the dog. Hamlet was written by Shakespeare.* Murray (1829) defined a passive verb as one that "expresses a passion or a suffering, or the receiving of an action" and "necessarily implies an object acted upon, and an agent by which it is acted upon." The passive is not a tense; it is a voice, a way of arranging the clause. The same event can be reported in either voice without any change of time.

**Forming the passive.** — Four steps: the active object becomes the subject; *be* takes the tense of the active verb; the past participle follows; and the old subject, if kept, goes into a *by*-phrase.

- Present simple: *eats* / *is eaten*
- Present progressive: *is eating* / *is being eaten*
- Present perfect: *has eaten* / *has been eaten*
- Past simple: *ate* / *was eaten*
- Future: *will eat* / *will be eaten*
- Modal: *must eat* / *must be eaten*
- Infinitive: *to eat* / *to be eaten*

Only verbs that take a direct object — transitive verbs — can normally be passivised. An intransitive verb cannot: *She slept* has no passive \**She was slept*.

**The get-passive.** — English also has a passive made with *get* instead of *be*: *He got fired. The window got broken.* The *get*-passive is colloquial and often suggests something unfortunate or a change of state. It does not take a *by*-phrase as freely. Sweet (1892) noticed the growth of this construction and called related forms passival.

**When the passive is the right choice.** — The passive earns its place in several situations. The agent may be unknown or not worth naming (*My bike was stolen*), or obvious (*The thief was arrested*). The action may matter more than the doer (*The novel was published in 1859*). The passive can keep the topic in subject position and put the new information at the end: *The manuscript was discovered in a cupboard* moves from the known to the new. Impersonal and scientific writing often prefers it (*The samples were heated to 200 degrees*), and it can smooth over a vague agent (*He was given a watch*). The same device can be evasive — *Mistakes were made* names no one — which is a reason to notice it, not a reason to ban it.

**When to prefer the active.** — Use the active when the doer matters, is known, and can be named: *The committee rejected the proposal* is stronger than *The proposal was rejected by the committee.* Chains of passives are the symptom of flabby prose: *It was decided that the report should be prepared and then submitted for approval* hides three actors. Fowler (1924) warned against the compound passive *This step has been attempted to be justified*, calling it "an incorrect and slovenly makeshift," because the real object of *attempted* is the whole phrase *to justify this step*, not *this step*. When the active subject can be stated, state it.

**Passive and progressive are not the same thing.** — Both progressive and passive use a form of *be*, but they use different participles, and the participle is what tells them apart.

- Progressive: *be* + present participle (*-ing*), reporting an action in progress. *She is eating. They were laughing.*
- Passive: *be* + past participle, reporting an action received. *The cake is eaten. The window was broken.*
- Progressive passive: *be* + *being* + past participle, both at once. *The cake is being eaten. The house was being painted.*
- Perfect passive: *have* + *been* + past participle. *The cake has been eaten.*
- Perfect progressive: *have* + *been* + present participle. *She has been eating.*

The test is the participle, not the *be*. If the form after *be* ends in *-ing*, the clause is progressive; if it is a past participle, the clause is passive. *He is reading* is progressive; *He is read to* is passive. *The song is playing* is progressive; *The song is played* is passive; *The song is being played* is both. A past participle can also describe a state rather than an event, and then the construction is called an adjectival passive: *The door was closed* may mean that someone closed it (an event) or that it was in a closed state. Context decides.

## Transitive, intransitive, and linking verbs

**Transitive verbs take a direct object.** — A direct object is the person or thing affected by the verb, and it can be found by asking *what?* or *whom?* after the verb: *She read the letter* (read what? the letter). A transitive verb can be passivised: *The letter was read.* The object can become the subject of a passive clause.

**Intransitive verbs do not take an object.** — *She slept. The baby cried. The train arrived.* They can be followed by adverbs and prepositional phrases but not by a direct object: *She slept soundly. She slept in the chair.* An intransitive verb has no passive: \**She was slept* is not English.

**Ambitransitive verbs do both.** — Very many English verbs are transitive in one use and intransitive in another, and the construction decides which: *She runs every morning* and *She runs a bakery*; *The door opened* and *She opened the door*; *The ice melted* and *The sun melted the ice.* Lowth (1762) saw this clearly: "In English many Verbs are used both in an Active and a Neuter signification, the construction only determining of which kind they are." This ergative pattern — the same verb used with the affected thing as subject — is one of the most productive features of English.

**Cognate objects.** — An intransitive verb can sometimes take a noun of related meaning, called a cognate object: *dream a dream*, *live a good life*, *sing a song*, *die a death.* Cobbett objected to stretching this too far: *I dream a dream* is allowed because the dream is genuinely had, but a neuter verb cannot simply be given any object. *Sleep the sleep of the just* is idiomatic; \**sleep the bed* is not.

**Linking verbs and complements.** — A linking verb, or copula, does not act on an object; it joins the subject to a complement that describes or identifies it. The central linking verb is *be*: *She is a doctor. The weather is cold. All the world's a stage* (As You Like It). Others include *become, seem, appear, remain, stay, grow, turn, prove*, and the sensory verbs *feel, taste, smell, sound*, and *look*: *She became president. He seems tired. The soup tastes good.* The complement is either a predicative noun, which renames the subject (*She became president*), or a predicative adjective (*He seems tired*). Because it describes the subject rather than acting on an object, it cannot be passivised: \**A doctor was become by her* is not English.

**Adjective, not adverb, after a linking verb.** — Linking verbs take adjectives, not adverbs. *The soup tastes good*, not *tastes well*. *She looks happy*, not *looks happily*. *I feel bad about it* (I am sorry) is standard; *I feel badly* originally meant that one's sense of touch was poor, and many usage writers still reserve it for that. Compare the linking use with the transitive use: *feel* links in *I feel cold* but is transitive in *I felt the fabric*; *taste* links in *The soup tastes good* but is transitive in *She tasted the soup.* Ask whether the word after the verb describes the subject; if it does, the verb is linking and the word is a complement.

## Phrasal verbs and prepositional verbs

**Verb + particle.** — A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small adverb called a particle, and the two together have a meaning that is not the sum of the parts: *give up* (surrender, quit), *turn off*, *look up* (search for), *put off* (postpone), *break down*, *run into* (meet by chance), *take off.* The particle can usually move to the other side of the object: *turn off the light* or *turn the light off*; *look up the word* or *look the word up.* But when the object is a pronoun, the particle must follow it: *turn it off*, *look it up*, never \**turn off it*.

**Separable and inseparable.** — Phrasal verbs whose particle can move are called separable. Some are inseparable: the particle must stay next to the verb: *look after the baby* (not \**look the baby after*), *get over an illness*, *call on a friend.* There is no simple rule; dictionaries mark them, and speakers learn them one by one.

**Verb + preposition.** — A prepositional verb is a verb plus a preposition that takes an object. The preposition cannot move: *look after the children*, *listen to the radio*, *wait for the bus*, *rely on her*, *deal with the problem.* The noun after the preposition is its object, so it takes the object case: *Wait for him and me*, not *for he and I.* The test against a phrasal verb is position: a particle can follow the object (*turn the light off*), but a preposition cannot (\**look the children after*). Stress helps too: in a phrasal verb the particle is often stressed (*TURN it OFF*), while the preposition is usually unstressed (*look AFter the children*).

**Three-part verbs.** — Some combinations add both a particle and a preposition, giving a phrasal-prepositional verb: *put up with* (tolerate), *look forward to*, *get away with*, *come up with*, *cut down on*, *make up for.* *I cannot put up with the noise.* The preposition governs the object, so the object case is required: *put up with him*, not *with he.*

**The preposition-at-end myth.** — English prepositions often stand at the end of a clause, especially when their object is a relative pronoun that has been omitted or moved: *the friends I was travelling with*; *the book I got it from*; *What are you looking at?* Fowler, in *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926), called the ban on this "a cherished superstition" and pointed to "the incurable English instinct for putting them late"; in *The King's English* (1924) he showed how "the superstitious avoidance of a preposition at the end" produces clumsy sentences. The rule was imported from Latin, where a preposition must precede its noun. Dryden went back through his essays to remove final prepositions and made some sentences worse. Shakespeare strands prepositions freely: *We are such stuff as dreams are made on* (The Tempest). Modern grammars (Huddleston and Pullum 2002) treat the stranded preposition as fully grammatical. Avoiding it can be a matter of formal style, but it is not a rule of grammar.

**The passive of a phrasal or prepositional verb.** — Because the particle or preposition can be stranded, phrasal and prepositional verbs can be passivised, and the particle or preposition stays with the verb at the end of the clause. *They turned the offer down* becomes *The offer was turned down.* *The nurse looked after the children* becomes *The children were looked after by the nurse.* *We will look into the matter* becomes *The matter will be looked into.* *They put up with him* becomes *He was put up with.* Murray's early example is *she smiled on him; he was smiled on by fortune in every undertaking.* With a separable phrasal verb the particle does not jump to the front: *The offer was turned down*, never \**The offer was down turned.* With a prepositional verb the preposition is left at the end: *He was looked after.* Sweet (1892) explained the history: in Old English only verbs that governed the accusative could be passivised, so *he thanked her* had no passive; once the old case distinctions were lost, dative and prepositional objects could become subjects, which is why *she was given a watch* and *he was smiled on* are now possible.

## Prescription, description, and three disputed rules

**The split infinitive.** — There is no rule against putting a word between *to* and the verb: *to boldly go*, *to really understand.* The construction is old and appears in good writers. Fowler (1924) called the belief that splitting "makes the difference between a good and a bad writer" a "curious superstition," though he thought the split infinitive was often "an ugly thing." The advice that survives is practical, not grammatical: avoid splitting when the adverb would read better elsewhere, or when the split is wide and awkward (*to carefully and systematically examine*). Do not contort a sentence to avoid it.

**Ending with a preposition.** — As above, a myth. *That depends on what they are cut with* is idiomatic English; the "corrected" version, *That depends on with what they are cut*, is worse.

**Starting with and or but.** — Also a myth. The King James Bible, Shakespeare, and almost every English novelist begin sentences with *And* or *But.* The advice against it is a schoolroom preference, useful for training young writers to subordinate, but not a fact about the language. *And the whole earth was of one language* (Genesis 11:1) opens a chapter, not an error.

**How to judge a disputed rule.** — Ask three questions. Is it a fact about English, or a preference? Whose usage does it describe — formal edited prose, or ordinary speech? Does following it make the sentence clearer? Descriptive grammarians (Sweet, Jespersen, and the modern corpus grammarians) record what speakers and writers do; prescriptive grammarians (Lowth, Murray, Fowler) recommend what they think best. The recommendation is worth hearing, but it is not a law of grammar.

## Exercises

1. Name the mood: *Close the window.* *It is vital that the window be closed.* *The window is closed.* *If the window were closed, we would be warmer.*
2. Choose the correct form: *I insist that she (be / is) told.* *If I (was / were) taller, I would play basketball.* *He spoke as though he (was / were) certain.*
3. Turn active into passive: *The chef cooked the meal.* *Someone has stolen my bag.* *They will announce the results tomorrow.*
4. Passive or progressive? *The song is playing.* *The song is played at every wedding.* *The song is being played too loudly.*
5. Transitive or intransitive? *She sang a song.* *She sang beautifully.* *The door opened.* *He opened the door.*
6. Linking verb or not? *The milk smells sour.* *She smelled the milk.* *He looked tired.* *He looked at the map.*
7. Fix the phrasal verb: *Turn off it.* *Look the baby after.* *The offer was turned down.*

Suggested answers: 1 imperative, subjunctive, indicative, subjunctive; 2 *be*, *were*, *were*; 4 progressive, passive, progressive passive; 5 transitive, intransitive, intransitive, transitive; 6 linking, transitive, linking, transitive; 7 *Turn it off*, *Look after the baby*.

## Chronology

- **1586** — William Bullokar, *Pamphlet for Grammar*, the first grammar of English.
- **1653** — John Wallis, *Grammatica Linguae Anglicanae*.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*; sets out the three modes and the subjunctive after conjunctions.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*; the standard school grammar for two generations.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*; separates the past tense from the passive participle.
- **1892–1898** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar, Logical and Historical*; the passive described historically.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*; 1924 *The Philosophy of Grammar*.
- **1906; 2nd ed. 1924** — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English*; **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, which calls the ban on final prepositions a superstition.
- **1957** — Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures*; the passive analysed as a transformation.
- **1985** — Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech, and Svartvik, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*; the NICE properties, the irrealis *were*, and stranded prepositions as fully grammatical.

## Questions to carry

- Why does English keep a distinct subjunctive only for *be*, and what does that fact say about the mood's history?
- If the passive is not a tense but a rearrangement of the clause, what exactly changes when a sentence moves from active to passive?
- How can the same *be* + participle string be progressive in one sentence and passive in another, and what does the answer show about the role of the participle?
- Is *I feel badly* a mistake, a hypercorrection, or a real distinction, and what evidence would settle it?
- When a phrasal verb is passivised, why does the particle stay at the end, and what does that reveal about the difference between a particle and a preposition?
