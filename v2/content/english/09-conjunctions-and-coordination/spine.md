# Conjunctions and coordination

**Period:** English grammar, sixteenth to twenty-first centuries

## The work of a conjunction

**What a conjunction is.** — A **conjunction** is a word whose work is to link two or more elements and to show the relation between them. "The Conjunction connects or *joins together* Sentences; so as out of two to make one Sentence," wrote Robert Lowth in 1762. The elements it links may be single words (*bread and butter*), phrases (*in the morning or in the evening*), or whole clauses (*The rain stopped, and we went out*). A conjunction describes nothing in the world; it is a **function word**, like a preposition or an article, and what it carries is a relation — addition, contrast, alternative, cause, result, condition, concession.

**Coordination and subordination.** — English links clauses in two different ways, and the difference is the central fact of this topic. **Coordination** joins elements of equal grammatical rank: two words, two phrases, or two clauses that could each stand in the same position. **Subordination** makes one clause a dependent part of another, so that it cannot stand alone. The first is the work of the **coordinating conjunctions**; the second, of the **subordinating conjunctions**. The two patterns are sometimes called **parataxis** (placing side by side) and **hypotaxis** (placing under). The distinction is not decoration: a conjunction tells the reader whether the parts it joins are equals or whether one of them is grammatically the boss.

**Three classes by function.** — The old grammars divided conjunctions into **copulative** and **disjunctive**. Lowth's copulatives "connect, or continue, the Sentence, by expressing an addition, *and*; a supposition, or condition, *if*, *as*; a cause, *because*; a motive, *that*; an inference, *therefore*." His disjunctives "connect and continue the Sentence; but ... express Opposition of meaning in different degrees: as, *or*, *but*, *than*, *altho'*, *unless*." Goold Brown, writing in 1851, kept those two classes and added a third, the **corresponsive** conjunctions — the paired words that answer one another, such as *either...or*, *neither...nor*, *both...and*, and *though...yet*. Modern grammar uses the same three ideas under the names **coordinating**, **subordinating**, and **correlative**.

**Conjunctions and the other connectives.** — Not every word that connects is a conjunction. A **relative pronoun** (*who, which, that*) both refers to a noun and introduces a clause. A **preposition** links a noun to the rest of the sentence. And a **conjunctive adverb** (*however, therefore, moreover*) signals a logical relation between clauses without joining them grammatically. The last of these is easily mistaken for a conjunction, and it gets its own section below.

## Coordinating conjunctions

**The seven.** — The coordinating conjunctions are conventionally listed as *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*, remembered by the mnemonic **FANBOYS**. The list is a teaching device of the twentieth century, not a canon handed down by the grammarians: Lowth grouped *for*, *and*, and *or* as copulatives and *but*, *or*, *than*, *although*, and *unless* as disjunctives, with no seven-word set anywhere in view. The list is nevertheless useful, because these are the words that behave alike in the one test that matters: they join two independent clauses without making either one dependent.

**What each one means.** — *And* adds. *Or* offers an alternative. *But* marks a contrast. *Nor* adds a second negative after a first. *For* gives a reason, and belongs to a formal, slightly literary register. *Yet* marks a contrast or a concession. *So* gives a result. The meanings overlap, and the choice is often rhetorical rather than logical: *He is old but strong* and *He is old yet strong* say almost the same thing, with *yet* sounding more deliberate.

**What coordination joins.** — Coordination joins **like with like**. It can join nouns (*bread and butter*), adjectives (*slow but steady*), verbs (*come and see*), adverbs (*quickly and quietly*), prepositional phrases (*over the river and through the woods*), and clauses (*I came and I saw*). What it cannot do, in the standard account, is join a noun to a clause or an adjective to a prepositional phrase; the two **conjuncts** (the joined elements) must belong to the same grammatical category. This requirement is **parallelism**, and when a writer violates it the result is a sentence that feels broken: *She likes swimming and to run* fails because *swimming* is a gerund and *to run* an infinitive, though both are verb forms. The repair is to make the conjuncts match: *She likes swimming and running*, or *She likes to swim and to run*.

**Coordination is not clause-only.** — Lowth and Murray often wrote as if a conjunction joined sentences, and a long tradition repeated that conjunctions "connect sentences." Goold Brown objected: in *The king and queen are an amiable pair*, *and* joins two nouns, not two clauses, and in *a being of more than human dignity* the word *than* joins two words. The point is grammatical, not pedantic: if *and* only joined clauses, the rules for a verb agreeing with a compound subject would have nothing to work on. Coordination is a general operation on constituents, not a special property of sentences.

**The disputed members.** — Three members of the seven are contested. *For* introduces a reason but cannot be moved or preceded by a second coordinator the way *and* and *but* can, and Huddleston and Pullum classify it as a preposition that takes a clause. *So* and *yet* are adverbs in many of their uses. Some modern grammars therefore recognise only *and*, *or*, *but*, and *nor* as true coordinators. The disagreement is not a quarrel about correctness; it is a question of which tests define the category.

## Commas with coordinating conjunctions

**The rule.** — When a coordinating conjunction joins two **independent clauses** — clauses that could each stand as a sentence — a comma goes before the conjunction: *The rain stopped, and we went out.* The comma marks the seam between two statements of equal rank. The same rule covers *but*, *or*, *nor*, *for*, *yet*, and *so*: *I was tired, so I went to bed.*

**When the comma is optional.** — Short, closely parallel clauses often go without it, and good writers have always left it out: *I came and I saw*; *Man proposes and God disposes*; *The spirit is willing but the flesh is weak.* The comma is a signal to the reader, not a grammatical requirement, and when the clauses are brief the signal is unnecessary. A comma is also unnecessary when the conjunction joins two verbs that share one subject (*She opened the door and walked in*): there is only one clause, so there is no seam to mark.

**The comma splice.** — A **comma splice** joins two independent clauses with a comma and no conjunction: *The rain stopped, we went out.* A **run-on** (or fused sentence) joins them with no punctuation at all: *The rain stopped we went out.* Both are treated as errors in edited English, because the comma is not strong enough to carry the boundary by itself. Four repairs are standard: a full stop (*The rain stopped. We went out.*), a semicolon (*The rain stopped; we went out.*), a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (*The rain stopped, so we went out.*), or subordination (*When the rain stopped, we went out.*). The comma splice is not a grammatical crime in every tradition — eighteenth-century prose is full of them — but modern edited prose treats it as one.

**The serial comma.** — When three or more items are coordinated, the comma before the last *and* is the **serial comma** (or Oxford comma): *eggs, bacon, and toast*. American book publishing usually keeps it; British newspapers often drop it: *eggs, bacon and toast*. Neither is more correct. The serial comma can prevent ambiguity — *I thank my parents, Ayn Rand and God* reads as though the parents are Ayn Rand and God, while *I thank my parents, Ayn Rand, and God* does not — and it can also create a false grouping, which is why no rule can replace judgment.

**Prescription and description.** — The comma rule is a convention of edited prose, codified in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by printers and style guides. It is not a fact about the English language. Writers who follow it are obeying a house style; writers who do not are not making a grammatical mistake, though they may be judged by readers who expect the convention.

## Correlative conjunctions

**The paired conjunctions.** — **Correlative** (or **corresponsive**) conjunctions come in pairs, with one half announcing a second: *either...or*, *neither...nor*, *both...and*, *not only...but also*, *whether...or*, *as...as*, and *no sooner...than*. Lowth called them "Correspondent Conjunctions belonging to" the first, and gave *although...yet*, *whether...or*, *either...or*, and *neither...nor* as his examples. Goold Brown's list is nearly identical.

**Parallelism is the rule that matters.** — The conjuncts that follow the two halves must match in form. *She likes swimming and to run* is wrong for the same reason it is wrong with a single *and*. The fault is especially common with *not only...but also*: *He not only plays the piano but also the violin* sets a verb phrase against a noun phrase. The repair is to put each half immediately before the same kind of element: *He plays not only the piano but also the violin.* The same test governs *either...or*: *He either ate the cake or the pie* is faulty because *either* stands before the verb while *or* stands before a noun; *He ate either the cake or the pie* is correct. The prescriptive rule that *either* and *neither* refer to two things only, with *any* and *none* for more, is a usage preference rather than a law, and *neither* with three or more items is common.

**Agreement with either/or and neither/nor.** — When two subjects are joined by *or* or *nor*, the verb normally agrees with the nearer one. This is the **proximity rule**: *Neither the manager nor the workers were ready*; *Neither the workers nor the manager was ready.* Some older authorities prefer agreement with the first subject, or with the pair taken together, and the disagreement has never been settled. What is settled is that the construction invites a second problem, in the pronoun: *Neither John nor Mary knew his own mind* is awkward because *his* cannot comfortably cover both. The usual escapes are to recast the sentence (*John and Mary were alike irresolute*) or to repeat the pronoun in full.

## Subordinating conjunctions

**The common subordinators.** — The subordinating conjunctions include *because, although, though, if, unless, when, whenever, while, since, until, till, after, before, as, whereas, so that, in order that, provided that, as if, as though, even though*, and *once*. Their work is to introduce a clause and to mark it as dependent — as a part of the larger sentence rather than a statement in its own right.

**The adverbial clause.** — A subordinating conjunction introduces an **adverbial clause**, a dependent clause that modifies the main verb or the whole main clause. It answers *when?*, *where?*, *why?*, *under what condition?*, or *in spite of what?*: *When the rain stopped, we went out*; *We stayed because the fire was warm*; *Although it was late, she kept working.* The clause can come first or last. When it comes first, it is followed by a comma; when it comes last, it usually is not. The comma is not decoration: without it, *When the rain stopped we went out* can force the reader to re-read the boundary.

**Subordination versus coordination.** — The two patterns can express similar relations, but they rank the clauses differently, and the ranking is what changes the emphasis. *He was tired, so he left* coordinates two statements of equal weight. *Because he was tired, he left* makes the tiredness a reason and the leaving the main assertion. Move the subordinator and the emphasis moves with it: *He left because he was tired* puts the main news first and the reason after. English tends to place the most important information at the end (**end-focus**) and the longest, heaviest element at the end (**end-weight**), so a writer who wants a reason to land hard will subordinate the other clause and let the reason fall last. The choice between coordination and subordination is therefore one of the strongest tools of emphasis in the language, and it costs no extra words.

**Other dependent clauses.** — Not every dependent clause is adverbial. A **noun clause** introduced by *that*, *whether*, or *if* fills a subject or object slot: *That she lied surprised no one*; *I know whether he came.* A **relative clause** introduced by *who*, *which*, or *that* modifies a noun: *The man who came to dinner stayed a week.* The traditional test — can the clause stand alone? — separates all of these from the main clause, and the label tells you what the clause is doing.

**The same word, two jobs.** — Several subordinators carry more than one sense, and the sense can change the grammar. *While* is temporal in *While I was reading, the phone rang* and concessive in *While I agree, I have doubts.* *Since* is temporal in *Since we last met, much has changed* and causal in *Since you ask, I will tell you.* *As* can mark time, cause, manner, or comparison. The context, not the word, decides.

## Conjunctive adverbs

**What they are.** — The **conjunctive adverbs** include *however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, furthermore, thus, hence, still, otherwise, meanwhile, then, besides, indeed*, and *namely*. Goold Brown's name for them was exact: they are adverbs that "perform the office of conjunctions, and serve to connect sentences, as well as to express some circumstance of time, place, degree, or the like." He listed *however, moreover, nevertheless, therefore, consequently*, and *furthermore* among the words "most frequently used as conjunctive adverbs," and he noticed that grammarians could not agree whether they belonged to the one part of speech or the other.

**They are not conjunctions.** — This is the crucial point, and it explains the commonest punctuation mistake in English. A conjunctive adverb is an adverb: it modifies its own clause and signals a logical relation, but it does not join two independent clauses. *I was tired, however I kept working* is therefore a **comma splice**, exactly as *The rain stopped, we went out* is. The relation is real, but the word carrying it has no power to bind the clauses. There are three repairs: a semicolon with the adverb (*I was tired; however, I kept working*), a full stop with the adverb (*I was tired. However, I kept working*), or a genuine conjunction (*I was tired, but I kept working*).

**Punctuation.** — The settled convention is a semicolon before the conjunctive adverb and a comma after it: *The plan was expensive; nevertheless, we approved it.* When the adverb moves inside its clause, the commas move with it: *The plan was, nevertheless, approved.* When it ends the clause, no comma precedes it: *We approved it nevertheless.* The pattern is a convention, not a law, but it is the one readers expect.

**However and but.** — The confusion between *however* and *but* is worth stating plainly. *But* is a conjunction and can join clauses with a comma. *However* is an adverb and cannot. A writer who reaches for *however* in the middle of a compound sentence has usually chosen the wrong word, and the fix is either a semicolon or *but*.

## Coordination or subordination: choosing

**Equal weight or ranked weight.** — Coordination says: these two things stand on the same level. Subordination says: one of them is the main point and the other is background, cause, condition, or concession. The same content can be arranged either way, and the arrangement is the argument. *The king died, and the queen died* gives two events equal weight; *The king died, and then the queen died of grief* makes the second event follow from the first; *After the king died, the queen died of grief* subordinates the first to the second. The famous advice to prefer subordination is really advice to say which idea is doing the work.

**Fowler's warning and the modern reversal.** — In *The King's English* (1924), H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler complained of what they called the **spot-plague**: the modern habit of writing in short, unconnected full stops, with "most of the conjunctions" gone and the semicolon avoided. They valued the older **period**, the long sentence "carefully worked out with a view to symmetry, balance, and degrees of subordination," and they warned that simplification, overdone, "becomes an affectation." Their complaint was stylistic, and later writers have reversed the preference: the short coordinate sentence, used deliberately, is now a mark of plain style. Both are right about the effect, and neither is stating a rule of grammar.

**The "and" of narrative.** — Two figures of style depend on coordination. **Polysyndeton** repeats the conjunction (*And God said, Let there be light: and there was light*, Genesis 1:3), producing a slow, insistent rhythm. **Asyndeton** omits it (*I came, I saw, I conquered*), producing speed. Neither is an error; each is a choice, and the choice is audible.

## And/or, nor, and other agreement problems

**And/or.** — The formula *and/or* came into English from legal drafting, where it was meant to cover three possibilities at once: A, B, or both. It saves a line and loses precision, since *and* and *or* do not mean the same thing and the slash does not tell the reader which is intended. Careful writers spell the possibilities out: *A, B, or both*; *A or B or both*. The construction is a borrowing from contract language, not a part of the ordinary grammar of coordination.

**Compound subjects with and.** — Two subjects joined by *and* normally take a plural verb: *The cat and the dog are asleep.* But when the two nouns name a single thing or a single idea, the verb is singular: *Bread and butter is all I want*; *The long and the short of it is that we were late.* The test is whether the two words form one notion. A related pattern is *each* or *every* before both subjects, which keeps the verb singular: *Every boy and every girl is expected.*

**Or and nor: the proximity rule.** — When subjects are joined by *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer: *Neither the president nor the members were ready*; *Neither the members nor the president was ready.* Some authorities prefer agreement with the first subject; others prefer a plural verb when the subjects differ in number. The disagreement is old, and the safest course is to recast the sentence so that both subjects have the same number. *Neither of them is* takes a singular verb, because *neither* is the subject and is singular.

**There is and there are.** — Existential *there* agrees with the notional subject that follows: *There is a problem*; *There are two problems.* With a coordinated subject, usage wavers, and the singular is common in speech even before a plural: *There was a table and four chairs.*

**Not only ... but also and inversion.** — When *not only* begins a clause, the subject and auxiliary invert: *Not only did he arrive late, but he also forgot the wine.* The inversion is the same one that follows *never* and *nor*, and it is required, not optional.

## Starting a sentence with And or But

**The myth.** — The rule that a sentence should not begin with *And* or *But* is not in Lowth or Murray, and it is not a fact about English. The King James Bible opens verse after verse with *And* (*And God said...*; *And it came to pass...*), Shakespeare begins speeches with *But*, and the prohibition itself seems to be a product of nineteenth- and twentieth-century schoolbooks, where it was offered as a cure for monotony rather than as a grammatical law. Huddleston and Pullum treat *and* and *but* as coordinators that can stand at the head of a sentence, and the usage is everywhere in published prose. What the rule sometimes means is that a writer has begun too many sentences the same way; that is a problem of rhythm, not of grammar.

**What the choice does.** — A sentence-initial *And* or *But* is a deliberate turn toward speech. It suggests that the thought is continuing from what came before, and it gives the sentence a conversational push. *But* at the head of a sentence often marks a sharp reversal: *We had no money. But we had each other.* Used once, it lands; used in every sentence, it flattens. The decision belongs to style, and the grammar permits it.

## Chronology

- **c. 1586** — William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar*, the first grammar of English written in English, treats conjunctions on the Latin model.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines the conjunction, divides conjunctions into copulative and disjunctive, lists the "correspondent" pairs, and argues that some conjunctions govern the subjunctive.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, popularises Lowth's scheme and fixes the teaching of connectives for a century.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, teaches conjunction and sentence-making to ordinary readers and insists that points be used to show sense.
- **1851** — Goold Brown, *The Grammar of English Grammars*, separates copulative, disjunctive, and corresponsive conjunctions and describes the conjunctive adverbs that "perform the office of conjunctions."
- **1891–1898** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar, Logical and Historical*, describes coordination and subordination by the form of the clause rather than by a list of words.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, treats connectives descriptively and analyses the rank of the elements they join.
- **1924** — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English*, discuss the conjunction, the semicolon, and the inversion after *nor*.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, records the disputed usages of the day, including *and/or* and the placement of *however*.
- **1957** — Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures*, shifts the analysis of coordination from lists of words to rules that combine constituents.
- **1967** — John Robert Ross, *Constraints on Variables in Syntax*, states the Coordinate Structure Constraint: no conjunct, and no element inside a conjunct, may be moved out of the coordination.
- **1985** — Randolph Quirk and others, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, describes coordination, correlatives, and conjuncts in full.
- **2002** — Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey K. Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, reclassifies *for* as a preposition and treats coordination as a general operation on like constituents.

## Questions to carry

- Coordination is said to join only like with like. What exactly is coordinated in *She tried and failed*, and what test tells you?
- Is *for* a coordinating conjunction, a subordinating conjunction, or a preposition? What evidence would settle the question?
- *I was tired, however I kept working* and *I was tired; however, I kept working* mean the same thing. Why is only one of them punctuated correctly?
- When does a comma before *and* mark a real clause boundary, and when is it only a matter of taste?
- Does subordination always make one idea less important, or can a *because* clause carry the main news?
