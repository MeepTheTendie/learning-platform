# Prepositions and their phrases

A preposition is one of the smallest and most heavily worked words in English. There are only about a hundred of them, they are a closed class that admits almost no new members, and yet they carry an outsized share of the language's meaning. They tell where, when, how, why, and in what relation one thing stands to another. They are also the part of speech that learners most often get wrong, because the choice between them is governed not by logic but by idiom, and because the same little word can be a preposition in one sentence and something else in the next. This unit sets out what prepositions are, what their phrases do, how they combine with verbs, and how to tell a preposition from a particle.

**What a preposition is.** — A preposition is a word that relates a noun or noun phrase — its object, or complement — to some other word in the sentence. The name is Latin: *prae-*, "before," and *ponere*, "to place," because a preposition is normally placed before the word it governs. Robert Lowth's 1762 definition is compact: "Prepositions, so called because they are commonly put before the words to which they are applied, serve to connect words with one another, and to shew the relation between them." The preposition and its object together make a prepositional phrase: *in the garden*, *after the storm*, *for my brother*. The object is nearly always a noun or pronoun; when it is a pronoun it takes the objective case — *with him*, *from her*, *to me* — which is why Lowth says prepositions "have a Government of Cases" and "always require the Objective Case after them." A preposition can also take a gerund (*by leaving early*), a clause (*after he left*), or another preposition's phrase as its object (*from under the table*).

**What a prepositional phrase does.** — A prepositional phrase does two main jobs. It can be adjectival, modifying a noun: *the house on the hill*, *a man of honour*, *the road to York*. Or it can be adverbial, modifying a verb, an adjective, or another adverb: *She left on Friday*; *He spoke with care*; *ready for mischief*; *studious of praise*. Lowth listed the adverbial type among his sentence patterns: "When a circumstance is added to a Verb or an Adjective by a Substantive with a Preposition before it: as, 'I write for you;' 'he reads with care.'" The same phrase can be ambiguous between the two jobs, and the ambiguity is real: in *the man with the telescope*, the phrase may tell which man (adjectival) or how he is seen (adverbial). Prepositional phrases also serve as complements required by a verb or adjective: *depend on*, *consist of*, *interested in*, *afraid of*. There the preposition is not optional decoration; it is part of the verb's or adjective's grammar.

**Simple and complex prepositions.** — The simple, or one-word, prepositions are the old core of the class: *at, by, for, from, in, into, of, off, on, onto, out, over, through, to, under, up, with*, and a few dozen more. They form a closed class — English has not coined a new simple preposition in centuries — and most of them began as words of place. Lowth observed that "most Prepositions originally denote the relation of Place, and have been thence transferred to denote by similitude other relations," which is why *in*, *out*, *under*, *by*, and *from* still feel physical even when they are abstract. Around this core stands a large and growing set of complex, or multi-word, prepositions: *in front of*, *because of*, *according to*, *on account of*, *in spite of*, *by means of*, *with regard to*, *as to*, *due to*, *apart from*. Most are historically a preposition plus a noun plus another preposition — *in front of* is literally "in the front of" — or a participle that has drifted into the class, like *according to*, *concerning*, *regarding*, *during*. Because they are phrases, complex prepositions are less fixed and less elegant than the simple ones. The Fowlers, in *The King's English*, complained that "the increasing use of these is much to be regretted," and that *as to* "flows from the pen now at every possible opportunity." The complaint is a matter of style, not grammar; *because of* and *according to* are entirely standard, while *as to* is often a wordy substitute for a plain *about* or *of*.

**Place, time, and direction.** — Prepositions fall into rough semantic families. Place: *in the room*, *on the table*, *at the door*, *under the bed*, *between the houses*, *behind the shed*, *among the crowd*. Time: *at noon*, *on Monday*, *in July*, *for two hours*, *since 1990*, *during the war*, *after lunch*, *until midnight*. Direction or motion: *to London*, *from Paris*, *toward the door*, *into the water*, *onto the roof*, *through the tunnel*, *across the field*. The families overlap, and the same preposition can serve more than one: *at* is a place in *at the corner* and a time in *at five o'clock*; *in* is a place in *in the box*, a time in *in April*, and a state in *in love*. The most useful contrast is between rest and motion, which English marks with pairs: *in* against *into*, *on* against *onto*. Goold Brown states the distinction exactly: "*Into* expresses a relation produced by motion or change; and *in*, the same relation, without reference to motion as having produced it: hence, 'to walk into the garden,' and, 'to walk in the garden,' are very different in meaning." The same contrast separates *on* from *onto*: *The cat sat on the mat* (rest), *The cat jumped onto the mat* (motion to the surface). The pairs are not always interchangeable, and the choice is one of the clearest places where a preposition carries meaning the verb does not.

**The idiom of prepositions.** — Beyond their meanings of place and time, prepositions are bound to particular words by idiom — a fact of usage, not of logic. English says *depend on*, not *depend of*; *different from*, not *different of*; *interested in*, not *interested on*; *afraid of*; *good at*; *arrive in* a city but *arrive at* a station; *married to*; *angry with* a person but *angry about* a thing. There is no rule that generates these pairings; they are conventions that a speaker learns by hearing and reading, and they must be memorized one by one. The Fowlers put the case plainly: misuse of a preposition "does not often result in what may be called in the fullest sense blunders of syntax, but mostly in offences against idiom," and it is "often impossible to convince a writer that the preposition he has used is a wrong one, because there is no reason in the nature of things, in logic, or in the principles of universal grammar ... why that preposition should not give the desired meaning as clearly as the one that we tell him he should have used." Idiom is the whole answer, and idiom is learned by exposure, not deduced. The same verb may take different prepositions with different senses: *agree with* a person, *agree to* a proposal, *agree on* a price; *consist of* a material, *consist in* a definition; *deal with* a problem, *deal in* antiques. A dictionary that lists the verb should also list its prepositions, and a learner who records *depend on* as one item is doing the right thing. This is also why the prepositions after adjectives and verbs are the last part of a foreign language to feel natural: they are arbitrary, and the ear must be trained to them.

**The rule against ending a sentence with a preposition.** — The best-known rule about prepositions is that a sentence should not end with one. It is not a rule of English grammar. It is a prescription borrowed from Latin, where prepositions are inseparable from the nouns they govern, and imported into English in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries by writers who admired Latin and thought English should behave like it. John Dryden, in 1672, objected to a final preposition in Ben Jonson and set about revising his own prose to avoid them; the eighteenth-century grammarians made the preference a rule, and it passed into schoolbooks. Lowth himself was careful, and his carefulness is worth quoting in full:

> The Preposition is often separated from the Relative which it governs, and joined to the Verb at the end of the Sentence, or of some member of it: as, "Horace is an author, whom I am much delighted with." ... This is an Idiom which our language is strongly inclined to; it prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with the familiar style in writing; but the placing of the Preposition before the Relative is more graceful, as well as more perspicuous; and agrees much better with the solemn and elevated Style.

Lowth therefore described the construction accurately and then gave a stylistic preference, not a prohibition. Later writers hardened it. Dr. Hugh Blair, in his *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres* (1783), declared that "what is called splitting of particles, or separating a preposition from the noun which it governs, is always to be avoided." Goold Brown, reviewing the tradition in 1851, noted that the censure had been copied from grammar to grammar without anyone showing how to mend the sentences it condemned. The verdict of modern linguistics is that stranding is native to English, inherited from its Germanic parent, and that the ban is a superstition. The supposed reply of Winston Churchill — that this is "the sort of arrant pedantry up with which I will not put" — is almost certainly invented, but it makes the point: the cure is often worse than the disease. Ending a sentence with a preposition is grammatical, and often the most natural choice. What is true is that a pile-up of final prepositions can be clumsy, and that in formal writing pied-piping frequently reads better.

**Stranding and pied-piping.** — Two terms name the two placements. Preposition stranding leaves the preposition behind when its object moves: *the book I was looking for*; *Who did you speak to?*; *That's the man I gave it to*. Pied-piping moves the preposition along with its object, as the Pied Piper led the children: *the book for which I was looking*; *To whom did you speak?*; *the man to whom I gave it*. Neither pattern is more grammatical than the other; the choice is governed by register, rhythm, and emphasis. Pied-piping is characteristic of formal written prose and is obligatory with *whom* in careful usage (*to whom*, not *to who*). Stranding is characteristic of speech and of informal writing, and it is often the only natural option when the object is a pronoun: *Who did you give it to?* is easy, *To whom did you give it?* is stiff, and *To who did you give it?* is wrong. Some constructions resist stranding — *the extent to which*, *the degree to which*, *since when* — and some resist pied-piping for idiomatic reasons. There is also the "prepositional passive," in which the object of a stranded preposition becomes the subject: *The bed was slept in*; *This matter must be dealt with*. The passive is impossible when the preposition is pied-piped (*In the bed was slept*), which shows how deeply stranding is built into English syntax. The practical advice is to choose by ear and by occasion, and not to treat either pattern as an error.

**Between and among.** — The traditional rule is that *between* is for two things and *among* for more than two. Goold Brown states it in the old formula: "*Between*, or *betwixt*, is used in reference to two things or parties; *among*, or *amongst*, *amid*, or *amidst*, in reference to a greater number." So *between the two houses*, *among the trees*; *divided between the two heirs*, *divided among the three heirs*. But the rule needs care. *Between* is also used when the relation is one-to-one even though more than two things are involved: a treaty *between* four nations, a conversation *between* the three of us, the difference *between* the several candidates. What matters is whether the things are being taken separately, as pairs or as distinct parties, rather than as an undifferentiated group. *Among* suggests a crowd or a mass in which the individuals are not singled out: *a house among the trees*, *a stranger among friends*. Brown also warns against using *between* with *more*, since *between* wants distinct parties: he prefers "concerning two or more authors" to "between two or more authors." The related forms *betwixt*, *amongst*, and *amidst* are archaic or literary variants of the same words. And note the pronoun: *between you and me*, never *between you and I*, because the preposition governs the objective case.

**In and into; on and onto.** — These pairs reward attention because the difference is real and the misuse is common. *In* names a position inside a place or a state; *into* names movement or change that brings something inside. Brown's garden example is the standard one: *walk in the garden* (you are already there) against *walk into the garden* (you enter it). *Into* also marks transformation: *turn water into wine*, *translate it into Latin*, *grow into a fine musician*. *In* marks the state that results: *in love*, *in debt*, *in a hurry*. The pair *on*/*onto* works the same way: *on the table* is rest on a surface, *onto the table* is motion to it. *Onto* is the newer word, not established until the eighteenth century, and it is still sometimes written as two words when *on* belongs to the verb rather than to the phrase: *hold on to your hat*, *pass it on to her*. The difference between *on* and *onto* is neutralized after some verbs (*put it on the table* and *put it onto the table* both work) but not after others: *The child climbed onto the roof* is right when the motion is the point, while *climbed on the roof* suggests he was already up there. A useful test is whether the verb already expresses motion: if it does, *in* or *on* may suffice; if the movement into or onto the place is what matters, use *into* or *onto*.

**Prepositions and particles.** — The same word can be a preposition or a particle, and the difference is grammatical, not one of spelling. A preposition takes an object; a particle does not, because it has fused with the verb to make a new meaning. Compare:

- *He climbed up the hill.* — *up* is a preposition; *the hill* is its object.
- *He looked up the word.* — *up* is a particle; it combines with *look* to mean "search for and find in a reference work."

The two *up*s look identical, but they behave differently, and four tests separate them. First, the object test: a preposition must have an object, while a particle leaves the verb with its own ordinary object — *look up the word* has *the word* as the object of the whole verb *look up*, not of *up*. Second, the movement test: a particle can move to the far side of a noun object (*look the word up*), while a preposition cannot leave its object behind in that way (*climb the hill up* is nonsense). Third, the fronting test: a preposition can move with its object to the front of a question (*Up which hill did you climb?*), but a particle cannot (*Up which word did you look?* is ungrammatical). Fourth, stress: the particle usually carries stress (*He looked it UP*), while the preposition is usually unstressed (*He climbed up the HILL*). The distinction is the historical descendant of Lowth's observation that a preposition placed after a verb "is no less apt to affect the sense of it, and to give it a new meaning." His example is exact: "*to cast* is to throw; but *to cast up*, or to compute, *an account*, is quite a different thing." He concluded that "the meaning of the Verb, and the propriety of the phrase, depend on the Preposition subjoined" — the earliest clear statement in an English grammar that the little word after the verb is part of the verb.

**Phrasal verbs and where the object goes.** — A verb plus a particle makes a phrasal verb, and phrasal verbs have their own rules about the object. When the phrasal verb is transitive and separable, a noun object may go either after the particle or between the verb and the particle: *turn on the light* or *turn the light on*; *pick up the book* or *pick the book up*. But a pronoun object must go between them: *turn it on*, *pick it up* — never *turn on it* or *pick up it*. The reason is weight: an unstressed pronoun clings to the verb, while a full noun can follow the particle. Some phrasal verbs are inseparable: the object must come after the particle, and the particle cannot move — *look after the baby*, *call on the doctor*, *get over the shock* — never *look the baby after*. Others are intransitive and take no object at all: *The plane took off*; *The meeting broke up*; *He showed up late*. A few have both an intransitive and a transitive life with different meanings: *The car broke down* (stopped working) and *He broke down the door* (demolished it); *She ran up a bill* means she accumulated one. The same verb and particle can even carry different senses: *turn on the light* (switch it on) and *The dog turned on its owner* (attacked him). Because meaning is not predictable from the parts, a learner should record each phrasal verb as a single lexical item, together with whether it is separable, inseparable, or intransitive.

**Phrasal verb and prepositional verb.** — A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle; a prepositional verb is a verb plus a preposition that keeps its object. The difference shows up in the tests above. *Look up the word* is phrasal: the particle can follow a noun object (*look the word up*), and it cannot be pied-piped (*Up which word did you look?*). *Look after the child* is prepositional: the preposition takes *the child* as its object, it cannot move past a noun (*look the child after* is wrong), and it can in principle be pied-piped, however stiffly: *After whom are you looking?* A second test is the position of an adverb. An adverb may be inserted between a verb and a following preposition (*He looked carefully after the child*), but not between a verb and its particle (*He looked carefully up the word* is wrong). A third test is the passive: both types allow it, but the prepositional passive keeps the preposition next to the verb (*The child was looked after*), while the phrasal passive can strand the particle (*The word was looked up*). There are also three-part verbs, sometimes called phrasal-prepositional, that combine both: *put up with* (*I can't put up with the noise*), *get away with*, *look forward to*, *come up with*. Here the first word is a particle and the second a preposition, and the object follows both. Linguists have refined these categories — Henry Sweet wrote of "group-verbs," Logan Pearsall Smith popularized the term "phrasal verb" in 1925, and Randolph Quirk and his colleagues set out the modern three-way division in 1985 — but the practical tests above are enough to sort any given combination.

**A practical test set.** — To decide what a word after a verb is doing, ask in order. Does it have its own object? If it does, it is a preposition, and the verb plus preposition is a prepositional verb. If it does not, it is a particle, and the verb plus particle is a phrasal verb. If it is a particle, can it move past a noun object? If yes, the phrasal verb is separable and a pronoun object must go between. If no, the phrasal verb is inseparable. If the combination takes no object at all, it is intransitive. These tests, applied honestly, resolve nearly every case, and they rest on a single principle: a preposition governs an object, while a particle belongs to the verb.

**Exercises.** — (1) Identify the prepositional phrase and say whether it is adjectival or adverbial: *The letter from my sister arrived on Tuesday.* (2) Choose the correct preposition: *She is interested ___ astronomy* (in/on/of/for). (3) Choose between *in* and *into*: *The children ran ___ the sea.* (4) Choose between *between* and *among*: *The estate was divided ___ the four cousins.* (5) Decide whether the italicized word is a preposition or a particle: *He turned off the road* and *He turned off the light.* (6) Rewrite with the pronoun in the right place: *Pick up it.* (7) Correct the sentence if it needs correcting: *This is the man whom I spoke to.* (8) Say whether *look after* is a phrasal or a prepositional verb, and give one test. Answers: (1) *from my sister* is adjectival, modifying *letter*; *on Tuesday* is adverbial, modifying *arrived*. (2) *in*. (3) *into*, because motion into the water is meant. (4) *among*, because the cousins are treated as a group of more than two; *between* would suit two, or four taken pairwise. (5) In *turned off the road*, *off* is a preposition with *the road* as its object; in *turned off the light*, *off* is a particle, since *the light* is the object of the whole verb and the particle can move (*turned the light off*). (6) *Pick it up.* (7) The sentence is acceptable; for formal prose, *the man to whom I spoke* or *the man whom I spoke to* is also fine, and *whom* is correct because it is the object of *to*. (8) *Look after* is prepositional: *the child* is the object of *after*, the preposition cannot move (*look the child after* is wrong), and an adverb may come between (*look carefully after the child*).

## Chronology

- **c. 1000** — Old English marks many relations with case endings and uses prepositions to supplement them; stranding of a preposition after its object is already possible.
- **1586** — William Bullokar publishes the first grammar of English written in English, giving prepositions a place among the parts of speech.
- **1611** — The King James Bible prints stranded prepositions, as in "Until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of" (Genesis 28:15).
- **1672** — John Dryden, in *A Defence of the Epilogue*, objects to a final preposition in Ben Jonson and revises his own prose to avoid them.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines the preposition, says it governs the objective case, notes that English is "strongly inclined" to place it last, and prefers the preposition before the relative.
- **1783** — Hugh Blair, *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres*, calls "splitting of particles ... always to be avoided."
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, repeats Lowth's treatment and spreads it through the schools.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, teaches prepositions by their use in plain sentences.
- **1851** — Goold Brown, *The Grammar of English Grammars*, distinguishes *in* from *into* and *between* from *among*, and criticizes the copied ban on separating a preposition from its noun.
- **1892–1898** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar*, treats preposition-groups and the verbal combinations that later grammarians call group-verbs.
- **1906–1924** — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English*, call the ban on the final preposition a "modern superstition" and discuss *different from* against *different to*.
- **1924** — Otto Jespersen, *The Philosophy of Grammar*, treats the placing of the preposition after its object as a native Germanic construction.
- **1925** — Logan Pearsall Smith, *Words and Idioms*, helps fix the term *phrasal verb* in English usage.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, treats preposition choice as a matter of idiom and defends *different from*.
- **1967** — John Robert Ross, in his doctoral dissertation, names the movement of a preposition with its object "pied-piping."
- **1985** — Randolph Quirk and colleagues, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, classify phrasal, prepositional, and phrasal-prepositional verbs.
- **2002** — Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey K. Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, treat particles as a distinct subclass of words and describe the gradience between preposition and particle.

## Questions to carry

- What makes a word a preposition rather than a particle, and why can the same word be either in different sentences?
- If preposition choice is idiom rather than logic, what does it mean to say a preposition is used "correctly"?
- Stranding is native and pied-piping is formal. Why did the Latin-derived rule against a final preposition take hold so firmly, and why has it weakened?
- Does the traditional *between*/*among* rule describe English as it is used, or only as it was taught?
- When a phrasal verb's meaning cannot be guessed from its parts, should it be taught as vocabulary or as grammar?
