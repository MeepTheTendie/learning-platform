# Irony and satire

**Period:** English prose and verse, seventeenth to nineteenth centuries

**The pamphlet that means the opposite.** — In 1729 an anonymous pamphlet appeared in Dublin under a long and sober title: *A Modest Proposal for preventing the children of poor people in Ireland, from being a burden on their parents or country, and for making them beneficial to the publick*. Its author was Jonathan Swift, Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, though his name was not on the title page. The pamphlet begins with a familiar sight: the streets crowded with beggars and their children. It agrees that something must be done. It then proposes, in the calm language of arithmetic and public benefit, that the year-old infants of the poor be fattened and sold as food — that a child "will make two dishes at an entertainment for friends," that the skin will make "admirable gloves for ladies, and summer boots for fine gentlemen," and that the scheme will spare the parents expense and bring profit to the nation. Not one sentence of the proposal is true in its literal sense, and not one sentence is false in its intended sense. Swift does not want children eaten. He wants his readers to feel the full horror of a society in which the poor are a burden to be managed and the landlords are the only people who matter. The whole of irony lies in that gap between what the words say and what the writer means, and in the confidence — never absolute — that the reader will cross it. How can a writer mean the opposite of the words and be sure we know it? That question governs this unit.

**The eirōn and the alazōn.** — The word "irony" comes from Greek comedy. In the plays of Aristophanes the *eirōn* was the character who understated, who pretended to be less clever, less powerful, or less knowing than he was, and the *alazōn* was the boaster who exaggerated. The eirōn won the audience by his modesty and his sideways way of speaking; he was the man who seemed to say less than he thought and in doing so said more. From the comic stage the word passed into rhetoric, where *eirōneia* named the figure in which a speaker says one thing and means another, and then into philosophy, where it named an entire attitude toward knowledge and other people. The first uses of "irony" in English belong to the sixteenth century, and the word has never settled down. It can name a single sentence, the design of a plot, a temperament, or the shape of history itself. Four varieties are usually distinguished, and the distinctions are worth keeping even where they overlap.

**Verbal irony.** — Verbal irony is the local figure: a speaker or writer says one thing and intends the opposite, and the tone, the context, or the absurdity of the literal sense carries the reversal. The friend who has just been betrayed is called "a fine friend"; the downpour is greeted with "lovely weather." The words are not false in the way a lie is false, because the speaker does not intend to deceive; the speaker intends to be understood, and assumes that the audience is in on the joke. Swift's whole pamphlet is a single sustained act of verbal irony, extended across thirty-five hundred words.

**Dramatic irony.** — Dramatic irony belongs to the structure of a work rather than to a sentence. The audience or reader knows something that a character does not, and the character's words carry a second meaning that the character cannot hear. Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus* is the classic case: Oedipus vows to hunt down the murderer of Laius and pronounce a curse upon him, and the audience knows that the murderer is Oedipus himself. Every word of his vow is true and deadly. In satire the reader occupies the seat of the audience. When the projector of *A Modest Proposal* congratulates himself on a scheme that "will prevent those voluntary abortions, and that horrid practice of women murdering their bastard children," the reader hears the compassion and also hears its opposite, and the projector does not.

**Situational irony.** — Situational irony names the reversal in events themselves: the outcome is the opposite of what was expected or intended. The fire station burns down; the man who takes a shortcut arrives last; the plot to save a life destroys it. The term is modern and easily overused — any unlucky coincidence can be dressed up as irony — but the pattern is ancient. Herodotus tells of Croesus, king of Lydia, who asked the oracle at Delphi whether he should attack Persia and was told that if he did, a great empire would fall. He attacked, and a great empire fell: his own. The oracle was not lying. It simply left the identity of the empire for the event to decide.

**Socratic irony.** — Socratic irony is the fourth variety, and the hardest to describe because it is a way of living rather than a figure of speech. In Plato's dialogues Socrates claims to know nothing, asks questions of men who are certain they know, and by patient questioning exposes the contradictions in their answers. He refuses to state his own position; he says he is only a midwife helping others to give birth to ideas. The claim of ignorance is not quite a lie, and it is not quite a joke. It is a method: by refusing to supply the answer, Socrates forces his interlocutor to examine himself. The nineteenth-century philosopher Søren Kierkegaard, in *On the Concept of Irony* (1841), read Socratic irony as an infinite negativity, a way of keeping every question open and every certainty on trial. Socratic irony is thus both the oldest form of the device and the one that most resembles a moral stance.

**Horace and Juvenal.** — Classical satire came to English writers in two temperaments, and the difference between them is the difference between a laugh and a blow. Horace, writing in Rome under Augustus, produced the *Sermones*, which he called conversations rather than poems, and the *Epistles*. His manner is urbane, tolerant, and self-mocking: he laughs at greed, at ambition, at the bore who will not stop talking, and he includes himself among the fools. His aim is correction by ridicule, and his weapon is wit. Juvenal, writing a century later, is the opposite. His *Satires* are savage, indignant, and rhetorically enormous; the world he describes is irredeemably corrupt, and the satirist can hardly keep his hand from the page. "It is hard not to write satire," he says in his first satire, and the difficulty is the point. The seventeenth-century poet and critic John Dryden, in *A Discourse Concerning the Original and Progress of Satire* (1693), gave the two modes their English names: Juvenal, he wrote, "excels in the tragical satire, as Horace does in the comical." The distinction is not only of temperament but of target and effect. Horatian satire assumes that folly can be laughed out of existence and aims to correct; Juvenalian satire assumes that vice is beyond correction and aims to punish by exposure.

**The contract of irony.** — Irony is not a private trick played on a text. It is a social act, and like every social act it depends on a shared understanding. The reader who catches an irony is being invited into a community of the knowing; the writer who produces it is trusting that the invitation will be accepted. The critic Wayne C. Booth, in *A Rhetoric of Irony* (1974), called this the reconstruction of an intended meaning beneath the surface, and argued that stable irony works only when writer and reader share the assumptions the irony depends on. His earlier book, *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961), had already given a name to the narrator whose account cannot be trusted — the unreliable narrator — and the unreliable narrator is one of irony's most useful instruments. Another theorist, D. C. Muecke, in *The Compass of Irony* (1969), described irony as a two-storey phenomenon: an innocent surface and a real meaning hidden beneath it, with the reader moving between the floors. Northrop Frye, in *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957), placed irony among the great narrative patterns, the mythos he called the mythos of winter, in which the heroic is reduced and the world is seen through a cold and knowing eye.

**When the contract fails.** — Because irony depends on shared assumptions, it can fail, and the failure can be dangerous. In 1702 the writer Daniel Defoe published a pamphlet called *The Shortest Way with the Dissenters*, in which he adopted the voice of a furious High Churchman and argued, in apparent earnest, that the surest way to deal with Protestant dissenters was to exterminate them. The irony was that Defoe was himself a dissenter and detested the policy. It did not work. High Church readers approved the pamphlet as a statement of their own views; dissenting readers were outraged that one of their own number could write such a thing. The government saw through it well enough to prosecute, and Defoe was arrested, fined, pilloried, and imprisoned. The case is the best evidence we have that irony is a contract and that a contract can be broken. Swift's proposal, twenty-seven years later, ran the same risk; some readers took it for a serious scheme, and one account claims that a bishop found it sensible.

**Swift's projector.** — The speaker of *A Modest Proposal* is not Swift but a persona: a "projector," one of those men of schemes and calculations who filled the pamphlets and the coffee-houses of the age and whom Swift had already ridiculed in the Academy of Lagado of *Gulliver's Travels* (1726). The projector is a reasonable man. He begins from the facts — the beggars, the children, the want of work — and proceeds by arithmetic. He computes the number of breeding couples, subtracts those who can support their children, subtracts the miscarriages and the infant deaths, and arrives at a hundred and twenty thousand children a year for whom no provision can be made. Then he offers his remedy. The horror of the pamphlet lies precisely in its reasonableness: the voice never rises, the numbers are exact, the compassion is professed, and the proposal is presented as the practical alternative to sentimental dreaming. The deepest irony is saved for the end. The projector lists all the remedies that a decent person might propose — tax the absentees, buy Irish goods, treat tenants with mercy — and dismisses them as visionary and idle, precisely because they would cost the comfortable something. The pamphlet's target is therefore not the poor, who are its subject, but the rich, who are its audience. Its final sentence, in which the projector assures the reader that he has no children to profit from the scheme — "the youngest being nine years old, and my wife past child-bearing" — completes the mask: a man with nothing to gain, offering the sacrifice of other people's children for the public good.

**Pope's mock-epic.** — Alexander Pope's *The Rape of the Lock* works its irony by a different method: not by pretending to approve a horror, but by treating a trivial event with the full apparatus of epic poetry. The occasion was a real quarrel. Robert, Lord Petre, had cut a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor, and the two families fell out; Pope's friend John Caryll asked him to write a poem that would laugh them back into friendship. A first version in two cantos appeared in 1712, and an expanded five-canto version, with the supernatural "machinery" of the sylphs, followed in 1714. The poem opens with the standard epic invocation — "What dire offence from am'rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things" — and the reader is told at once that the subject is slight and the treatment will not be. The cutting of a curl becomes a battle; a game of cards becomes a war with kings and knaves as its armies; the heroine's toilet becomes a religious rite, in which she adorns herself before "the sacred rites of Pride" and "awful Beauty puts on all its arms." The sylphs, the "light Militia of the lower sky," are Pope's comic invention, and they belong to the mock-heroic as the gods belong to the *Iliad*. The irony is layered: it mocks the vanity of Belinda and her world, it mocks the epic conventions that are being borrowed, and it mocks the whole human habit of inflating small things into great ones. But the Horatian manner is not merely playful. In the final canto Clarissa delivers a genuinely sensible speech about beauty and good humour, and nobody applauds; Belinda frowns and Thalestris calls her a prude. The poem allows its one piece of wisdom to be ignored, and the irony closes around the reader as well as the characters. The lock does not return; it rises into the sky and becomes a star, "And 'midst the stars inscribe Belinda's name," and the poem ends by granting the trivial thing the immortality that epic grants to heroes.

**Austen's ironic narrator.** — Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) shows irony operating not in a pamphlet's persona or a mock-epic's borrowed grandeur, but in the voice of the narrator itself. The novel opens with one of the most famous sentences in English: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." The sentence has the form of a general truth and the content of a local wish. It is not the man who is "in want of a wife"; it is the mothers of the neighbourhood who are in want of a rich husband for their daughters, and the narrator knows it. The irony is quiet and complete: the narrator adopts the idiom of the society being described and lets the reader hear the discrepancy. From the first page the narrator and the reader share a secret at the expense of the characters, and the rest of the novel is built on that shared understanding. When Darcy enters the assembly at Meryton, the narrator reports the room's judgment in the room's own idiom — "His character was decided. He was the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world, and everybody hoped that he would never come there again" — and the flat certainty of the sentences is itself a comment on the certainty of the town. This technique, in which the narrator's voice slides into the voice of a character without quotation marks, is called free indirect discourse, and Austen is one of its masters. It allows her to be ironic without ever saying "Mrs. Bennet was foolish"; the reader is trusted to supply the judgment. Mr. Bennet's dry verbal irony — his offer to introduce Mr. Bingley to Mrs. Long, his claim to respect his wife's nerves as "old friends" — is a character's wit; the narrator's irony is the larger design in which that wit is placed. Booth's "implied author," the version of the writer that a work implies, is here a thoroughly ironic intelligence, and the reader who laughs with it has been made a member of its community.

**Twain's deadpan.** — Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884) carries the ironic narrator into American fiction and gives the device its flattest, most unnerving voice. Huck tells his story in the first person, in his own ungrammatical and matter-of-fact idiom, and he never signals that anything he says is funny. The deadpan is the method: Huck reports the absurd and the cruel as ordinary events, and the reader must do the laughing and the judging. He describes the Widow Douglas's attempt to "sivilize" him and his own flight back to his old rags with the same level tone he uses for everything else, and the comedy and the criticism arrive together, without a nudge from the teller. The novel's central irony, though, is not comic but moral. Huck has been taught by his society that a slave is property and that to help a slave escape is theft, and he believes it. In the thirty-first chapter, after he has decided to write to Miss Watson and give Jim up, he finds that he cannot pray, and he understands why: "You can't pray a lie—I found that out." He writes the letter, feels "all washed clean of sin," and then remembers Jim's kindness — standing his watch, calling him honey, saying Huck was "the best friend old Jim ever had in the world, and the only one he's got now." Huck holds the letter and makes his choice: "All right, then, I'll go to hell"—and tore it up. He believes he has chosen damnation. The reader knows he has chosen decency. The gap between Huck's judgment of himself and the reader's judgment of him is the whole irony, and it is the clearest example in this unit of the unreliable narrator at work. Booth's test applies exactly: a narrator is unreliable when his account disagrees with the norms the work establishes, and Huck's account of his own wickedness disagrees with everything the novel has shown. Huck is not unreliable about facts; he is unreliable about himself. The reader must see past him to the meaning, and the meaning is the opposite of what the narrator says. The novel's ending, in which Tom Sawyer's elaborate scheme reduces Jim's escape to a game, has divided critics for decades — Leo Marx's essay "Mr. Eliot, Mr. Trilling, and Huckleberry Finn" (1953) argued that the evasion betrays the book's moral seriousness, while others have read the ending as one more irony at the expense of a society that cannot imagine freedom plainly. The dispute is itself a sign of how much work the reader of an ironic book is asked to do.

**Reading for irony.** — Irony asks the reader to hold two meanings at once and to decide, without being told, which one is meant. The signals are various: a tone too calm for its subject, as in Swift's projector; a style too grand for its occasion, as in Pope's sylphs; a narrator whose voice merges with a society's foolishness, as in Austen; a first-person teller who misjudges himself, as in Huck. None of the signals is proof, and the reader who guesses wrong becomes part of the joke. That risk is what makes irony a test of community as much as a figure of speech. It assumes a body of readers who share certain values and can be trusted to recognise when those values are being outraged or inverted. When the community is absent or divided, the irony collapses into literal statement, as it did for Defoe, and the writer may be punished for a meaning he never held. The four varieties — verbal, dramatic, situational, Socratic — are four ways of staging that gap between surface and depth, and the two classical modes, Horatian and Juvenalian, are two answers to the question of what the gap is for: to correct by laughter or to punish by exposure. Swift and Pope, Austen and Twain all mean more than they say. The reader who knows it has been let in on the secret; the reader who does not has read only the words.

## Chronology

- **c. 65–8 BCE** — Horace writes the *Sermones* and *Epistles*, the model of urbane, corrective satire.
- **c. 55–c. 127 CE** — Juvenal writes the *Satires*, the model of savage, indignant satire.
- **c. 429 BCE** — Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus*, the founding instance of dramatic irony, is performed at Athens.
- **c. 399 BCE** — Socrates is tried and executed; Plato's dialogues preserve the irony of his feigned ignorance.
- **1702** — Daniel Defoe's *The Shortest Way with the Dissenters* is read literally, and Defoe is pilloried.
- **1712** — Pope publishes the first, two-canto *Rape of the Lock*.
- **1714** — The five-canto *Rape of the Lock*, with the machinery of the sylphs, appears.
- **1726** — Swift publishes *Gulliver's Travels*, including the projectors of the Academy of Lagado.
- **1729** — Swift's *A Modest Proposal* is printed in Dublin.
- **1813** — Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* is published.
- **1841** — Kierkegaard's *On the Concept of Irony* treats Socratic irony as infinite negativity.
- **1884** — Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* is published.
- **1953** — Leo Marx's essay on the ending of *Huckleberry Finn* opens a lasting critical dispute.
- **1957** — Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* places irony among the mythoi.
- **1961** — Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* names the unreliable narrator.
- **1969** — D. C. Muecke's *The Compass of Irony* classifies the forms of irony.
- **1974** — Booth's *A Rhetoric of Irony* argues for stable irony and the ironic community.

## Questions to carry

- Swift's projector never admits he is joking. What in the pamphlet tells the reader that the speaker is a mask and not the author?
- Pope treats a card game and a haircut as if they were the matter of epic. What does the poem gain by refusing to admit that the subject is small?
- Austen's narrator never says that Mrs. Bennet is foolish. How does the reader come to know it anyway?
- Huck believes he is damning himself when he tears up the letter. Whose judgment does the reader trust instead, and why?
- Defoe was punished for an irony his readers did not catch. If irony depends on a shared understanding, how can a writer ever be sure the understanding is there?
