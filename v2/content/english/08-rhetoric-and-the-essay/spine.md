# Rhetoric and the essay

**Period:** The essay in English, sixteenth to nineteenth centuries

## The rhetorical situation

**The question.** — A report can be true and still fail. A column of figures does not move anyone to act, and a plain statement of fact does not, by itself, change a mind that is already made up. Rhetoric is the name the Greeks gave to the art that closes that gap: the study of how words are arranged to persuade. Its subject is not truth but the means by which truth is made effective for a particular audience on a particular occasion. The question that organizes this unit is what makes a piece of prose persuade rather than merely inform.

**Audience, purpose, occasion.** — Every act of persuasion happens in a situation, and the situation shapes the speech. The twentieth-century critic Lloyd Bitzer gave this a name, the rhetorical situation, and analyzed it into three parts: an audience to be moved, a purpose or exigence that calls for speech, and an occasion or set of constraints that limits what can be said and how. A funeral oration and a legal defense may use the same appeals, but the occasion demands different tones, lengths, and decorums. Bitzer's essay "The Rhetorical Situation" (1968) is a modern account of an ancient insight; Aristotle had already taught that a speaker must consider the audience's character and mood, not only the facts of the case.

**Aristotle's three appeals.** — In the Rhetoric (c. 350 BCE), Aristotle distinguished three means of persuasion available through speech itself. The first is ethos, the character of the speaker as it appears in the speech: an audience's sense of the speaker's competence, honesty, and goodwill is itself an argument. The second is pathos, the feelings of the audience, since judgments change with mood — a fearful jury and an angry one hear the same evidence differently. The third is logos, the argument in the speech: the reasons, examples, and inferences that support the claim. Aristotle's point is that these are not decorations added to a message but three separate channels through which persuasion travels, and that a speaker who neglects any one of them leaves an argument weaker than it need be.

**Three kinds of speech.** — Aristotle also sorted speeches by their audience's task. A deliberative speech addresses an assembly about the future and urges a course of action, appealing to what is expedient or harmful. A judicial, or forensic, speech addresses a court about the past and accuses or defends, appealing to what is just or unjust. An epideictic speech addresses an audience at a ceremony and praises or blames, appealing to what is honorable or shameful. The essay, when it argues, borrows from all three, but its closest ancestor is the deliberative speech: it asks a reader to think or act differently from now on.

**The three styles.** — Roman rhetoric also graded style by purpose. The plain style aims to instruct, the middle style to please, and the grand style to move; Cicero, in the Orator (46 BCE), assigned the speaker three duties — to prove, to delight, and to sway — and taught that a single speech could move among the levels as its work required. A legal argument is usually plain, a ceremonial panegyric grand, and a philosophical essay middle. The scheme survives in the instinct that a writer should not sound the same when explaining a contract, praising a friend, and rousing a crowd. Style, the third canon, is a choice among available keys and not a fixed ornament.

**The five canons.** — Roman teachers of rhetoric, whose textbooks survive in the Rhetorica ad Herennium (c. 80s BCE) and in Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria (c. 95 CE), divided the making of a speech into five stages, later called the canons. Invention is the finding of arguments and material. Arrangement is the ordering of them so that each supports the next. Style is the choice of words and figures. Memory is the holding of the speech in mind, aided by the technique of the memory palace, in which items to be recalled are placed in imagined rooms along a familiar route. Delivery is the control of voice, face, and gesture. The five canons describe not only public speech but any deliberate piece of writing; an essay invents its material, arranges it, styles it, and — when it is read aloud, as most essays once were — delivers it.

**A concrete case: the memory palace.** — The method of loci is not a metaphor but a technique. A Roman orator who had to speak without notes would walk in imagination through a building he knew, placing the first point of his speech in the entrance hall, the second on the stairs, the third in a particular room, and so on; when he came to speak he would walk the route again and find each point waiting. The technique shows how thoroughly ancient rhetoric treated the mind as a space to be furnished, and how the canons describe practical work rather than abstract categories.

## The essay begins

**Montaigne's attempt.** — The essay as a form begins with Michel de Montaigne, a Bordeaux magistrate who retired in 1571 to a round tower on his estate and, in 1580, published two books of Essais. The word is his: an essai is a trial, an attempt, a testing of a judgment rather than a finished system. Montaigne's subject is himself — his reading, his habits, his fears, his digestion, his friendships — and he follows his thought wherever it wanders, which is why the word "essay" still carries a suggestion of the tentative and the digressive. His motto, Que sais-je? ("What do I know?"), is the form's first principle: the essayist thinks in public, doubts as he goes, and revises. John Florio's English translation, published in 1603, made Montaigne a model for English writers within a generation.

**Bacon's counsels.** — Francis Bacon's Essays appeared in a different key. Bacon published ten short pieces in 1597 and enlarged the book in 1612 and again in 1625, until it held fifty-eight essays with titles like "Of Truth," "Of Great Place," and "Of Studies." Where Montaigne rambles, Bacon compresses. His essays are made of aphorisms — short, self-contained sentences that claim to state a general truth — and they are impersonal, addressed less to the writer's own experience than to a young man who must learn to conduct himself at court. "Of Studies" opens by dividing study into three uses, "for delight, for ornament, and for ability," and closes by prescribing a study as a cure for each "defect of the mind." Bacon's sentence "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man" is the whole method in twelve words.

**Two kinds of essay.** — The difference between Montaigne and Bacon became a permanent distinction in English. The personal essay, descending from Montaigne, is informal, first-personal, and digressive; its interest lies in the temper of the mind it reveals. The formal essay, descending from Bacon, is orderly, impersonal, and instructive; its interest lies in the weight and finish of its judgments. Most essays mix the two, but the mix is itself a rhetorical choice, and a reader who can name which kind is dominant can better say what the essay is trying to do.

## The periodical essay

**The Spectator.** — The essay found its largest audience in the eighteenth-century periodical. On 1 March 1711 Joseph Addison and Richard Steele began The Spectator, a single sheet published every morning in London. Its run of 555 numbers over twenty-one months was a commercial and literary event: Addison claims in No. 10 that three thousand copies were distributed daily, and that if twenty people read each, he has sixty thousand "Disciples." The paper invented a fictional club, including the country gentleman Sir Roger de Coverley, and used these characters as vehicles for commentary on manners, taste, and morals. The essay had become a serial, tied to the rhythm of the week and the life of the coffee-house.

**Sir Roger de Coverley.** — The Spectator's most durable invention was Sir Roger de Coverley, an old-fashioned country knight whose eccentricities the paper reports with affection and whose good intentions are never in doubt. He appears at church, on the road, and at the assizes, and the papers let the reader watch him rather than listen to a sermon about him. The fiction let Addison and Steele examine manners without preaching at the reader, because the reader is invited to smile at Sir Roger and, in smiling, to recognize the folly being described. The persona and the fictional club are rhetorical instruments: they give the essay a cast of characters through which a judgment can be made indirectly, and they keep the paper's morality from turning into a lecture.

**The occasion of the coffee-house.** — The Spectator's rhetoric is shaped by its occasion. It was read aloud in coffee-houses and at breakfast tables, in a London of expanding print, growing literacy, and a middle class with leisure for the day's "Speculation." Addison's declared purpose in No. 10 is to "enliven Morality with Wit, and to temper Wit with Morality," so that instruction and entertainment reinforce each other. He promises to bring philosophy "out of Closets and Libraries, Schools and Colleges, to dwell in Clubs and Assemblies, at Tea-tables, and in Coffee-houses." That sentence is itself a piece of persuasion: it flatters the reader by making philosophy's descent into common life sound like an advance rather than a dilution.

**Addison on method and the essay.** — The Spectator also gave the clearest contemporary account of the two kinds of essay. In No. 476 Addison distinguishes the papers "written with Regularity and Method" from those that "run out into the Wildness of those Compositions which go by the Names of Essays." He names Seneca and Montaigne as patterns of the second kind, Cicero and Aristotle of the first. The metaphor he chooses is spatial: the unmethodical writer is a "Wood that abounds with a great many noble Objects" in "Confusion and Disorder," while the methodical writer is a "regular Plantation" whose walks can be surveyed from its centres. Both have their pleasures, but only the second leaves an idea "not easily worn out of the Memory." Addison is arguing for arrangement, one of the five canons, and he makes the argument through an image rather than a rule.

**The cuttle-fish and Tom Puzzle.** — Addison's essay is also a demonstration of the thing it describes. To show what happens without method, he invents Tom Puzzle, a disputant who "has read enough to make him very Impertinent," whose knowledge "is sufficient to raise Doubts, but not to clear them," and who turns every conversation to half a dozen commonplaces. Against him stands Will Dry, "a Man of a clear methodical Head, but few Words," who cuts Puzzle short with a question: "what is it to our present Purpose?" The illustration — an argument by caricature — persuades the reader by making the abstract defect visible in a person. Earlier Addison had compared the muddled disputant to a cuttle-fish that "blackens all the Water about him till he becomes invisible." The images do the work that a rule could not.

## The personal essay

**Lamb and the mask of Elia.** — In the nineteenth century the personal essay returned, and the English Romantic essayists made the constructed self of the essayist into a subject. Charles Lamb published the Essays of Elia in the London Magazine from 1820 and collected them in 1823; "Dream-Children: A Reverie" appeared in 1822. Lamb writes under a persona, "Elia," and the persona licenses a tone he could not otherwise sustain: tender, whimsical, self-mocking, and finally desolate. "Dream-Children" begins as a story told to two children about their great-grandmother, moves through the remembered rooms of a great house, and ends with the revelation that the children are not real — "We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams" — and the dreamer wakes alone in his arm-chair. The essay's argument, if it has one, is about the power of the past to haunt the present, and it is carried not by a thesis but by the cadence of the sentences and the pressure of withheld grief.

**The sentence as argument.** — Consider the ending of "Dream-Children." The sentences lengthen as the dream deepens, gathering clause upon clause with commas and dashes, and then stop. The final sentence runs to nearly a hundred and fifty words before the dash and the flat, short conclusion: "but John L. (or James Elia) was gone for ever." The rhythm enacts the argument. A reader is carried forward by the accumulating syntax, as the dreamer is carried deeper into the reverie, and is then dropped by the abrupt final clause. To read the sentence aloud is to feel the persuasion working through pace and pause, not through statement.

**Emerson and the essay as oracle.** — In America the essay became a platform and a sermon. Ralph Waldo Emerson's "Self-Reliance," published in Essays: First Series in 1841, opens with the claim that "To believe your own thought, to believe that what is true for you in your private heart is true for all men,--that is genius," and proceeds in a series of imperatives and declarations: "Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string." Emerson's sentences are built for the ear. He uses antithesis, balanced clauses, and sudden short sentences after long ones. The essay does not argue toward a conclusion; it asserts, and the force of the assertion is meant to convert the reader by contagion. Where Bacon instructs and Montaigne doubts, Emerson prophesies.

**Thoreau and the essay as excursion.** — Henry David Thoreau's "Walking," published in the Atlantic Monthly in 1862, takes the essay outdoors and makes the walk itself an argument. "I wish to speak a word for Nature," it begins, "for absolute Freedom and Wildness." Thoreau's method is the digression raised to a principle: the etymology of "saunterer," the crusade, the Holy Land, the claim that "the saunterer, in the good sense, is no more vagrant than the meandering river." The essay's form imitates its subject — it wanders — and its persuasion lies in making the reader want to go outside. Its evidence is observation and etymology; its ethos is the walker's independence; its pathos is the desire for wildness.

## Argument and evidence

**The enthymeme.** — Aristotle's chief tool of logos was the enthymeme, which he called the rhetorical syllogism. A formal syllogism states both premises and the conclusion; an enthymeme leaves one premise for the audience to supply. "She is a citizen, so she may vote" works only if the audience already accepts the unstated rule that citizens may vote. The audience's participation is not a defect but the source of the argument's force: a conclusion the hearer reaches is more persuasive than one delivered ready-made. This is why rhetoric studies what an audience already believes, and why an argument aimed at the wrong audience fails however sound its logic.

**What counts as evidence.** — The essay draws on kinds of evidence that a laboratory report would not admit. Bacon cites an aphorism and a classical tag. Montaigne tells a story about himself. Addison invents a character. Emerson appeals to the intuitions of "the universal sense." Thoreau reads a dictionary. Each is an attempt to give the reader something to stand on, and each has its own risk: the anecdote may be unrepresentative, the invented character may beg the question, the appeal to intuition may only flatter what the reader already thinks. To read an essay critically is to ask what kind of evidence it offers and whether that kind can bear the weight the argument puts on it.

**The rhetorical stance.** — The modern critic Wayne C. Booth, in "The Rhetorical Stance" (1963), argued that effective persuasion depends on the writer's finding a proper balance among three things: the writer's own voice, the audience addressed, and the subject at hand. When the balance fails, one of three false stances takes over. The pedant's stance attends to the subject and forgets the audience; the advertiser's stance attends to the audience and forgets the subject; the entertainer's stance attends to the audience's pleasure and forgets both. Booth's terms are a practical test for any piece of prose: which of the three is being slighted? His account has been challenged by reader-response critics, who argue that meaning is made by interpretive communities rather than controlled by the writer's stance, but the three failures he names remain useful descriptions of essays that do not persuade.

**Ethos and the invented "I."** — Every essayist constructs a character on the page. Addison's Spectator is a fictional observer who claims to have "lived in the World without having any thing to do in it." Lamb's Elia is a bachelor with a dead brother and a lost love, standing in for a man whose real circumstances were different. Franklin's early essays were signed "Silence Dogood," a widow. The "I" of the personal essay is not a transcript of the author but a rhetorical device, and its credibility is the essay's ethos. A reader who mistakes the persona for the person has been persuaded by exactly the effect the essayist designed.

**Identification.** — The twentieth-century critic Kenneth Burke, in A Rhetoric of Motives (1950), argued that persuasion works less by proof than by identification: a speaker wins an audience by sharing its language, its interests, and its sense of what is proper, so that the audience comes to see the speaker's cause as its own. Burke called this shared substance "consubstantiality." Addison's opening in No. 10 is an example: by addressing his readers as fellow members of a great audience, he invites them to identify with the paper and its project before he has argued a single point. The "we" of an essay is always doing rhetorical work.

## How to read an essay

**Questions to ask.** — A reader can put any essay through a short interrogation. Who is the audience, and how does the essay signal that it knows them? What is the occasion, and what does it permit or forbid? What does the essay want the reader to think, feel, or do? What does it take for granted — about its readers, about the world, about what counts as proof? Which of the three appeals is doing most of the work, and which is slighted? Where does the argument rest on an unstated premise, and is that premise one the reader can accept?

**Informing and persuading.** — The distinction that runs through the unit is between information and persuasion. Information can be checked against the world; it is true or false independently of the reader's feelings. Persuasion must also move, which means it must engage ethos and pathos as well as logos, and it must be shaped to an audience and an occasion. A piece of prose can be entirely accurate and entirely ineffective. This is not a defect of accuracy but a reminder that truth has to be delivered — arranged, styled, and spoken — before it can act on anyone.

**The test of a sentence.** — One practical test of an essay is to read a single sentence aloud and ask what its rhythm is doing. Bacon's tricolon, "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man," is persuasive partly because its three parallel clauses and its falling rhythm make the three-part claim feel complete. Emerson's "Trust thyself" is an imperative in two words, and its brevity is its authority. Addison's long survey sentence in No. 476, with its balanced clauses and its "In the one... In the other," makes order itself feel pleasant. The rhythm is not ornament added after the thought; it is one of the means by which the thought is carried into a mind.

**A worked example.** — Take Addison's sentence in No. 10: "It was said of Socrates, that he brought Philosophy down from Heaven, to inhabit among Men; and I shall be ambitious to have it said of me, that I have brought Philosophy out of Closets and Libraries, Schools and Colleges, to dwell in Clubs and Assemblies, at Tea-tables, and in Coffee-houses." The structure is a parallel: Socrates did X; I shall do Y. The first half borrows Socrates' authority, an ethos by association; the second claims it for the essayist. The lists lengthen from the abstract ("Closets and Libraries") to the domestic ("Tea-tables, and Coffee-houses"), so that the sentence itself performs philosophy's descent into ordinary life. And the whole is balanced so that the reader hears the parallel before analyzing it. This is what it means for a sentence's rhythm to carry an argument.

## Chronology

- **1580** — Michel de Montaigne publishes the first two books of his Essais at Bordeaux.
- **1597** — Francis Bacon publishes the first edition of his Essays, ten short pieces; the book is expanded in 1612 and 1625.
- **1603** — John Florio's English translation of Montaigne appears in London.
- **1711–1712** — Addison and Steele publish The Spectator; No. 10 (12 March 1711) states its program, No. 476 (5 September 1712) distinguishes method from essay.
- **1714** — The Spectator is revived for a short second run.
- **1820–1823** — Lamb's Essays of Elia appear in the London Magazine and are collected; "Dream-Children: A Reverie" is published in 1822.
- **1841** — Emerson's Essays: First Series, including "Self-Reliance," is published in Boston.
- **1862** — Thoreau's "Walking" is published in the Atlantic Monthly, after his death.
- **1950** — Kenneth Burke publishes A Rhetoric of Motives.
- **1963** — Wayne C. Booth publishes "The Rhetorical Stance."

## Questions to carry

- What does an essay ask of its reader that a report does not?
- Bacon and Montaigne both write essays. Which kind persuades you more, and why?
- Addison says method helps both writer and reader. What does the personal essay lose, and gain, by refusing it?
- Where does the argument of "Dream-Children" or "Self-Reliance" rest on a premise the essay never states?
- When a sentence is arranged for the ear, is that a form of evidence or a form of force?
