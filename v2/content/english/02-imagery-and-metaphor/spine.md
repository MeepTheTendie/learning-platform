# Imagery and metaphor

**Period:** English poetry, sixteenth to nineteenth centuries

**The compass and the flea.** — In the ninth stanza of John Donne's "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning," written about 1611 and printed in 1633, a poet about to leave his wife asks how two people who love each other can survive a parting. The answer is a pair of draughtsman's compasses: "If they be two, they are two so / As stiff twin compasses are two; / Thy soul, the fix'd foot, makes no show / To move, but doth, if th' other do." The poem has already compared the couple's parting to "gold to aery thinness beat," and their love to a refined thing that "eyes, lips and hands" can miss without harm. None of these comparisons is literally true. A pair of lovers is not a pair of compasses; grief is not a tear-flood; death is not a proud tyrant. Yet each comparison says something that plain statement does not quite manage. The compass's fixed foot at the centre, and the moving foot drawing a circle that closes where it began, gives shape to an idea of constancy that would otherwise remain an abstraction. That is the subject here: the figures of speech by which poetry compares two unlike things and, in comparing them, makes a meaning that was not there before. The organizing question is how comparing two unlike things makes a new meaning — and whether the comparison is ornament laid on the surface of thought, or the machinery by which thought is done at all.

**Tenor and vehicle.** — In 1936 the English critic I. A. Richards, in *The Philosophy of Rhetoric*, gave names to the two parts of a metaphor that are still used. The **tenor** is the subject being talked about — here, the lovers' relationship and the speaker's departure. The **vehicle** is the image carried over to it — the compasses. A third element is the **ground**: the respects in which the two are claimed to be alike, in this case firmness, mutual dependence, and a movement that returns to its starting point. Richards argued that a metaphor is not a simple substitution of one word for another but a transaction between the two terms, in which the vehicle brings a whole field of associations — geometry, precision, the instrument's two legs, the small circle of a private life — that modifies how the tenor is understood. His pupil William Empson pressed the case further in *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930), showing that a single figure can carry several incompatible readings at once, and that the reader's effort to hold them together is part of the meaning. On this account a metaphor is not decoration applied to a ready-made thought; it is a way of thinking.

**Simile: the comparison made explicit.** — The simplest figure of comparison is the simile, which names the likeness with "like" or "as." Donne writes that the two souls expand "Like gold to aery thinness beat"; Marvell, in "To His Coy Mistress," invites his mistress to sport "like amorous birds of prey"; Shakespeare asks, "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" The simile is a metaphor with its likeness advertised. The classical rhetoricians thought the difference important. Aristotle, in the *Rhetoric* (fourth century BCE), treats the simile as an expanded metaphor, and in the *Poetics* calls the ability to see resemblances the mark of a poet, since "to metaphorize well" is to perceive sameness in things that are unlike. Quintilian, writing the *Institutio Oratoria* in the first century CE, lists simile among the tropes and warns that it is weaker than metaphor because it explains itself, whereas metaphor asserts an identity and leaves the reader to grasp it. The distinction is grammatical rather than conceptual: "my love is a red, red rose" and "my love is like a red, red rose" ask the mind to do almost the same work, but the first is a metaphor and the second a simile. Poetry uses both, and often moves between them within a few lines.

**Metaphor: the identity asserted.** — A metaphor does not say that two things resemble each other; it speaks of one as if it were the other. Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 opens, "That time of year thou mayst in me behold / When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang / Upon those boughs which shake against the cold, / Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang." The tenor is the speaker's age; the vehicle is late autumn. The poem never says "I am old," and never uses "like." It says "thou mayst in me behold" the season, and the reader must make the identification. The same sonnet then turns to a second vehicle — the twilight that "after sunset fadeth in the west," which "black night doth take away" — and a third, the dying fire that lies "on the ashes of his youth." Three images, drawn from a year, a day, and a hearth, all carry the same tenor. Metaphor is thus not one device among many but the characteristic instrument of lyric: it lets a poem say what a proposition cannot, because it presents the unknown through the known rather than describing it from outside.

**The Metaphysical conceit.** — The seventeenth-century poets now called the Metaphysical poets, chief among them John Donne and George Herbert, pushed metaphor to an extreme that their contemporaries found either dazzling or absurd. The **conceit** is an elaborate, extended comparison between two things that seem to have nothing to do with each other, developed with logical precision. Donne's compasses are the type of it; so is the flea in "The Flea," in which the poet argues that a flea that has bitten both lovers has joined them more truly than marriage, and so is the "bracelet of bright hair about the bone" in "The Relic." The phrase "Metaphysical poets" was coined by Samuel Johnson in his *Life of Cowley* (1779–81), and Johnson did not mean it as praise. He complained that in these poets "the most heterogeneous ideas are yoked by violence together," and named their habit *discordia concors*, a "combination of dissimilar images." Johnson thought the wit was a trick of learning rather than a movement of feeling. T. S. Eliot, in the essay "The Metaphysical Poets" (1921), reversed the verdict: he praised Donne for fusing thought and feeling in a single act of mind, and argued that the later separation of the two, which he named the dissociation of sensibility, was a decline in English poetry. Helen Gardner, in *The Metaphysical Poets* (1957), treated the conceit as a deliberate instrument of argument rather than a display of cleverness. Whether the conceit is wit or wisdom is a dispute that has never quite settled, and it is a dispute about what metaphor is for.

**Extended and controlling metaphor.** — A metaphor may be struck once and dropped, or it may be sustained through a whole poem, governing its structure and its argument. Donne's compass conceit is introduced in the seventh of nine stanzas and completes the poem; the comparison is not a flourish at the end but the poem's conclusion. Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress" builds its entire argument on a single controlling metaphor, that life is a span of time that can be spent or seized: "But at my back I always hear / Time's winged chariot hurrying near." The three movements of the poem — the impossible leisure of "Had we but world enough, and time," the desolation of the grave, and the urgent plea to "tear our pleasures with rough strife" — are stages in one extended figure, the seduction as a race against time. George Herbert's "The Pulley" makes a single metaphor do the work of a whole theology: God, at the creation, pours blessings on man but withholds "rest," so that "restlessness" will draw man back to God. When a metaphor controls a whole work in this way, critics call it a **controlling metaphor**; the test is whether the poem can be paraphrased without losing what the figure carried. For the conceit and the controlling metaphor alike, paraphrase loses most of the meaning, which is one reason poetry cannot be reduced to its argument.

**Personification and apostrophe.** — A personification is a metaphor in which the vehicle is a person, or the qualities of a person, applied to something not human. Donne's Holy Sonnet 10 addresses Death directly — "Death be not proud, though some have callèd thee / Mighty and dreadfull" — and then argues with it as a defeated opponent: "Thou art slave to Fate, Chance, kings, and desperate men." Death is given a will, a pride, and a social rank, and is then stripped of all three: "death, thou shalt die." The figure that speaks to an absent or inanimate thing is called **apostrophe**; personification supplies the human shape, apostrophe the direct address. John Ruskin, in the third volume of *Modern Painters* (1856), gave the name **pathetic fallacy** to the ascription of human feeling to nature — the "cruel" wind, the "weeping" clouds — and judged it a weakness when it produced false feeling and a strength when the emotion was real. Ruskin's test was not whether the figure was logically defensible but whether the feeling behind it was genuine. Personification is thus a place where the boundary between metaphor and belief is hardest to draw: the ancient poets who spoke of the sun as a god were not using a figure of speech, and the modern poet who speaks of the sun's "eye" is.

**Symbol.** — A symbol is a concrete image that carries a meaning larger than itself, and that meaning is not fixed by a one-to-one rule. Walt Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider" describes a spider on a promontory, launching "filament, filament, filament out of itself," and then turns the image into the soul: "And you O my soul where you stand, / Surrounded, detached, in measureless oceans of space, / Ceaselessly musing, venturing, throwing." The spider is not a code for the soul; it is an object that the poem makes the soul resemble, and the resemblance is left open. William Blake's "The Tyger" asks what "immortal hand or eye" could frame the tiger's "fearful symmetry," and the tiger becomes a symbol of creative energy that is also terrible, without ever being reduced to a single meaning. Samuel Taylor Coleridge, in *The Statesman's Manual* (1816), drew a distinction that is still used: an **allegory** is a translation of an abstract idea into a picture, with the parts corresponding one to one, while a **symbol** is a part of the thing it represents, and its meaning is inexhaustible. The distinction is contested — many works sit between the two — but it marks the difference between a puzzle with an answer and an image that keeps answering.

**Metonymy and synecdoche.** — Two figures name a thing by something associated with it. **Metonymy** substitutes an associated object for the thing meant: "the crown" for the monarchy, "the pen" for writing, "the White House" for the American presidency. **Synecdoche** substitutes a part for the whole, or a whole for a part: "all hands on deck," "a fleet of twenty sail," "wheels" for a car. The two are often grouped, and the boundary between them is not sharp; Quintilian treated both under the same heading. The figures matter because they are not neutral shorthand. To call a government "the crown" is to lodge authority in a single symbol; to call workers "hands" is to name them by their labour and to leave out the rest of them. In Shakespeare's Sonnet 116 love is "an ever-fixed mark" and "the star to every wandering bark" — a metaphor — but when the poem speaks of "rosy lips and cheeks" that come within Time's "bending sickle's compass," the body stands for the person, and the person for mortal beauty. Metonymy and synecdoche are the quietest figures, because they are the ones ordinary speech uses without noticing.

**Imagery and the five senses.** — **Imagery** is the name for language that appeals to the senses, and it is broader than the visual. Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 gives the eye the yellow leaves and the fading west, the ear the "sweet birds" that sang in the "bare ruin'd choirs," and the touch the "boughs which shake against the cold" and the fire that is "consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by." Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale," written in 1819, crowds taste and smell into a few lines — "O, for a draught of vintage! that hath been / Cool'd a long age in the deep-delvèd earth, / Tasting of Flora and the country green, / Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth" — so that the wine becomes not a drink but a bundle of remembered sensations. The word "image" can mislead, because it suggests a picture and poetry works through time and through the body as well as through the eye. The Imagist movement of the early twentieth century made the point sharply: Ezra Pound's manifesto "A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste" (1913) demanded direct treatment of the thing and forbade abstraction, and William Carlos Williams later insisted that ideas be rendered through concrete things rather than through generalities. The Imagists were reacting against what they saw as vague poetic diction, but the reaction itself shows how much weight a tradition can place on the sensory image.

**Dead metaphor.** — Most metaphors are not felt as metaphors at all. The "mouth" of a river, the "leg" of a table, the "foot" of a bed, the "branch" of a government, the "arm" of a chair: each was once a live comparison, and each has been used so often that the comparison has worn away and the word now seems literal. These are called **dead** or **fossilized** metaphors. Friedrich Nietzsche, in the essay "On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense" (1873), made the dead metaphor a weapon: he argued that truths are "illusions which we have forgotten are illusions," metaphors that have been worn smooth by use and then mistaken for the things themselves. Max Black, in the essay "Metaphor" (1955) and in *Models and Metaphors* (1962), treated the same phenomenon from the side of logic, asking how a metaphor can be understood at all when its two terms are incompatible, and arguing that the interaction of the terms produces a new meaning rather than a substitution of one for the other. George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980), went further and claimed that ordinary thought is structured by metaphor: "argument is war" generates "he attacked my position" and "she defended her claim"; "time is money" generates "spend an hour" and "waste a day." If they are right, the dead metaphor is not a figure that has lost its life but the trace of a habit of mind that is very much alive.

**How a figure shapes what can be thought.** — The claim that metaphor is not mere ornament has a long history, and it has been contested at every stage. In the Renaissance, George Puttenham's *The Arte of English Poesie* (1589) called figures the "flowers" and "colours" of speech, and treated them as a courtly art of persuasion — ornament, though ornament with power. Aristotle had already said that metaphor is the one thing that cannot be learned from another, because it depends on seeing likenesses. I. A. Richards and Max Black gave the modern version of the strong claim: that the two terms of a metaphor interact, and the meaning that results belongs to neither term alone. Donald Davidson, in "What Metaphors Mean" (1978), argued the opposite: that a metaphor means only what its words literally mean, and that its "effect" is a matter of use rather than of meaning. Paul Ricoeur, in *The Rule of Metaphor* (1975; English translation 1977), answered that metaphor is a semantic innovation, a way of redescribing reality rather than merely decorating it. The debate matters because it decides what kind of knowledge a poem can carry. If Davidson is right, a metaphor tells one nothing that could not be said plainly; if Ricoeur is right, some truths exist only in the figure that first found them. Between these positions, the practice of poetry offers the evidence: the compass, the ruined choir, the spider, and the tiger have outlived every paraphrase of them.

**Reading a figure.** — To read a figure closely, the reader can ask a small set of questions. What is the tenor — the thing being talked about? What is the vehicle — the image brought to it? What is the ground — the likeness the poem asks one to see? What does the vehicle bring with it that the poem did not ask for, and what does it leave out? In Sonnet 73, the ground of the first image is decline: the year and the life are both ending. But the vehicle is not only "autumn"; it is a particular autumn scene, and the phrase "Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang" carries a second, half-buried comparison, because "choirs" belongs to church music and to the ruined monasteries of the English Reformation. That association is not required by the argument, and it is not accidental. It is what the vehicle smuggles in. A metaphor selects some features of the vehicle and suppresses others: the compass is precise and constant, but it is also a tool of measurement and control, and a love imagined as a compass is a love imagined as geometry. Reading a figure means noticing both what it says and what it refuses to say.

**The argument about ornament.** — The poets of the period had their own theories of imagery. William Wordsworth, in the Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* (1800), argued that poetry should use "a selection of language really used by men," and that personification and poetic diction too often stood between the feeling and the reader. Samuel Taylor Coleridge, in *Biographia Literaria* (1817), distinguished **fancy** — which merely assembles images — from **imagination**, which "dissolves, diffuses, dissipates, in order to re-create," and he made the secondary imagination the power that gives form to a poem. Percy Bysshe Shelley, in *A Defence of Poetry* (written 1821, published 1840), called poetry "the language of the imagination" and said that it "makes familiar objects be as if they were not familiar," which is a description of metaphor's work. These are arguments about whether figures are the substance of poetry or a surface on it. The Metaphysical conceit, the Romantic symbol, and the Imagist image are three answers to the same question, and each answer has its own way of failing: the conceit can become a stunt, the symbol can become vague, the image can become a snapshot. The best figures do not fail in these ways, and the reason they do not is the subject of the unit.

## Chronology

- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle's *Poetics* and *Rhetoric* treat metaphor as a perception of resemblance and the mark of poetic genius.
- **c. 95 CE** — Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* codifies simile, metaphor, metonymy, and synecdoche among the tropes.
- **1589** — George Puttenham's *The Arte of English Poesie* calls figures the "flowers" and "colours" of speech.
- **1609** — Shakespeare's *Sonnets* are printed, including 18, 73, and 116.
- **1611 (printed 1633)** — Donne writes "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning"; its compass conceit becomes the model of the Metaphysical figure.
- **1633** — Donne's *Poems* and *Holy Sonnets* are published, including "Death be not proud."
- **1681** — Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress," with its controlling metaphor of time, is published after his death.
- **1779–81** — Samuel Johnson's *Life of Cowley* names the "Metaphysical" poets and condemns their "heterogeneous ideas yoked by violence together."
- **1800** — Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* argues against poetic diction and personification.
- **1816** — Coleridge's *The Statesman's Manual* distinguishes symbol from allegory.
- **1817** — Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* distinguishes fancy from imagination.
- **1819** — Keats writes "Ode to a Nightingale," dense with sensory imagery.
- **1821** — Shelley writes *A Defence of Poetry*.
- **1856** — Ruskin's *Modern Painters*, volume III, names the "pathetic fallacy."
- **1868 (separately, 1881)** — Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider" appears in *Leaves of Grass*.
- **1873** — Nietzsche's "On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense" calls truths worn-out metaphors.
- **1913** — Pound's "A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste" demands direct treatment of the thing.
- **1921** — T. S. Eliot's "The Metaphysical Poets" praises Donne's "unified sensibility."
- **1930** — Empson's *Seven Types of Ambiguity* shows a figure carrying incompatible readings at once.
- **1936** — Richards's *The Philosophy of Rhetoric* introduces tenor and vehicle.
- **1955 (collected 1962)** — Max Black's "Metaphor" argues the interaction view.
- **1975 (English 1977)** — Ricoeur's *The Rule of Metaphor* treats metaphor as semantic innovation.
- **1978** — Davidson's "What Metaphors Mean" denies metaphor any meaning beyond the literal.
- **1980** — Lakoff and Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* argues that ordinary thought is metaphorical.

## Questions to carry

- When a metaphor asserts that two things are one, what is gained over saying that they are merely alike?
- The compass conceit is admired by some as a fusion of thought and feeling and dismissed by others as a learned trick. What in the poem could support either verdict?
- If "the mouth of a river" is a dead metaphor, is it still a metaphor — and who decides when it died?
- Coleridge separated symbol from allegory. Does Whitman's spider obey the distinction, or blur it?
- Davidson says a metaphor means only what its words literally mean. Can that account for what the compass stanza does that a plain statement of constancy would not?
