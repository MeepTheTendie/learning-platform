# Punctuation and its grammar

**Period:** English grammar

## Marks that show structure

**Punctuation is grammar made visible.** — A written sentence carries no voice to lift it, pause it, or bind its parts; the marks do that work. **Punctuation** is the set of conventional signs — the comma, semicolon, colon, period, dash, parenthesis, apostrophe, quotation mark, hyphen, and ellipsis — that a writer uses to mark the boundaries of grammatical units and the relations between them. It is not decoration added after the sentence is built. It is part of how the sentence is built, and a change of mark can change the structure, and therefore the meaning.

**The classic demonstration.** — Compare:

> Woman, without her man, is nothing.
> Woman: without her, man is nothing.

The words are identical; the marks are not. In the first, *without her man* is a parenthetical phrase inside the clause *Woman is nothing*; in the second, the colon turns the sentence into a statement followed by its explanation, and *without her* becomes an introductory phrase. The punctuation assigned grammatical roles. A smaller version of the same effect:

> Let's eat, Grandma.
> Let's eat Grandma.

The comma is the difference between an address to a person and a meal.

**Two theories of the mark.** — Why are the marks where they are? One old answer is the **pause theory**: punctuation records the rests a reader takes aloud. Robert Lowth, in *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762), defined punctuation as "the art of marking in writing the several pauses, or rests, between sentences, and the parts of sentences, according to their proper quantity or proportion, as they are expressed in a just and accurate pronunciation." On this account the period is a long pause, the semicolon shorter, the comma shorter still. The other answer is the **structural theory**: punctuation marks grammatical boundaries — where a clause ends, where a phrase is set off, what modifies what. Modern grammars take the structural view, because the pause theory cannot explain the comma in *However, the plan failed* (there need be no pause). Pauses follow the sense; the marks follow the grammar, and the sense follows from the grammar.

## The comma

**The comma in a list.** — A comma separates three or more items in a series: *We bought bread, cheese, and apples.* Two items take no comma: *bread and cheese*, not *bread, and cheese*. The items may be words, phrases, or clauses: *She sang, danced, and recited a poem.*

**The comma between independent clauses.** — An **independent clause** has a subject and a finite verb and can stand alone as a sentence. When two independent clauses are joined by one of the coordinating conjunctions — *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so* — a comma goes before the conjunction: *The rain stopped, and the sun came out.* *I wanted to stay, but the last train had gone.* The comma is doing grammatical work: it marks the seam between two sentences. Without the conjunction, the comma alone is not enough, and the result is the **comma splice** (below). With a short pair of clauses, writers sometimes drop the comma — *I came and I saw* — but in careful prose the comma is usual.

**The comma after an introductory element.** — A word, phrase, or dependent clause placed before the main clause is normally followed by a comma: *However, the plan failed.* *After the storm, the roads were blocked.* *When the bell rang, the class fell silent.* The comma marks the point where the subordinate material ends and the main clause begins. When the introductory element is short and closely bound to the verb, the comma may be omitted: *In 1914 war broke out.* The choice is one of clarity and rhythm.

**The comma around a nonrestrictive element.** — This is the comma's most important grammatical use. A **restrictive** (or defining) modifier narrows the meaning of the word it modifies and is not set off by commas; a **nonrestrictive** (or non-defining) modifier adds information that could be removed without changing which thing is meant, and it is set off by commas. Compare:

> The students who passed the exam went home.
> The students, who passed the exam, went home.

The first sentence picks out only those students who passed; the *who* clause is essential to the meaning, so no comma. The second says that all the students passed and went home; the clause is extra, so commas enclose it. The same contrast governs **appositives**, nouns or phrases that rename another noun:

> My brother the doctor lives in Leeds. (I have more than one brother; the phrase identifies him.)
> My brother, a doctor, lives in Leeds. (I have one brother; the phrase merely describes him.)

Get this distinction wrong and the sentence says something other than what was meant. Fowler made the point the centre of his advice on the comma: a defining relative must not be cut off from its antecedent.

**The comma in direct address.** — A noun naming the person spoken to is set off by commas: *Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears.* *Good morning, class.* *I tell you, John, it cannot be done.* The noun is not part of the clause's subject or object; the commas mark it as a separate element.

**The comma with dates and addresses.** — In American practice a date is written *July 4, 1776*, with a comma between the day and the year, and another after the year if the sentence continues: *On July 4, 1776, the Declaration was adopted.* British practice often omits the commas: *4 July 1776*. In an address, commas separate the parts: *123 Main Street, Springfield, Illinois.* When the address sits inside a sentence, a comma follows the last element as well.

**Where the comma does not go.** — A comma must not split a subject from its verb (*The tall man, left the room* is wrong) or a verb from its object (*She bought, a new coat*). It does not separate two items joined by *and* (*bread, and cheese* is wrong when only two items are meant). It does not fall between an adjective and the noun it modifies. Between two adjectives, the test is whether *and* can be inserted and whether the order can be reversed: *a tall, dark stranger* (coordinate adjectives, comma) versus *a little old lady* (cumulative, no comma — *old little lady* is not idiomatic).

## The Oxford comma

**The serial comma.** — The comma before the final *and* or *or* in a list of three or more is called the **serial comma**, or, from the house style of Oxford University Press, the **Oxford comma**. *red, white, and blue* has it; *red, white and blue* does not. Both are correct. The question is whether the writer's style requires it.

**The case for.** — The serial comma can prevent ambiguity. A dedication reads awkwardly without it:

> I would like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand and God.

With the comma they are three separate parties. Without it, the sentence can be read as saying that the parents are Ayn Rand and God. A notorious example runs:

> We invited the strippers, JFK and Stalin.

The comma after *JFK* removes the suggestion that the strippers were the two statesmen.

**The case against.** — Newspapers and many publishers drop the serial comma as a matter of economy and house style, and the comma can itself create a new ambiguity. Punctuated the other way, the same dedication can suggest that *Ayn Rand* is an appositive renaming *my parents*:

> I would like to thank my mother, Ayn Rand, and God.

No arrangement of commas can make every list unambiguous; the writer must sometimes recast the sentence. Punctuation cannot repair a structure that is already tangled.

**What the evidence shows.** — The serial comma is a **style preference**, not a rule of English grammar. British book publishing tends to use it; American newspapers tend to omit it; American book publishing tends to use it. The only firm rule is consistency within a document. A writer may choose either convention and then hold to it.

## The comma splice

**Two clauses, one comma.** — A **comma splice** (also called a comma fault) occurs when two independent clauses are joined by a comma alone:

> It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.

The two halves could each stand as a sentence, and a comma is not strong enough to join them. Charles Dickens wrote that line in *A Tale of Two Cities*, and the effect is deliberate: the rhythm rushes forward, the clauses tumble after one another. Many fine writers have used the comma splice, especially in fiction and in older prose, and the modern prohibition is a matter of style rather than of syntax. In formal writing, though, the four repairs below are the standard ones.

**Fix one: a period.** — Separate the clauses into two sentences: *It was the best of times. It was the worst of times.* This is the simplest repair, and it works whenever the clauses are not closely linked in sense.

**Fix two: a semicolon.** — Join the clauses with a semicolon, which is strong enough for the job and signals that they belong together: *It was the best of times; it was the worst of times.*

**Fix three: a comma and a coordinating conjunction.** — Keep the comma but add *and*, *but*, *or*, *nor*, *for*, *yet*, or *so*: *It was the best of times, and it was the worst of times.* The conjunction changes the relation between the clauses, so choose the one that says what is meant.

**Fix four: subordination.** — Turn one clause into a dependent element so that only one independent clause remains: *Although it was the best of times, it was the worst of times.* *Because it was the best of times, it was also the worst.* This fix does the most grammatical work, because it names the logical relation between the two ideas.

**The run-on.** — A comma splice differs from a **fused sentence** (or run-on), in which two independent clauses run together with no punctuation at all: *It was the best of times it was the worst of times.* Both are repaired in the same four ways.

## The semicolon

**Balancing two clauses.** — The semicolon joins two independent clauses that are closely related in meaning, where a period would be too final and a comma too weak:

> To err is human; to forgive, divine.

Alexander Pope's line shows the balance the semicolon can create: two clauses, the same shape, set against each other. The semicolon says *these belong together*, while leaving each clause grammatically whole.

**The complex list.** — When the items in a list already contain commas, semicolons separate the items so that the reader can see the larger divisions:

> We visited Paris, France; Berlin, Germany; and Rome, Italy.

Without the semicolons the cities and countries would blur into one long list.

**With conjunctive adverbs.** — A semicolon precedes a conjunctive adverb such as *however*, *therefore*, *moreover*, *nevertheless*, or *consequently* when it links two clauses:

> It rained all day; nevertheless, we went out.

The semicolon does the joining; the adverb supplies the logical relation; the comma after the adverb marks the end of the transition. A comma alone before *however* is a comma splice.

## The colon

**Introducing a list.** — A colon tells the reader that what follows completes or specifies what came before. Before a list:

> The recipe calls for three things: flour, water, and salt.

The colon must follow a complete clause; it should not stand between a verb and its object in careful prose. *The ingredients are: flour, water, and salt* is widely used but often criticised, because *are* already reaches for its complement; better to write *The ingredients are flour, water, and salt*, or to add a noun: *The ingredients are these: flour, water, and salt.*

**Introducing an explanation.** — The colon can present the second half of a sentence as an explanation or restatement of the first:

> There was only one problem: the key was missing.
> He had one rule: never apologise.

**Introducing a quotation.** — A colon introduces a quotation, especially a long one or one that is formally set off:

> Hamlet asks the question that opens his soliloquy: "To be, or not to be."

When the quotation is a complete sentence, it may begin with a capital; when it is woven into the writer's sentence, it begins lower case.

**Other uses.** — The colon separates hours from minutes (*6:30*), marks ratios (*3:1*), and divides a title from its subtitle (*Grammar: A Guide*).

## The dash and the parenthesis

**The dash.** — The **dash** is a mark of separation and emphasis. It sets off an interruption, a sudden turn, or a summary:

> The cooks — all three of them — walked out.
> He had one talent — and only one.
> The plan was simple — or so it seemed.

In print the dash is usually the **em dash** (—), so called because it is the width of the letter *m*. American practice sets it closed up; British practice often uses a spaced **en dash** (–). The dash is more abrupt and more emphatic than the comma; it can do almost anything, which is why overuse weakens it.

**The parenthesis.** — **Parentheses** (or round brackets) enclose material that is grammatically independent of the sentence and could be removed: *The treaty (signed in 1919) ended the war.* The aside is quieter than one set off by dashes. **Square brackets** mark an insertion by an editor into quoted material: *He said that "they [the committee] had already decided."*

**Choosing among them.** — Commas are the quietest way to set off an aside, dashes the loudest, and parentheses mark a genuine departure. A writer who uses dashes everywhere leaves nothing with which to shout.

## The apostrophe

**Possession.** — The apostrophe marks the possessive case of nouns. A singular noun takes *'s*: *the girl's book*, *the dog's tail*, *James's hat*. A plural noun ending in *s* takes an apostrophe alone: *the girls' books*, *the dogs' tails*. A plural noun that does not end in *s* takes *'s*: *the children's books*, *the men's coats*, *the mice's cage*. Names ending in *s* may take either *James's* or *James'*; the first is now commoner in American book style, the second in some British practice. Fowler noted the older custom of writing *Achilles'* and *Jesus'* without the extra *s*, and the modern habit of adding it.

**Joint and separate possession.** — When two people share a thing, only the second noun takes the possessive: *Jane and John's house*. When each owns a separate thing, both take it: *Jane's and John's houses*.

**Contractions.** — In a **contraction** the apostrophe marks letters left out: *do not* becomes *don't*, *cannot* becomes *can't*, *I will* becomes *I'll*, *it is* becomes *it's*, *who is* becomes *who's*. The apostrophe stands where the missing letters were.

**The its/it's trap.** — The commonest apostrophe error is *it's* for the possessive *its*. The rule is simple once its reason is known: possessive **pronouns** — *his*, *hers*, *its*, *ours*, *yours*, *theirs*, *whose* — take no apostrophe, because they are already possessive. *Its* means "belonging to it": *The cat licked its paw.* *It's* is always a contraction, of *it is* or *it has*: *It's raining; it's been a long day.* The same trap produces *who's* (who is) for *whose* (possessive), *you're* (you are) for *your*, and *they're* (they are) for *their*.

**Other uses.** — An apostrophe forms the plural of a letter or numeral (*dot the i's*), but not of a date: *the 1990s*, not *the 1990's*. The **greengrocer's apostrophe** — *apple's* for the plural — misapplies it where no possession and no omission is meant.

## Quotation marks

**Double and single.** — Quotation marks (inverted commas) enclose words taken from another speaker or writer, and titles of short works. American practice uses double marks first and single for a quotation inside: *She said, "He told me, 'Wait here.'"* British practice reverses the order, using single first and double inside. Both are correct within their own convention.

**Punctuation inside or outside.** — This is the most conspicuous difference between the two conventions. American practice puts commas and periods inside the closing quotation mark, whether or not they belong to the quoted words: *He called it "a fine mess."* British practice, and the "logical" style, puts them inside only when they are part of the quotation, and outside otherwise: *He called it "a fine mess".* Semicolons and colons always go outside in both conventions. Question marks and exclamation marks go inside if they belong to the quoted material and outside if they belong to the enclosing sentence:

> He asked, "Where are you going?" (the question is his)
> Did she say, "I am leaving"? (the question is the writer's)
> Did she ask, "Am I late?" (both — one mark serves)

**Titles.** — Quotation marks enclose the titles of short works: a poem, a short story, an article, a chapter, a song, an episode. Longer works — books, plays, films, journals, albums — are set in italics (or underlined when italics are not available). So *Paradise Lost* is an epic, but "Lycidas" is a poem; *The Waste Land* is a poem long enough to be printed as a book. The mark distinguishes the container from the thing contained.

**Other uses.** — Quotation marks set off a word treated as a word (*the word "however"*) or a term used with reservation, the so-called **scare quotes** (*a "reform" that changed nothing*). A long quotation set off as a block takes no marks; the indentation does the work.

## The hyphen versus the dash

**Two marks, two jobs.** — The **hyphen** (-) joins; the **dash** (—) separates. The hyphen is the shortest of the three horizontal marks, the en dash (–) is the width of an *n* and usually marks a range, and the em dash (—) is the width of an *m* and marks a break. They look alike on a page and do opposite work.

**The hyphen joins.** — A hyphen links the parts of a compound word (*mother-in-law*), a compound number (*twenty-one*), a prefix to a word (*re-enter*, *co-operate*), or two or more words working as a single modifier before a noun: *a well-known author*, *a state-of-the-art system*, *a three-year-old child*. The hyphen says *read these as one unit*. Its grammatical importance is clearest when it is missing:

> a man-eating shark (a shark that eats men)
> a man eating shark (a man who is eating a shark)

**When not to hyphenate.** — A compound modifier is not hyphenated when it follows the noun: *The author is well known*, not *well-known*. With an adverb ending in *-ly* no hyphen is needed, because the adverb already attaches to the adjective: *a highly paid job*. Suspended hyphens let one word serve two compounds: *two- and three-year-olds*.

**The en dash.** — The en dash marks a range: *pages 10–20*, *1990–2000*, *the London–Paris train*.

## Ellipsis points

**Leaving words out.** — **Ellipsis points** (or an ellipsis) mark the omission of words from a quotation. Three points stand for the missing words. Quoted in part, Tolstoy's opening becomes "All happy families are alike . . ." The reader understands that words have been left out. If the omission falls at the end of a sentence, a fourth point — the period — is added before the three: "It was the best of times. . . ." Many publishers now set a single ellipsis character (…); the three-point form is the older convention.

**Trailing off.** — In fiction the ellipsis also marks a sentence that trails away or a speaker who does not finish: *"If only I had known . . ."* This is a different job from quotation, and it belongs to the representation of speech rather than to citation.

**What it is not.** — The ellipsis is not a general mark of suspense, and it does not replace a period at the end of an ordinary sentence. Used too freely, it makes prose look uncertain. In formal quotation, the writer must not use it to alter the sense of the original: omitted words may be dropped, but the remaining words must be quoted exactly.

## Description, prescription, and disputed points

**Pause versus structure.** — Lowth and the elocutionists taught that the marks record pauses. The modern, descriptive account is that they mark grammatical structure, and that pauses are a by-product. The two views agree often enough to seem the same, and they part company in cases such as the comma after an introductory *However* (structural, not necessarily a pause), the comma in *The students, who passed, went home* (it changes the meaning, not just the rhythm), and the semicolon between balanced clauses (a structural join, whatever the reader's breath does).

**Rules that are style preferences.** — Several familiar "rules" of punctuation are not rules of grammar at all. The **serial comma** is a house style; either convention is correct. The ban on **starting a sentence with *And*** or *But* is a schoolroom habit that ignores the King James Bible and most English prose. The ban on **ending a sentence with a preposition** is a grammar myth imported from Latin, and it concerns word order, not marks. The prohibition on the **split infinitive** (*to boldly go*) has no basis in English syntax. The rule against the **comma splice** is a genuine convention of formal prose, but it is a style rule, not a statement that the two-clause sentence is ungrammatical; Dickens and many others break it on purpose.

**Rules that are structural.** — Other points are real. A comma cannot join two independent clauses by itself in formal writing. A restrictive modifier must not be set off by commas. *It's* is not the possessive of *it*. A hyphen is not a dash. The apostrophe marks possession and omission, not plurality. These follow from the grammar, and getting them wrong changes the structure or the meaning of the sentence.

**The method.** — To punctuate a sentence, find its structure first. Locate the independent clauses and the conjunctions that join them. Find the subordinate clauses and decide whether each is restrictive or nonrestrictive. Find the appositives, the introductory phrases, and the nouns of address. Then apply the marks: comma for the list and the introductory element, comma pair for the nonrestrictive element, semicolon between balanced clauses, colon before a list or explanation, apostrophe for possession, quotation marks around borrowed words. Punctuation is not applied to a finished sentence; it is the last step in building one.

## Chronology

- **c. 250 BCE** — Aristophanes of Byzantium, librarian at Alexandria, devises a three-dot system to mark short, medium, and long pauses for reading aloud.
- **c. 1450–1500** — Printing fixes the modern set of marks. The comma, colon, and period come into general use.
- **1494** — The semicolon first appears in print, in a book issued by the Venetian printer Aldus Manutius.
- **16th–18th centuries** — The apostrophe enters English printing, at first to mark omitted letters; its possessive use and quotation marks develop over the following centuries.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* sets out punctuation as the marking of pauses, with the comma, semicolon, colon, and period in fixed proportion.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray's *English Grammar* carries Lowth's scheme into the schoolroom, where it is taught for generations.
- **1818** — William Cobbett's *A Grammar of the English Language* treats points as marks of sense and taste, and calls the apostrophe of elision a mark of "laziness and vulgarity" in prose.
- **1892** — Henry Sweet's *A New English Grammar* describes punctuation as part of the written language, to be studied alongside syntax.
- **1906** — H. W. and F. G. Fowler's *The King's English* argues that punctuation should follow grammatical structure.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* gives the classic modern account of the stops, treating the comma's work as grammatical rather than rhythmic.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum's *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* describes punctuation as a system of marks governed by grammatical and conventional rules, not by pauses.

## Questions to carry

- Why does a comma change the meaning of *The students who passed went home* and *The students, who passed, went home*?
- If the serial comma can remove one ambiguity and create another, what should guide a writer's choice?
- Dickens joins two independent clauses with a comma and makes it work. When is the comma splice a fault, and when is it a device?
- Why do possessive pronouns such as *its* and *whose* take no apostrophe, while nouns such as *girl* and *James* do?
- The pause theory and the structural theory of punctuation agree in most sentences. Where do they disagree, and which explains the marks better?
