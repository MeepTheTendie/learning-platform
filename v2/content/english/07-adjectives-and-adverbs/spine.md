# Adjectives and adverbs

**Period:** English grammar

## The adjective and its two positions

**What an adjective is.** — An **adjective** is a word that names a quality or property and attaches it to a noun. It answers the questions *what kind?*, *which one?*, *how many?*: *a red apple*, *the tall woman*, *several attempts*. Robert Lowth, in *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762), defined it as "a word joined to a Substantive to express its Quality." The traditional tests are that an adjective can stand between a determiner and a noun (*the old house*) and that it can follow a linking verb (*the house is old*).

**Attributive position.** — When the adjective sits inside the noun phrase, before the noun, it is **attributive**: *a kind neighbour*, *the empty street*, *a wooden spoon*. English adjectives do not change for gender, number, or case; one form serves every noun, as Murray illustrated with "A careless boy; careless girls." The determiner comes first: *the wise man*, never *wise the man*.

**Predicative position.** — When the adjective follows a linking verb and completes its meaning, it is **predicative**, or a subject complement: *The street is empty*, *The soup smells good*, *She became angry*. Most adjectives work in both slots: *a hot day* and *the day is hot*; *a wise man* and *the man is wise*.

**Adjectives that keep to one position.** — Some adjectives are restricted. *Main*, *chief*, *principal*, *former*, *latter*, *mere*, *utter*, *sheer*, *sole*, and *elder* are normally attributive only: *the main reason* is idiomatic, but *the reason is main* is not; *the former president*, not *the president is former*. Others are predicative only: *asleep*, *awake*, *alone*, *alive*, *afraid*, *aware*, *ashamed*, *glad*, and the *a-* group generally, plus *well* in the health sense. One says *the baby is asleep* but not *the asleep baby*; the attributive slot takes *sleeping*. A few adjectives change meaning with position: *the late king* (dead) against *the king is late* (delayed); *a poor man* (impoverished or unfortunate) against *the man is poor* (impoverished); *a certain fact* (a definite one) against *the fact is certain* (sure).

**After the noun.** — Some adjectives follow the noun. The *a-* adjectives can follow in fixed phrases (*the children asleep*, *the house ablaze*), and a set of adjectives borrowed from French follow the noun in titles and legal phrases: *court martial*, *attorney general*, *heir apparent*, *notary public*, *poet laureate*. Indefinite pronouns take a following adjective: *something nice*, *nothing new*, *anybody clever*. Adjectives with a complement follow the noun too: *a man worthy of praise*, *the only person available*, and Lowth's example *food convenient for me*.

## The order of adjectives

**The royal order.** — When several adjectives precede a noun, English arranges them in a fixed sequence, usually taught as **opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose**. The name "royal order" comes from the regal illustration *a lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knife*. Each adjective has its own zone: *a beautiful big old round brown Italian leather riding boot*. Native speakers obey the order without being taught it, and a phrase that reverses it sounds wrong: *a leather Italian brown boot* is not English, although every word in it is.

**Why the order holds.** — The sequence runs from the most subjective judgement to the most objective fact, ending with purpose, which sits closest to the noun. Opinion is the speaker's verdict and can be dropped; material and purpose are properties of the thing itself. Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech, and Svartvik (*A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, 1985) give a zone order for premodifying adjectives, and Huddleston and Pullum (*The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, 2002) describe the same regularities as tendencies of premodification. The order is a fact of usage, not a rule handed down by grammarians.

## Participial adjectives

**Two directions.** — Participles often work as adjectives, and they come in pairs. The present participle in *-ing* describes the cause of a feeling; the past participle in *-ed* (or an irregular form) describes the person or thing that experiences it. *A boring book* makes the reader *bored*; *an interesting idea* leaves the student *interested*; *a frightening storm* leaves the villagers *frightened*; *a satisfying meal* leaves the diner *satisfied*. The same contrast appears in *a tiring journey* and *a tired traveller*, *a charming host* and *a charmed guest*, *an alarming report* and *an alarmed public*.

**When a participle is really an adjective.** — Some participles have become full adjectives with their own senses: *a learned judge*, *a wicked stepmother*, *a beloved friend*, *a trying day*, *a telling remark*, *a fitting tribute*. These can be compared and intensified (*more learned*, *very trying*) and name a lasting property rather than an event. Others remain primarily verbs: *the man reading the paper* is a reduced relative clause. The test is whether the word names a quality of the noun or an action the noun is performing or undergoing.

## Comparison

**Gradable and non-gradable.** — Most adjectives are **gradable**: they name a property that can hold to a greater or lesser degree, and they can be modified by *very*, *quite*, *extremely*, or *rather* and used in comparisons: *tall*, *cold*, *interesting*, *careful*. A few are **non-gradable**, also called **absolute** or **ungradable**: *dead*, *pregnant*, *square*, *unique*, *perfect*, *infinite*, *empty*, *complete*, *first*. Non-gradable adjectives normally reject *very* and comparison, although usage is not perfectly consistent, as the section on absolute adjectives shows.

**The three degrees.** — The tradition, set out by Lowth and Murray, recognises three degrees: the **positive** (*wise*), the **comparative** (*wiser*), and the **superlative** (*wisest*). Murray notes that some writers objected that the positive is not a degree at all, since it merely states the quality; he answers that even *a tall man* implies a reference to the ordinary size of men.

**-er/-est or more/most.** — English forms the comparative and superlative in two ways. The **inflectional** way adds *-er* and *-est*: *mild, milder, mildest*. The **periphrastic** way puts *more* and *most* before the adjective: *frugal, more frugal, most frugal*. The usual guidance is that monosyllables take *-er/-est*; most two-syllable adjectives take *more/most*, but those ending in *-y* (*happy, happier*), in *-le* after a consonant (*able, abler*), in *-er* (*tender*), and those stressed on the second syllable (*polite, politer*; *discreet, discreeter*) often take *-er/-est*; and adjectives of three or more syllables take *more/most*. The line is a tendency, not a wall, and it moves. *Common*, *pleasant*, *quiet*, *stupid*, and *subtle* take either form, and *fun* is used as an adjective in speech with *funner* and *funnest*, forms that formal writing still avoids.

**Irregular comparisons.** — A handful of very common adjectives are irregular, as Lowth and Murray both list: *good, better, best*; *bad, worse, worst*; *little, less, least*; *much* and *many, more, most*; *far, farther* and *further, farthest* and *furthest*; *old, older* and *elder, oldest* and *eldest*; *late, later* and *latter, latest* and *last*. *Elder* and *eldest* are attributive and apply mainly to people (*my elder sister*), not predicative (*she is elder* is nonstandard). *Further* is the usual form for the figurative sense (*further discussion*), while *farther* usually refers to physical distance, although British usage often uses *further* for both.

**Comparative constructions.** — The comparative is followed by *than*: *She is taller than her brother*. Equality uses *as ... as*: *He is as clever as she is*; the negative is *not as ... as* or, more formally, *not so ... as*. The superlative takes *the* and is completed by *in* or *of*: *the tallest building in the city*, *the best of the three*. Two comparatives can be paired (*the more the merrier*) or repeated for a continuing change (*the days are getting longer and longer*). *Than* can be a conjunction or a preposition. As a conjunction it introduces a clause, sometimes with the verb left out: *She is taller than I* (that is, *than I am*); as a preposition it takes the objective case: *She is taller than me*. Both are standard, but the pronoun can change the meaning: *She likes him more than me* means more than she likes me, while *She likes him more than I* means more than I like him.

**Double comparatives.** — *More better* and *most best* are nonstandard, and modern handbooks treat them as errors. But the double comparative and double superlative were once ordinary, and they survive as deliberate emphasis. Shakespeare's Antony calls the wound "the most unkindest cut of all," piling *most* on *unkindest*; the line is now read as rhetorical heightening rather than as a mistake.

## Absolute adjectives

**The argument from logic.** — An **absolute** adjective names a property that cannot, in strict logic, hold in degrees. *Unique* means "the only one of its kind"; *perfect* means "complete, with nothing lacking"; *dead*, *square*, *infinite*, *empty*, and *pregnant* are similarly all-or-nothing. On that reasoning, *very unique*, *more perfect*, *almost dead*, and *rather square* are odd, and the nineteenth- and twentieth-century handbooks treated them as faults. H. W. Fowler, in *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926), argued that *unique* should not be qualified by *more*, *most*, *very*, *somewhat*, or *rather*, because it means "one of a kind."

**The argument from use.** — Usage does not obey the logic. Many absolute adjectives have a second, weaker sense that admits degrees. *Unique* is commonly used to mean "unusual," and so *very unique* appears. *Perfect* can mean "as good as possible," and so *more perfect* is defensible; the phrase *a more perfect Union* stands in the preamble to the United States Constitution of 1787. *Empty* can be compared when it means "nearly empty" (*the emptiest room in the house*), and *dead* is compared in the jocular *deader than a doornail*. The practical rule is to ask which sense is meant, and to avoid *very unique* in careful formal prose because it invites the objection, not because the syntax forbids it.

## The adverb and its classes

**What an adverb is.** — An **adverb** is a word that modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or a whole sentence. Lowth defined adverbs as words "added to Verbs and Adjectives to denote some modification or circumstance of an action or quality," and listed the circumstances they name: manner, order, time, place, quantity, quality, comparison, doubt, affirmation, negation, and interrogation. An adverb is typically invariable; it does not change for number or tense. A few have comparative forms: *soon, sooner, soonest*; *often, oftener, oftenest*; and those in *-ly* use *more* and *most* (*more wisely*).

**The chief classes.** — Grammars from Murray's onward group adverbs by the question they answer. **Manner** (*how?*): *quickly, well, badly, carefully*. **Time** (*when?*): *now, then, yesterday, soon, already*. **Place** (*where?*): *here, there, outside, upstairs, nearby*. **Frequency** (*how often?*): *always, often, sometimes, seldom, never*. **Degree** (*to what extent?*): *very, quite, rather, extremely, almost, too, enough*. **Negation**: *not, never*. **Affirmation and doubt**: *certainly, indeed, perhaps*. **Interrogative**: *how, when, where, why*. Murray's list also includes number (*once, twice*) and order (*first, secondly*).

**Conjunctive adverbs.** — A **conjunctive adverb**, also called an **adverbial conjunction**, links two clauses while also modifying one of them: *however, therefore, nevertheless, moreover, consequently, meanwhile, thus*. Murray observed that *therefore* is an adverb when it merely means "for that reason," but a conjunction when it both carries that sense and joins sentences: *He is good, therefore he is happy*. Modern grammar calls these **linking adverbials**, or **conjuncts**, and notes that they join clauses less tightly than *and*, *but*, and *or*. A **relative adverb** (*where, when, why*) introduces a clause that modifies a noun: *the house where I grew up*.

**Sentence adverbs.** — A **sentence adverb**, called a **disjunct** in Quirk's terminology, modifies the whole statement rather than the verb: *Fortunately, the train was on time*; *Frankly, I don't care*; *Clearly, he was lying*. These are sometimes called **comment adverbs**. They are movable, and their position changes the shade of the sentence: *He was clearly lying* is a comment on the evidence, while *Clearly, he was lying* presents the judgement as the speaker's framing.

**Formation and the -ly problem.** — Most manner adverbs are formed by adding *-ly* to an adjective: *quick, quickly*; *bad, badly*; *cheerful, cheerfully*. Adjectives ending in *-y* change it to *-i-* (*happy, happily*), those in *-le* drop the *e* (*gentle, gently*), and those in *-ic* add *-ally* (*automatic, automatically*). But some adjectives already end in *-ly* — *friendly, lovely, lonely, silly, likely, early* — and these cannot take a second *-ly*: one says *she greeted me in a friendly way*, not *she greeted me friendlily*. A few words have two adverb forms with different meanings: *hard* (with effort) and *hardly* (scarcely); *late* (after the expected time) and *lately* (recently); *near* (close by) and *nearly* (almost); *high* (at a height) and *highly* (to a great degree); *most* and *mostly* (mainly).

## Adjectives and adverbs after linking verbs

**The copular test.** — A **linking verb**, or **copula**, does not name an action; it joins a subject to a word that describes or identifies it. The central one is *be*; others are *seem, appear, become, remain, stay, prove, turn, grow*, and the sensory verbs *feel, look, smell, sound, taste*. After a linking verb the describing word is a predicative complement, and it must be an **adjective**, not an adverb: *She is happy*, *He seems tired*, *The soup tastes salty*, *The music sounds loud*. The same verbs can be ordinary action verbs, and then they take an adverb of manner: *She looked carefully at the map* against *She looked happy*; *He tasted the soup cautiously* against *The soup tasted good*.

**Good and well.** — *Good* is an adjective and *well* is normally the adverb: *She sings well*, not *She sings good*; *The team played well*, not *played good*. But *well* is also an adjective meaning "in good health" or "satisfactory": *I am well*, *She looks well*, *All is well*. This is why *She looks good* and *She looks well* are both correct and differ: *good* describes her appearance, *well* describes her health. *I feel good* usually means "I feel happy or healthy in general"; *I feel well* specifically denies illness. *I feel badly* is best avoided in formal writing when the meaning is "I feel bad," because *badly* is the manner adverb.

**Bad and badly.** — *He smells bad* means his odour is unpleasant; *He smells badly* means his nose works poorly. *The dog looks bad* means it appears unwell or ill-behaved; *The dog looks badly* would describe how the dog uses its eyes. *She feels bad about the mistake* is correct; *She feels badly* is the disputed form. The pattern is general: after a sensory linking verb, the adjective describes the subject, while the adverb would describe the act of sensing.

## Flat adverbs

**Adverbs without -ly.** — Some adverbs have the same form as the adjective. *Fast, hard, late, early, high, low, right, wrong, straight, long, near, far, little*, and *much* are both: *a fast car* and *the car runs fast*; *a hard task* and *she worked hard*; *an early train* and *he arrived early*. These are called **flat adverbs**, or **bare adverbs**. They are not errors; they are the older layer of English, from a time when the *-ly* ending was optional. What looks like a missing *-ly* is often a survival.

**The disputed flats.** — Other adjectives are used as flat adverbs in informal speech and in fixed phrases, and the handbooks disagree about them. *Drive slow* appears on road signs and is idiomatic; *drive slowly* is the more formal choice. *Come quick*, *speak loud*, *hold tight*, *sit up straight*, *buy direct*, and *the engine runs smooth* are established in some register. *Real* for *really* (*real good*) and *sure* for *surely* (*sure, I'll help*) are common in American speech and marked as informal. *Good* as an adverb (*he did good*) is nonstandard; the adverb is *well*. Use the *-ly* form in formal writing unless the flat form is fixed in a phrase.

## Less and fewer

**Count and mass.** — The traditional rule pairs **fewer** with count nouns, which can be counted one by one, and **less** with mass nouns, which are measured in bulk. *Fewer books*, *fewer people*, *fewer mistakes*; *less water*, *less money*, *less time*. The rule is widely taught and widely broken.

**Where less is standard.** — *Less* is standard with plural nouns that name an amount, a distance, a span of time, or a sum of money, because these are treated as single quantities: *less than ten miles*, *less than five years*, *less than twenty pounds*, *less than fifty dollars*. It is also standard in *no less than* and *less than* used of degree. In ordinary speech *less people* and *less cars* are very common, and the objection to them is a matter of formality. Fowler attacked a different fault, the phrase *fewer number* (as in *a fewer number of days*), which he called a solecism and corrected to *smaller number*. Keep *fewer* for countable things in formal prose and reserve *less* for quantity, while recognising that *less* with a plural measure phrase is not an error.

## Where the adverb goes

**The neutral order.** — Adverbs are movable, and their position is governed by their class. Manner adverbs usually follow the verb and its object: *She sang the song beautifully*. Frequency adverbs go before the main verb but after *be*: *She often visits*, *She is often late*, *He has never been there*. Degree adverbs sit immediately before the word they modify: *very tall*, *quite quickly*, *almost finished*; but *enough* follows (*warm enough*, *quickly enough*). When manner, place, and time appear together, the order is manner, place, time: *She sang beautifully at the hall last night*. Time and place can be moved to the front: *Last night she sang beautifully*. Fowler's article "Position of Adverbs" collects the common misplacements, including the split infinitive, the split compound verb (*has yet adequately to be measured*), and the separation of a verb from its object.

**Only, even, and scope.** — The position of *only* can change the meaning, because *only* applies to whatever follows it or to what is stressed. Compare *Only John ate the cake* (John alone), *John only ate the cake* (he did nothing else with it), *John ate only the cake* (nothing else was eaten), and *John ate the cake only* (in that single case). The same is true of *even* and *just*. The prescriptive advice is to place *only* immediately before the word it limits, and this avoids ambiguity in writing; in speech, stress and rhythm do much of the work. The scope of a sentence adverb changes the meaning in the same way: *Happily, she left* means "it is fortunate that she left," while *She left happily* means "she was happy as she left." *He answered the question foolishly* describes a foolish answer; *He foolishly answered the question* blames him for answering at all.

## The split infinitive

**What it is.** — The **infinitive** is the base form of the verb, usually preceded by *to*: *to go*, *to read*, *to understand*. A **split infinitive** places a word, almost always an adverb, between *to* and the verb: *to boldly go*, *to really understand*, *to carefully read the letter*, *to at least try*. The name is old, but the thing is native to English, and it has been written since the thirteenth century.

**The history of the ban.** — The objection arose in the nineteenth century, when schoolmasters, reasoning from Latin, held that because a Latin infinitive is a single word (*amare*) it cannot be split, and that English should follow. The analogy fails: English *to* is not part of the verb but a separate particle. The prohibition has never had a basis in English syntax; it is a **style preference** and a schoolroom shibboleth.

**Fowler's five classes.** — H. W. Fowler's article "Split Infinitive" in *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926) divides the English-speaking world into five classes: those who neither know nor care what a split infinitive is; those who do not know but care very much; those who know and condemn; those who know and approve; and those who know and distinguish. Fowler places himself in the fifth class. His advice is a test, not a rule: keep the adverb outside the infinitive if nothing is gained and no difficulty attends moving it, but allow the split when moving the adverb would distort the sentence or destroy the rhythm. He also warns against the **imaginary split infinitive**: in *to be really understood*, *really* modifies *understood*, not the infinitive, so the phrase is not split at all, and the fear of it produces contortions such as *really to be understood*. The real fault is sacrificing natural rhythm to a phantom rule.

**Modern practice.** — Descriptive grammars treat the split infinitive as unproblematic, and corpus studies show it is common in edited prose, especially when the adverb is *not*, *also*, *just*, *only*, or *really*. The choice among *to boldly go*, *boldly to go*, and *to go boldly* is a matter of rhythm, emphasis, and idiom; *to boldly go* is now the most natural of the three. Place the adverb where it does the least damage and ignore the superstition.

## Description, prescription, and disputed points

**Rules that are style preferences.** — Several familiar "rules" about adjectives and adverbs are not grammar but taste. The ban on the **split infinitive** has no syntactic basis. The ban on **ending a sentence with a preposition** (*the person I spoke to*) ignores the fact that English verbs and prepositions form units; Lowth himself noted that the construction "is an Idiom which our language is strongly inclined to," and that the alternative is merely more formal. The ban on beginning a sentence with *And* or *But* is a schoolroom habit; the King James Bible and most English prose writers do it. None of these is a rule the language enforces.

**Genuine faults.** — Other faults are real. *More better* and *most best* are nonstandard. *She sings good* is wrong in standard English, and *He did good* for *he did well* is nonstandard. *I feel badly* is disputed and best avoided when the meaning is "I feel bad." *Elder* and *eldest* are not used predicatively, and *friendly* and *lovely* cannot be made into adverbs with *-ly*. These are matters of the language's actual forms.

**Points where prescription and description part.** — The *less/fewer* rule is a genuine distinction with legitimate exceptions. The absolute adjectives are a logical argument, not a fact of syntax: *unique* and *perfect* are compared in real use, and the objection to *very unique* is about clarity, not grammar. *Hopefully* as a sentence adverb was attacked in the twentieth century but is now standard. *Between you and I* is a hypercorrection; the preposition takes the objective case, so *between you and me* is correct. What looks like an error is often a difference of register or dialect.

## Chronology

- **Old English (to c. 1100)** — Adjectives inflect for gender, number, and case, and adverbs are regularly formed with *-e* or *-lice*; flat adverbs are ordinary.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* defines the adjective as a word "joined to a Substantive to express its Quality," sets out the degrees of comparison, and defines the adverb.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray's *English Grammar* codifies the adjective, the degrees of comparison, and the classes of adverbs for a century of schoolroom use.
- **1818** — William Cobbett's *A Grammar of the English Language* mocks the elaborate schemes of grammar and insists that the learner attend to usage.
- **1892** — Henry Sweet's *A New English Grammar* treats adjectives and adverbs by their function in the sentence rather than by meaning alone.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen's *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles* describes the adjective and adverb boundary, the flat adverbs, and the history of comparison.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* contains the article "Split Infinitive" and the long article "Position of Adverbs."
- **1985** — Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech, and Svartvik's *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language* states the zone order for premodifying adjectives and the classes of adverbials.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum's *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* describes adjectives and adverbs by their functions and treats the split infinitive as unproblematic.
