# Agreement, reference, and usage

English grammar is a small set of rules and a very large set of habits. Agreement — the matching of a verb to its subject, and of a pronoun to the noun it stands for — is one of the few places where English still shows the machinery of an older, more heavily inflected language. Reference and modification are the other side of the same coin: a pronoun or a modifying phrase must be attached to the right word, or the sentence means something the writer did not intend. Around both clusters of rules has grown a thicket of usage disputes — *who* and *whom*, *less* and *fewer*, *lay* and *lie* — where a genuine distinction is sometimes confused with a mere preference. This unit separates the two: what the grammar requires, what good usage favours, and what is only a shibboleth.

**What agreement is.** — Agreement, which older grammars called concord, is the requirement that two words linked in a sentence carry matching grammatical features — number, person, and, in a few places, gender. Lowth defined it in 1762 as the case "when one word is required to be in like case, number, gender, or person, with another." English keeps two kinds of agreement: the verb agrees with its subject, and a pronoun agrees with its antecedent. Everything else — adjective with noun, article with noun — has been lost. Old English matched adjectives to nouns in number, case, and gender, and had a full set of verb endings; the modern language retains only a few traces. The interest of agreement in English is therefore not its richness but its exceptions: because so little is marked, the places where it is marked become visible, and the mismatches become noticeable.

**The thin system that remains.** — In the present tense, English marks the person and number of its verbs in only one place: the third person singular adds *-s* or *-es*. *I walk, you walk, he walks, we walk, they walk.* The past tense marks nothing except *was* and *were*. The verb *be* is the great exception, with *am, is, are, was, were*. This means that agreement is mostly invisible, and that a mistake shows up only when the subject is third-person singular in the present, or when the verb is *be*. That thinness is why the rules matter more than they look: *The dog barks* and *The dogs bark* are distinguished by a single consonant, and a missing *-s* is easy to overlook in a long sentence. The grammar also has a competing principle, notional agreement (also called synesis): the verb may follow the sense of the subject rather than its grammatical form. *The crowd were restless* treats the crowd as many people; *The crowd was restless* treats it as one body. Both are grammatical, and the choice is a matter of meaning.

**Finding the subject: intervening phrases.** — The verb agrees with the grammatical subject, not with a noun that happens to stand next to it. A phrase between the two does not change the number: *The box of apples is heavy* (subject *box*), *The boxes of apples are heavy* (subject *boxes*). The trap is that the ear catches the nearest noun. Phrases beginning with *of, with, together with, as well as, along with, including, in addition to* are not part of the subject, and the verb ignores them: *The teacher, as well as the students, was late.* The same test applies to *one of those who*: in *He is one of those people who believe in luck*, the relative *who* refers to *people*, so the plural *believe* is right; the *one* does not govern the relative clause. A related trap is the subject after the verb: in *There is a man at the door*, the true subject is *a man*, and *there* is an expletive with no number of its own. When the real subject is plural, formal usage wants the plural: *There are three reasons.* Informal English often says *There's three reasons*, and the contraction is so common that many writers accept it in speech; in edited prose, the plural verb is safer.

**Compound subjects and the nearest-noun rule.** — Two subjects joined by *and* normally take a plural verb: *Socrates and Plato were wise.* Murray made this Rule II: two or more singular nouns joined by a copulative conjunction "must have verbs, nouns, and pronouns, agreeing with them in the plural number." When the two subjects are a single thing, however, the verb stays singular: *Bread and butter is my breakfast*; *The founder and director was present* (one person wearing two titles). A subject joined by *or* or *nor* takes the verb according to the nearer part, the rule Murray called disjunctive: *Neither the manager nor the players were ready*; *Neither the players nor the manager was ready.* The same nearest-noun principle governs *either ... or* and *not only ... but also*. With *and* and mixed persons, the traditional order of politeness puts the first person last, and the plural pronoun takes the most inclusive person: *You and I are agreed*; *He and you and I won it* (not *He and you and me*). Murray's Note 3 states the old rule: in the plural pronoun, the second person takes precedence over the third, and the first over both.

**Indefinite pronouns.** — Indefinite pronouns refer to people or things without naming them, and they split into three groups. Always singular in formal grammar: *everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, anyone, anybody, anything, no one, nobody, nothing, each, either, neither, one.* Always plural: *both, few, many, several.* Variable, taking their number from what they refer to: *all, some, any, none, most, more.* The traditional rule is that singular indefinites take singular verbs and pronouns: *Everyone is here; Each of the boys has a book; Neither of them knows.* *Each* is singular even when it follows a plural noun: *The boys each have a book*, but *Each of the boys has a book.* *Every* before a plural noun still governs a singular verb: *Every student has a card.* *Either* and *neither* are singular when they stand alone as pronouns (*Neither is ready*), but when they are determiners they agree with the noun that follows (*Neither shoe fits*). *None* is the great variable: it can be singular (*None of the money is missing*) or plural (*None of the guests have arrived*), because it means both "not one" and "not any." Fowler's generation treated the plural as idiomatic when the sense is plural; the singular is now more common in American edited prose. *Every one* as two words means each individual; *everyone* as one word means everybody; *no one* is always two words. The pronoun in these constructions is where the real battle is fought, and the next section takes it up.

**Collective nouns.** — A collective, or noun of multitude, names a group: *team, family, committee, government, jury, audience, class, crowd, herd, staff, public.* Such a noun is grammatically singular, but it may be thought of as one body or as many members, and English lets the verb follow the thought. Murray's Rule IV put it plainly: a noun of multitude "may have a verb or pronoun agreeing with it, either of the singular or plural number; yet not without regard to the import of the word, as conveying unity or plurality of idea." So *The committee has issued its report* (acting as a unit) and *The committee have disagreed among themselves* (acting as individuals) are both defensible. British English uses the plural more freely than American English, which prefers the singular except when the members are clearly acting separately. The choice should be consistent: *The team was winning, and they knew it* mixes the two and sounds careless. Proper names of institutions usually take the singular (*The United States is*; *The New York Times has*), and so do measures and amounts.

**Amounts, distances, and quantities.** — A plural expression that names a single quantity takes a singular verb: *Ten miles is a long way to walk*; *Five dollars is too much*; *Two and two makes four* (or *make four*); *Fifty years is a long time.* The plural form is a measure, and the measure is one thing. The same logic makes *a number of* plural and *the number of* singular: *A number of students are waiting* (a number = several), but *The number of students is growing* (the number = a figure). Fractions and percentages follow the noun they modify: *Half of the cake is gone*; *Half of the students are absent.* *There* sentences with a list often take the verb from the first item, but formal writing prefers the plural when the list is plural: *There were a book, a pen, and two maps on the desk.*

**Pronoun-antecedent agreement and singular they.** — A pronoun should agree with its antecedent in number, person, and gender — Murray's Rule V: "Pronouns must always agree with their antecedents, and the nouns for which they stand, in gender and number." The difficulty is that the language has no dedicated third-person singular pronoun for a person of unknown or unspecified gender. The traditional solution was the generic *he*: *Everyone should do his best.* That solution is now widely rejected, both because it makes the male pronoun do double duty and because it clashes with the actual usage of centuries. Singular *they* is old: the *Oxford English Dictionary* traces it to the fourteenth century, the King James Bible has "if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses" (Matthew 18:35), Shakespeare wrote "There's not a man I meet but doth salute me / As if I were their well-acquainted friend" (*The Comedy of Errors*), and Jane Austen wrote "everybody has their failing, you know." What changed was the rule, not the language: in the eighteenth century Ann Fisher and John Kirkby began to prescribe *he* for both sexes, and Lowth and Murray fixed the agreement rule that made singular *they* look wrong. In 1850 Parliament declared that in legal language "words importing the masculine gender shall be deemed and taken to include females." For two centuries schoolbooks taught the generic *he*. Major style guides now accept singular *they*, dictionaries record it, and it is the ordinary pronoun for a person of unknown gender and for many people who use it of themselves. Where a sentence allows it, singular *they* is often the cleanest choice; *he or she* remains available in formal contexts but is clumsy; and a writer who wants the singular can usually rewrite: *All students should bring their own lunch.*

**Clear pronoun reference.** — Agreement is only half the job; the pronoun must also point to the right antecedent. Three failures recur. The ambiguous antecedent leaves the reader a choice: *The disciples of Christ, whom we imitate* — Murray's own example — could mean that we imitate Christ or that we imitate the disciples. *When she met her sister, she was upset* does not say who was upset. The remote antecedent has been separated from its pronoun by so much material that the reader has lost track: *The chairman resigned after the report, which the board had commissioned months earlier, was leaked to the press, and he was replaced.* The implied antecedent was never stated at all: *In the old house they had no running water* — who are *they*? *It says in the manual that ...* — what is *it*? The demonstratives *this, that, which* are especially prone to pointing at a whole clause: *She resigned without notice, which surprised no one* is clear, but *He said the plan would fail, and this annoyed her* leaves *this* hovering. The repairs are simple: repeat the noun, move the pronoun closer, or recast the sentence. Lowth and Murray both insisted that "the accuracy and clearness of the sentence depend very much upon the proper and determinate use of the relative, so that it may readily present its antecedent to the mind of the hearer or reader, without any obscurity or ambiguity." That is as good a statement of the rule as any.

**Misplaced modifiers.** — A modifier should sit next to the word it modifies. English word order carries much of the meaning, and a modifier in the wrong place changes it. *She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates* suggests the children were on paper plates; move the phrase: *She served sandwiches on paper plates to the children.* Limiting words — *only, just, even, almost, nearly, merely* — are the worst offenders because they qualify whatever follows them. *Only John ate the cake* means no one else ate; *John only ate the cake* means he did nothing else with it; *John ate only the cake* means he ate nothing else. In speech the voice supplies the emphasis; in writing the position must do the work. Adjective order is also fixed by convention: *a small round wooden table*, not *a wooden round small table.*

**Dangling modifiers.** — A dangling modifier is a phrase, usually participial, whose implied subject does not match the subject of the main clause. *Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful* makes the trees do the walking. *Having finished the assignment, the television was turned on* makes the television finish the assignment. The test is to ask who performs the action of the modifier; that word must be the subject of the main clause. Repairs: name the subject (*Walking down the street, I found the trees beautiful*), turn the phrase into a clause (*After I finished the assignment, I turned on the television*), or make the modifier absolute, with its own subject (*The assignment finished, I turned on the television*). Some participles have become free-floating connectives and are accepted: *Generally speaking, the plan is sound*; *Considering the weather, we did well*; *Judging by the results, the method works.* These are idioms, not errors, because the implied subject is the speaker or people in general. The absolute construction — *The doors being shut, Jesus stood in the midst* — is a legitimate pattern in which the participle has its own subject and is not dangling at all.

**Faulty parallelism.** — When two or more items are joined in a series or by a paired conjunction, they should have the same grammatical form. *She likes swimming, hiking, and to ride* mixes a gerund with an infinitive; *She likes swimming, hiking, and riding* is parallel. The paired conjunctions *both ... and, not only ... but also, either ... or, neither ... nor* demand matching structures: *He is not only intelligent but also kind* (two adjectives), not *He is not only intelligent but also has kindness.* Abraham Lincoln's "government of the people, by the people, for the people" is parallel; so is Julius Caesar's report *Veni, vidi, vici*, rendered *I came, I saw, I conquered.* Parallelism is not only elegant; it is the reader's guide to the structure of the sentence. A list whose members change shape forces the reader to rebuild the sentence midway.

**The usage disputes.** — Many of the disputes that surround agreement and reference are not about grammar at all but about usage: which of two forms is appropriate in which register. A genuine error breaks a rule of the language; a style preference is a choice that some writers have been taught to prefer. The distinctions below are real enough to be useful, but only some of them are errors to correct.

**Who and whom.** — *Who* is the subject form, *whom* the object, and the choice follows the pronoun's function inside its own clause: *Who called?* (subject); *Whom did you call?* (object); *the man who saw me*; *the man whom I saw*. The trap is the intervening clause: in *Whom shall I say is calling?* the pronoun is the subject of *is calling*, so *Who shall I say is calling?* is correct — and this is exactly the error Fowler attacked in the Shakespeare line *Young Ferdinand, whom they suppose is drown'd*. In informal English *who* has largely displaced *whom* except after a preposition (*to whom, with whom*), where *whom* survives. Using *whom* correctly is never wrong; using it where *who* belongs is a genuine error, and the "wrong *whom*" in indirect questions is common in journalism.

**Lay and lie.** — *Lay* is transitive and means "put down": *lay, laid, laid* — *Lay the book on the table.* *Lie* is intransitive and means "recline": *lie, lay, lain* — *Lie down; the book lay there; it has lain there all day.* The past tense of *lie* is *lay*, which is identical to the present of *lay*, and that is the source of the confusion: *Yesterday I lay on the couch* (correct, intransitive) versus *Yesterday I laid the book on the couch* (correct, transitive). *I laid on the couch* is the common error, because *laid* belongs only to the transitive verb. This is a genuine distinction, not a preference, though the two verbs have been sliding together in speech for centuries.

**Affect and effect.** — These are different words, not variants. *Affect* is usually a verb meaning "influence": *Smoke affects the lungs.* *Effect* is usually a noun meaning "result": *The effect was immediate.* As verbs, *effect* means "bring about": *effect a change*; *affect* can also mean "pretend": *She affected indifference.* The confusion is a spelling error with a meaning behind it: *The new law will effect us all* should be *affect*.

**Its and it's; who's and whose.** — Possessive pronouns take no apostrophe: *its, whose, theirs, yours, ours, hers.* *It's* is *it is* or *it has*; *who's* is *who is* or *who has.* The test is to expand: if *it is* or *who is* fits, use the apostrophe; if not, use the possessive. *The dog wagged its tail*; *It's raining*; *Whose book is this?*; *Who's coming?* These are genuine errors, though they are errors of spelling rather than of grammar, and the same trap produces *their / they're / there* and *your / you're*.

**Fewer and less; amount and number.** — *Fewer* goes with count nouns, *less* with mass nouns: *fewer apples, less milk*; *fewer people, less traffic.* Fowler, as the source shows, thought the distinction worth keeping and called *less* with plurals a mark of indifference to precision. But *less* has been used with count nouns for a thousand years — "less than ten items" is the supermarket checkout line, and "one less car" is a slogan — and many modern guides treat the count/mass distinction as a strong preference rather than an absolute rule. The pair *amount* and *number* follows the same logic: *a large amount of money* (mass), *a large number of coins* (count). *A large amount of people* is a genuine error, because people are countable; *less people* is disputed, and *fewer people* is the safe choice in edited prose. The larger point is that usage disputes of this kind are about register and clarity, and a writer who knows the distinction can choose to keep it or to break it deliberately.

**Rules that are not rules.** — Three rules taught for generations are not rules of English grammar at all. The ban on the split infinitive — *to boldly go* — has no basis in English; it was borrowed from Latin, where the infinitive is a single word and cannot be split, and good writers have split infinitives since the fourteenth century. The ban on ending a sentence with a preposition — *the house I grew up in* — contradicts the whole history of the language; Lowth himself noted that the construction "prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with the familiar style in writing," while conceding that placing the preposition before the relative is "more graceful" in the elevated style. And the ban on beginning a sentence with *and* or *but* is a schoolroom habit, not a grammatical constraint; the King James Bible and every major novelist open sentences that way. These are style preferences, and a writer may follow them for effect; they are not errors to be corrected. The genuine errors are the ones where the form or the reference actually fails: a verb that does not match its subject, a pronoun that points at the wrong noun, a modifier attached to the wrong word, a list whose parts do not match.

**Practice.** — Identify the error, if any, and correct it. (1) *The box of tools were on the bench.* (2) *Neither the coach nor the players was satisfied.* (3) *Everyone should bring their own pencil.* (4) *Walking to school, the rain soaked my coat.* (5) *She is not only a good singer but also dances well.* (6) *There's three reasons for the delay.* (7) *The committee have issued its report.* (8) *Whom did you say is calling?* Answers: (1) *was*, because the subject is *box*; (2) *were*, because the nearer subject is *players*; (3) standard; singular *they* is accepted; (4) dangling — *Walking to school, I was soaked by the rain*; (5) not parallel — *She is not only a good singer but also a good dancer*; (6) *There are*, because the subject is *reasons*; (7) mixed — choose *has issued its* or *have issued their*; (8) *Who did you say is calling?*, because the pronoun is the subject of *is calling*.

## Chronology

- **1586** — William Bullokar publishes the first grammar of English written in English.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines concord and sets out the agreement of verb and nominative, the noun of multitude, and the clear use of the relative.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, states the rules of syntax: the verb agrees with its nominative; copulative subjects take the plural; disjunctive subjects the singular; a noun of multitude may take either; pronouns agree with their antecedents in gender and number.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, teaches usage through letters and warns against needless refinements.
- **1850** — An Act of Parliament rules that the masculine gender includes females in legal language, giving the generic *he* a legal footing.
- **1891–1898** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar*, treats usage, not correctness, as the standard and describes concord as a matter of idiom.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, documents notional concord, the collective nouns, and the long history of singular *they* and of *less* with plurals.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, defends the *fewer/less* distinction and attacks the wrong *whom* in indirect questions.
- **1975** — Anne Bodine documents how the ban on singular *they* was invented.
- **1986** — Dennis Baron, *Grammar and Gender*, traces the history of the generic *he*.
- **1999** — Steven Pinker, *Words and Rules*, argues that disputed usages are part of the ordinary machinery of language.
- **2002** — Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey K. Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, treat singular *they* and *less* with count nouns as standard.
- **2017–2019** — Major style guides and dictionaries accept singular *they*.

## Questions to carry

- Why does English mark agreement so lightly, and what does that thin marking cost the reader?
- When a collective noun takes a plural verb, is the writer following sense or breaking a rule?
- Singular *they* is older than the rule against it. What does that say about where grammar rules come from?
- How much of the *who/whom*, *lay/lie*, and *fewer/less* apparatus is real grammar, and how much is a mark of register?
- A modifier in the wrong place changes meaning without breaking any rule. Is precision a matter of grammar or of rhetoric?
