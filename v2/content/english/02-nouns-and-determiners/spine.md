# Nouns, number, and determiners

A noun is the class of words that name. Verbs assert something about nouns, adjectives describe them, prepositions relate them, pronouns stand in for them. The noun also carries more internal machinery than any other class: number, countability, possession, and the little words that cluster in front of it. This unit works through that machinery — what nouns are, how they are classified, how they form plurals and mark possession, how they agree with verbs when they name groups, and how determiners and appositives attach to them.

**What a noun is.** — Traditional grammars defined the noun by meaning. Lowth: "A Substantive, or Noun, is the Name of a thing; of whatever we conceive in any way to subsist, or of which we have any notion." Murray: "the name of any thing that exists, or of which we have any notion." The definition is generous — "any notion" includes ideas, qualities, and relations — but it leaks. It would make "run" a noun, since running is a notion; it would make "beautiful" a noun, since beauty is a notion. Modern grammar therefore leans on form and function rather than meaning. A word is noun-like if it can (1) take a plural ending, (2) follow a determiner such as *the*, *a*, *this*, or *my*, (3) serve as subject, object, or complement, (4) be modified by an adjective, and (5) head a noun phrase. No single test catches every noun: mass nouns fail the plural test, proper nouns fail the determiner test, and gerunds pass some tests and not others. Grammarians since the 1980s (Paul Hopper and Sandra Thompson, "The Discourse Basis for Lexical Categories in Universal Grammar", 1984) treat noun-hood as a matter of degree, with a best example at the centre and fuzzier members at the edges. The tests are useful even when they do not give a clean yes or no.

**Common and proper.** — Lowth drew the old distinction: "Substantives are of two sorts; Proper, and Common, Names. Proper Names are the names appropriated to individuals ... Common Names stand for kinds, containing many sorts; or sorts, containing many individuals under them." A proper noun names a particular individual — *Elizabeth*, *London*, *Everest*, *Tuesday*, *the Thames*. A common noun names a kind or a class — *woman*, *city*, *mountain*, *day*, *river*. The grammatical consequence is that a proper name is already determinate, so it normally takes no article and no plural: we say "Elizabeth arrived", not "the Elizabeth arrived". But proper names are regularly "recategorized" as common nouns. Lowth lists the cases: a common name may be understood ("the Thames", that is, the river Thames); a name may be used for a type ("an Alexander", "the two Scipios"); and a family may be referred to in the plural ("the Howards"). Murray adds "the Cicero of his age". Names of rivers, seas, deserts, mountain ranges, and plural countries take the definite article as a matter of convention (*the Nile*, *the Alps*, *the Netherlands*, *the United States*). Names of languages, meals, games, and most diseases usually take no article.

**Concrete and abstract.** — A concrete noun names something perceptible — *stone*, *rain*, *hand*, *engine*. An abstract noun names a quality, state, or relation — *courage*, *justice*, *hunger*, *freedom*, *arrival*. Murray's footnote names them directly: "Abstract nouns, or the names of qualities abstracted from their substances; as, knowledge, goodness, whiteness." The distinction is semantic, not grammatical, and it does not line up neatly with countability: some abstract nouns are count (*an idea*, *a hope*, *two theories*), and some concrete nouns are mass (*mud*, *cotton*). Abstract nouns are the ones most often personified in verse; Lowth thought English gained a stylistic advantage here, because its natural gender made the personification more distinct.

**Count and mass.** — The most important grammatical division among common nouns is count versus mass (or countable versus uncountable). A count noun denotes an individual or a class of individuals and has a plural: *book/books*, *idea/ideas*, *child/children*. A mass noun denotes a substance or an undifferentiated quantity and has no plural: *water*, *rice*, *music*, *advice*, *information*, *furniture*. The two classes take different determiners and quantifiers. Count nouns take *many*, *few*, *several*, *each*, *every*, and the numerals: *many books*, *three ideas*. Mass nouns take *much*, *little*, and *a good deal of*: *much water*, *little music*, *a good deal of advice*. Some nouns belong to both classes with a change of meaning. *Chicken* is a count noun when it names the bird and a mass noun when it names the meat. *Paper* is mass when it names the material and count when it names a newspaper or a document. *Glass* is mass as a substance and count as a drinking vessel. Mass nouns can be "recategorized" as count when they denote a kind or a serving: *the wines of France*, *three coffees*, *a fine cheese*. The division is grammatical, not a fact about the world; *oats*, *scissors*, and *trousers* are plural in form, *wheat*, *news*, and *information* are singular, and languages disagree about where the line falls.

**Number: the regular plural.** — Number is the grammatical distinction between one and more than one. Lowth: "Whatever is spoken of is represented as one, or more, in Number: these two manners of representation in respect of number are called the Singular, and the Plural, Number." English marks the plural on the noun itself, and the regular mark is *-s* or *-es*. The choice is mostly a matter of spelling and pronunciation. Add *-s* after most sounds: *cat/cats*, *dog/dogs*, *tree/trees*. Add *-es* after a sibilant — a sound that hisses: *s*, *z*, *sh*, *ch*, *x*, and the sound written *-ge*: *kiss/kisses*, *box/boxes*, *church/churches*, *brush/brushes*, *judge/judges*. Change a final *-y* to *-ies* when a consonant precedes it: *lady/ladies*, *fly/flies*; keep the *-y* after a vowel: *boy/boys*, *key/keys*. Change a final *-f* or *-fe* to *-ves* in a set of old words: *leaf/leaves*, *wolf/wolves*, *knife/knives*, *wife/wives*, *shelf/shelves*, *half/halves* — but not in *roof/roofs*, *chief/chiefs*, *cliff/cliffs*, or *proof/proofs*. Add *-es* to some nouns in *-o* (*hero/heroes*, *potato/potatoes*, *echo/echoes*) and merely *-s* to others (*piano/pianos*, *photo/photos*, *radio/radios*). The ending is pronounced three ways: /s/ after a voiceless sound (*cats*), /z/ after a voiced sound (*dogs*, *trees*), and /ɪz/ after a sibilant (*kisses*, *boxes*).

**Irregular plurals.** — A small but high-frequency set of nouns does not use *-s* at all. Three patterns cover most of them. First, the vowel-change plural, inherited from an old Germanic pattern called umlaut: *man/men*, *woman/women*, *foot/feet*, *tooth/teeth*, *goose/geese*, *mouse/mice*, *louse/lice*, and the archaic *brother/brethren*. Lowth lists these alongside the *-en* plurals and notes that "this form we have retained from the Teutonic". Second, the *-en* plural, also Germanic and now reduced to a handful: *ox/oxen*, *child/children*, *brother/brothers* or *brethren*. Third, the unchanged plural, where singular and plural look identical: *sheep*, *deer*, *swine*, *fish*, *moose*, *aircraft*, *spacecraft*, and the borrowed *series*, *species*, and *means*. Murray gives the unchanged list as "deer, sheep, swine". A few nouns have two plurals with different senses: *brother/brothers* and *brethren*; *penny/pennies* and *pence*; *die/dice* and *dies*; *index/indexes* and *indices* (as Murray notes). Compounds move the plural to the head noun: *mother-in-law* becomes *mothers-in-law*, *passer-by* becomes *passers-by*, *court-martial* becomes *courts-martial*, *manservant* becomes *menservants*. Where the compound is opaque, the plural goes at the end: *handful/handfuls*.

**Latin and Greek plurals.** — English borrowed thousands of nouns from Latin and Greek, and for a long time educated writers kept the classical plural. Murray's grammar sets out a long table of these pairs, and it is still worth knowing. From Latin: *datum/data*, *erratum/errata*, *stratum/strata*, *medium/media*, *memorandum/memoranda*, *arcanum/arcana*, *radius/radii*, *stimulus/stimuli*, *fungus/fungi*, *alumnus/alumni*, *alumna/alumnae*, *appendix/appendices* or *appendixes*, *index/indices* or *indexes*, *vortex/vortices*. From Greek: *criterion/criteria*, *phenomenon/phenomena*, *automaton/automata*, *analysis/analyses*, *basis/bases*, *crisis/crises*, *thesis/theses*, *hypothesis/hypotheses*, *emphasis/emphases*, *ellipsis/ellipses*, *metamorphosis/metamorphoses*. A few came through Hebrew: *cherub/cherubim*, *seraph/seraphim*. Usage has drifted toward the English plural for centuries: *stadiums* has beaten *stadia*, *forums* has beaten *fora*, and *gymnasiums* and *syllabuses* are now common beside *gymnasia* and *syllabi*. Where both survive, they often divide the meanings — *indexes* for books, *indices* for mathematics, as Murray's note recommends. Two classical plurals have become singular in general use: *data* is now often treated as a mass noun ("the data is clear"), and *criteria* is frequently used as a singular ("a useful criteria"). Scientific and academic writing tends to keep the classical agreement ("the data are"), while everyday speech regularizes. This is a live case of the split between description and prescription: the classical form is not more logical, only older and more conservative, and no rule of English requires a borrowed noun to keep a foreign plural.

**Always plural, always singular.** — Some nouns occur only in the plural. These are the pluralia tantum, Latin for "plural only". They include tools and garments made in pairs (*scissors*, *trousers*, *pliers*, *tongs*, *glasses*, *jeans*) and a mixed group of others (*clothes*, *thanks*, *riches*, *alms*, *proceeds*, *outskirts*). They take a plural verb — "the scissors are sharp", "my trousers are wet" — and they cannot be counted directly. To count them, English uses "a pair of": *a pair of scissors*, *two pairs of trousers*. Murray lists "bellows, scissors, lungs, riches". Other nouns occur only in the singular, the singularia tantum. These include the names of subjects and sciences that end in *-s* but take a singular verb: *news*, *mathematics*, *physics*, *economics*, *politics*, *ethics*, *linguistics*, *athletics*, *billiards*. "The news is good." "Mathematics is hard." Murray observes that "the word news is now almost universally considered as belonging to the singular number". Some of these change number with sense: "the statistics were alarming" (the figures), "statistics is a branch of mathematics" (the subject); "politics is dull" (the activity), "the politics of the region are complicated" (the affairs). And a small set is genuinely ambivalent, taking either number according to sense: *means*, *series*, *species*, and the unchanged nouns listed above. "By all means" is plural in idiom; "a means to an end" is singular. Murray notes that "the noun means is used both in the singular and the plural number".

**The possessive.** — English marks possession in two ways. The first is the *-'s* genitive, sometimes called the Saxon genitive, a surviving case ending. The second is the *of*-genitive, a prepositional phrase. Lowth called the ending the Possessive Case and rejected the old story that the *-'s* is a shortened "his": "It was formerly written *Godis* grace; we now very improperly always shorten it with an Apostrophe ... not '*Thomas his* book,' as it is commonly supposed." He was right on the history; the ending descends from the Old English genitive *-es*, not from the pronoun. The rules of the written form are simple. A singular noun adds *-'s*: *the child's toy*, *the man's coat*, *James's book*. A plural noun ending in *-s* adds only the apostrophe: *the girls' room*, *the teachers' lounge*. An irregular plural not ending in *-s* adds *-'s*: *the children's room*, *the men's coats*, *the oxen's yoke*. Names ending in *-s* divide usage. One style adds *-'s* in all cases (*Charles's*, *James's*, *Jones's*), which is the older practice and the one recommended by Fowler and by the Chicago Manual; another adds only the apostrophe after names ending in *-s*, especially classical and biblical names (*Socrates' death*, *Jesus' parables*, *Moses' law*). Neither is wrong, but a writer should be consistent within a document. The possessive is not only about ownership. It marks measure and duration (*a day's work*, *a moment's notice*), description (*a women's college*, *a children's book*), origin (*Shakespeare's plays*), and subjective or objective relations (*the army's defeat*, *the city's destruction*). The *of*-genitive is usual with inanimate things (*the roof of the house*), but the *-'s* is common with time, distance, and personified nouns (*a week's delay*, *the earth's surface*). Two constructions deserve names. The group genitive puts the ending on the last word of a phrase rather than on the head noun: "the King of England's daughter", which Lowth renders "The King of Great Britain's Soldiers" and Murray "The king of Great Britain's dominions". The double genitive combines both: "a friend of my father's", which Lowth and Murray both analyse as containing two possessives, since it means "one of my father's friends". Finally, do not confuse the possessive with the plural: *its* (possessive) versus *it's* (it is), and *students* (plural) versus *student's* (possessive).

**Collective nouns and agreement.** — A collective noun is a singular noun that names a group: *team*, *family*, *committee*, *government*, *jury*, *audience*, *staff*, *flock*. The question is whether such a noun takes a singular or a plural verb. The answer is that it may take either, depending on whether the group is thought of as one unit or as a collection of individuals. Singular agreement treats the group as a single entity: "The committee has decided." Plural agreement, sometimes called notional agreement, treats the members as individuals: "The committee have decided." British English often prefers the plural; American English usually prefers the singular, except with nouns such as *police* ("the police are"). Fowler, writing in 1926, treated the choice as a matter of tact rather than rule: "The Cabinet is divided is better, because in the order of thought a whole must precede division; & The Cabinet are agreed is better, because it takes two or more to agree ... In general it may be said that while there is always a better & a worse in the matter, there is seldom a right & a wrong." What Fowler does insist on is consistency. Once a writer has chosen singular or plural, the pronouns and verbs must stay with that choice; "plunging about between it & they, have & has", he says, "can only be called insults to the reader". Two related traps involve number. "A number of" takes a plural verb ("A number of students were absent"), because it means several; "the number of" takes a singular verb ("The number of students is rising"), because it means the total. And a few collective nouns look plural but are singular in form, or the reverse: "the people are", but "the public is" or "the public are". Where usage is genuinely divided, the practical rule is to pick the agreement that matches the sense and then keep it.

**The noun phrase and its head.** — A phrase is a group of words that works as a single unit within a sentence. The noun phrase (NP) is built around a noun, called its head, with optional words before and after it. The slots, in order, are the determiner, the premodifiers, the head, and the postmodifiers. In "the three tall ships that entered the harbour", the determiner is *the*, the premodifiers are *three* and *tall*, the head is *ships*, and the postmodifier is the relative clause "that entered the harbour". The head is the element that determines the phrase's number (*ships*, not the modifiers, decides that the verb will be *were*) and that fixes what the phrase is about. Premodifiers are usually adjectives, participles, or nouns used attributively ("a stone wall"). Postmodifiers are usually prepositional phrases ("the man in the hat"), relative clauses ("the book that I bought"), or appositives ("my friend the doctor"). Noun phrases can be embedded inside one another without limit ("the cover of the book on the table in the corner"), which makes the phrase remarkably flexible. Traditional grammarians did not use the term "noun phrase"; Lowth and Murray spoke of the noun together with its articles and adjuncts. The phrase as a formal unit is the work of twentieth-century structural linguistics (Leonard Bloomfield, *Language*, 1933; C. C. Fries, *The Structure of English*, 1952). A further dispute remains: since 1987, when Steven Abney proposed the "DP hypothesis", many linguists have argued that the determiner, not the noun, is the head of the phrase, so that "the book" is a determiner phrase (DP) with *book* as its complement. Traditional grammar and many teaching grammars still call the noun the head. Both analyses describe the same words; they disagree about which one governs the phrase.

**Determiners.** — A determiner is a word that fixes the reference of a noun: it tells the listener whether the speaker means this one or that one, one or many, all or some, a known thing or an unknown one. Determiners form a closed class — a fixed, short list that English does not readily add to — and they occupy the first slot of the noun phrase. The main kinds are these. Articles: the definite *the*, the indefinite *a* or *an*, and the zero article, where no article appears at all ("Dogs bark"). Demonstratives: *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*. Possessives: *my*, *your*, *his*, *her*, *its*, *our*, *their*, and the interrogative *whose*. Quantifiers: *some*, *any*, *no*, *every*, *each*, *either*, *neither*, *much*, *many*, *more*, *most*, *little*, *few*, *fewer*, *less*, *several*, *enough*, *all*, *both*, *half*, *whole*, *other*, *another*. Numerals: cardinals (*one*, *two*, *three*) and ordinals (*first*, *second*, *third*). When several determiners appear together they keep a fixed order: a predeterminer such as *all*, *both*, or *half* comes first, then a central determiner such as *the*, *a*, *this*, or *my*, then a postdeterminer such as *many*, *few*, *three*, or *other*. "All the many books" and "both her young children" follow the pattern. Central determiners are mutually exclusive: "the my book" and "a this book" are impossible, because two words cannot both fix the same reference in the same way. Some combinations are fixed idioms: "many a", "half a", "such a". The zero article is a real choice, not an absence of grammar. "Man is mortal" is generic; "I like coffee" is a mass reading; "she went to school" names an institution rather than a building, which is why "she went to the school" means something different. A few points of usage are worth keeping. *A* and *an* are chosen by sound, not spelling: *an hour*, *an MP*, but *a university* and *a European* (those begin with a consonant sound /j/). *Some* is usual in affirmatives and *any* in negatives and questions, though *some* appears in offers ("Would you like some tea?"). *Much* and *little* go with mass nouns, *many* and *few* with count nouns. Prescriptive guides insist on *fewer* for count nouns and *less* for mass nouns, though *less* with count nouns is common in speech — a difference of register, not an error of grammar.

**Determiner or adjective?** — Traditional grammars often called *my*, *this*, *some*, and *any* "adjectives" — possessive, demonstrative, and indefinite adjectives. Modern grammar separates determiners from adjectives because the two classes behave differently. Four tests distinguish them. First, adjectives are gradable and take comparison; determiners are not: *bigger*, *more careful*, but not *thisser* or *more this*. Second, adjectives can be predicative, standing after the verb *be*; determiners cannot: "The book is heavy" is fine, but "The book is this" or "The book is my" is not. Third, adjectives can be modified by *very*; determiners cannot: *very heavy*, but not *very this*. Fourth, determiners and adjectives occupy different positions and can co-occur: the determiner comes first, the adjective next — "the big house", never "big the house". The labels matter because they change the analysis. Calling *my* a possessive adjective suggests it describes the noun like *red* does, which is false: *my* identifies a possessor, and it cannot be used predicatively or graded. The same is true of *this*, *some*, and *every*. Some words are genuinely dual. *Many* and *few* are determiners in "many people" but pronouns in "many were called". *Which* and *what* are determiners in "which book?" and pronouns in "which is it?". *That* is a determiner, a pronoun, and a conjunction. Numerals can be determiners ("three books") or nouns ("three of them"). One more pattern deserves a name: the adjective used as a noun. In "the rich", "the poor", and "the young", an adjective follows *the* and functions as a plural noun meaning "rich people" and so on. The same construction appears in titles such as *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*.

**Appositives.** — An appositive is a noun phrase placed beside another noun phrase and referring to the same person or thing, adding information about it. In "My brother, a doctor, lives in Leeds", the appositive "a doctor" is co-referential with "my brother". In "the poet Wordsworth", the appositive "Wordsworth" identifies which poet is meant. The two examples show the two kinds. A restrictive (or integrated) appositive is not set off by commas and is essential to identifying the referent: "the poet Wordsworth" distinguishes him from other poets, and "my friend John" picks out one friend among several. A non-restrictive (or supplementary) appositive is set off by commas and adds extra, non-essential information: "Wordsworth, the poet, lived in the Lake District"; "Charlotte, the wife of Mr. Collins, was a most humiliating picture" (Austen). Punctuation therefore carries meaning. "My friend John" implies more than one friend; "my friend, John" implies one, whose name is added as an aside. Appositives can be introduced by markers such as *namely*, *that is*, *i.e.*, or *or*: "the capital of France, namely Paris". They can modify not only nouns but whole clauses: "He arrived two hours late, a fact that annoyed everyone". Three constructions are easily confused with the appositive. The predicate nominative follows a linking verb and completes it: in "She is a doctor", "a doctor" is a complement, not an appositive, because it is part of the predicate and there is no comma. The vocative names the person addressed: in "John, come here", *John* is not an appositive but a direct address. And a noun in apposition to a pronoun usually agrees with it: "We teachers work hard" and "you students" are appositive constructions in which the pronoun and noun share a referent. The appositive packs identification and description into a single noun phrase, which is why it is common in titles, journalism, and literary prose.

**Try it.** — Test the machinery on these. (1) In "All the young students in the seminar submitted their three essays", name the head noun and list every determiner. (2) Choose the verb: "The jury (has/have) not reached a verdict, and they are still arguing." (3) Correct the possessive: "the childrens books". (4) Classify each noun as count or mass: *advice*, *chair*, *information*, *idea*. (5) Mark the appositive as restrictive or non-restrictive: "Marie Curie, the physicist, won two Nobel Prizes."

## Chronology

- **c. 1586** — William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar*, the first grammar of English written in English, adapts the Latin parts of speech to English.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines the substantive, distinguishes proper and common names, lays out number and the possessive case, and adds "critical notes" on usage.
- **1761** — Joseph Priestley, *The Rudiments of English Grammar*, treats usage rather than Latin analogy as the standard, and begins the descriptive tradition.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar, Adapted to the Different Classes of Learners*, becomes the most widely used school grammar in Britain and America; it fixes the noun-number-case framework and the table of classical plurals for a century of classrooms.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, writes grammar for working people and attacks the "learned" grammarians' Latinizing.
- **1891–1898** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar, Logical and Historical*, treats English on its own terms and distinguishes word classes by form and function rather than by meaning.
- **1914** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, Vol. II, groups articles, demonstratives, possessives, and quantifiers as "determinatives" and analyses number and the collective.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, codifies the usage of "collectives" and "nouns of multitude" and treats singular/plural agreement as a matter of sense and consistency.
- **1933** — Leonard Bloomfield, *Language*, makes the phrase a formal unit and separates function words from form classes, preparing the way for the noun phrase.
- **1952** — C. C. Fries, *The Structure of English*, defines word classes by position and distribution and treats determiners as a distinct structural class.
- **1985** — Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech, and Svartvik, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, gives determiners full word-class status and describes the noun phrase in detail.
- **1987** — Steven Abney proposes the DP hypothesis, arguing that the determiner is the head of the noun phrase.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, adopts the determiner-head analysis and reclassifies the traditional "possessive adjectives" as determiners.

## Questions to carry

- Why does English say "the Netherlands" and "the Alps" but "France" and "Everest"? Is the rule about grammar or about convention?
- "The data is clear" and "the data are clear" are both used. What does each choice reveal about how the speaker thinks of the noun?
- When "the committee have decided" is right and "the committee has decided" is right, is there a rule, or only a habit of mind?
- Lowth said proper names "admit not of Articles"; but we say "the Thames" and "an Alexander". What kind of statement is Lowth's — a description, a prescription, or a myth?
- If a determiner, not a noun, is the head of "the book", what would change in how the phrase is taught?
