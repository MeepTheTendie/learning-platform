# Modal verbs

**Period:** English grammar

## The shape of a modal

**A small, closed class.** — English has a handful of verbs that behave unlike every other verb: *can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *will*, *would*, *shall*, *should*, *must*, and the marginal *ought*, which keeps its *to*. Grammarians call them **modal auxiliaries**, **modal verbs**, or simply **modals**. They are a closed class: new members do not arrive, and the old ones are irregular and "defective." Their work is not to name an action but to colour a statement — to say whether something is possible, permitted, required, certain, or merely predicted.

**Five formal tests.** — A modal is best identified by form rather than meaning. First, it takes no third-person *-s*: *he can*, *she may*, *it must* — never *he cans*, *she mays*, *it musts*. Second, it has no non-finite forms: no infinitive (*to can*), no participle or gerund (*canning*). Third, it is followed by a **bare infinitive**, the verb without *to*: *I can swim*, not *I can to swim*. Fourth, it makes its negative and its question without *do*: *I cannot go*, *Can I go?*, not *I do not can go* or *Do I can go?*. Fifth, it has no imperative (*Can!*, *Must!*) and cannot stand after another auxiliary or after *to*.

**The NICE properties.** — The linguist Rodney Huddleston grouped the tests under a mnemonic: auxiliaries license **N**egation with *n't* (*can't*, *won't*), **I**nversion with the subject (*May I?*, *Should he?*), **C**ode, meaning they can stand in for a whole predicate (*"He can swim." "So can she."*), and **E**mphasis, carrying contrastive stress (*"I CAN do it"*). All of them hold for the modals, and that is what separates them from ordinary verbs such as *walk* or *want*, which need *do* for all four.

**Defective, and repaired by substitutes.** — Because a modal has no infinitive and no participle, it cannot fill every slot a verb normally fills. There is no *I will can*, no *to must*, no *having could*. English repairs the gap with **semi-modals** (also called **periphrastic modals** or **semi-auxiliaries**): *be able to* replaces *can*; *be allowed to* replaces *may*; *have to* replaces *must*; *be going to* covers one use of *will*. Thus *I will be able to come* stands in for the impossible *I will can come*. This pattern, where one word supplies another's missing forms, is called **suppletion**.

**Root and epistemic.** — Modals carry two broad kinds of meaning, and a single modal usually has both. **Root** modality concerns ability, permission, and obligation — the way the world's rules and circumstances bear on an action. Linguists divide it further into **deontic** modality (from Greek *deon*, "that which is binding": permission and obligation) and **dynamic** modality (ability and volition). **Epistemic** modality (from *episteme*, "knowledge") concerns the speaker's confidence in a proposition. Compare *You must leave now*, which imposes an obligation, with *You must be tired*, which draws a conclusion. F. R. Palmer's *Mood and Modality* (1986) and Jennifer Coates's *The Semantics of the Modal Auxiliaries* (1983) set out this division, which is the single most useful tool for sorting the meanings below.

## Can and could

**Ability.** — *Can* names present ability or capacity: *She can speak three languages.* Its past counterpart for a general ability is *could*: *He could swim before he was five.* For a single past achievement, however, *could* often will not do; English prefers *was able to* or *managed to*: *The boat was sinking, but we were able to reach the shore*, not *we could reach the shore*.

**Permission and possibility.** — *Can* asks and grants permission in ordinary speech: *Can I borrow your pen? You can leave when you have finished.* It also expresses general possibility — what is sometimes true or possible in principle: *It can get very hot here in summer.* *Could* makes a request more tentative: *Could you pass the salt?* The negative can state an impossibility of ability or circumstance, as in the opening of Emily Dickinson's poem: "Because I could not stop for Death, / He kindly stopped for me;".

**Can't and could have.** — The negative *can't* has two lives. As a root modal it denies ability or permission (*I can't lift it*); as an epistemic modal it denies possibility and expresses near-certain disbelief: *That can't be true.* The past form *could have* + participle marks a possibility that was not realized, and often a mild reproach: *You could have told me* means "you were free to, and you did not." With the negative, *couldn't have* makes a past deduction: *She couldn't have known* — it was impossible for her to know.

## May and might

**Permission.** — *May* is the formal modal of permission, and it survives in polite questions: *May I come in? May I make a suggestion?* *Might* is more hesitant still: *Might I trouble you for a moment?* The famous classroom correction — "Can I?" is wrong, "May I?" is right — is a point of etiquette and register, not of grammar; *can* has been used for permission since the Middle English period.

**Possibility.** — *May* and *might* place a proposition as possible: *It may rain tomorrow; she may already be at home.* *Might* generally signals a lower or more remote possibility, and is the usual form after a past-tense verb: *He said it might rain.* The contrast between *may* and *can* here is subtle: *It can rain in April* states a general truth about April; *It may rain tomorrow* offers a particular possibility about a particular day.

**Wish, concession, and purpose.** — *May* keeps an old optative use in fixed blessings: *May you live long! Long may she reign!* It marks concession after *whatever*, *however*, *whoever*: *Whatever you may say, the decision stands.* It also marks purpose in a subordinate clause: *He worked hard so that he might succeed.* *Might* appears in the same concessive frame with inversion: *Try as he might, he could not open it.* Shakespeare's *Macbeth* holds two modal meanings in one line: "If chance will have me king, why, chance may crown me" — *will* of disposition, *may* of possibility.

**May have and might have.** — Both form past possibilities: *She may have missed the train; he might have taken the wrong road.* *Might have* often carries the extra note of an unfulfilled possibility or a reproach: *You might have warned me!*

## Will and would

**Prediction and volition.** — *Will* is the ordinary future marker: *The sun will rise at 6.04; I will be thirty next year.* But it never loses its older meaning of **volition**, the will of the subject: *I will help you* is a promise; *I won't go* is a refusal. It can carry a decision made at the moment of speaking (*I'll get it*), a command (*You will report at dawn*), or a habit and characteristic disposition: *Boys will be boys; oil will float on water.*

**Would.** — *Would* is the past of *will* and the modal of the hypothetical. It reports a future from a past point (*He said he would come*), expresses a past habit (*When we were children, we would play in the lane*), and builds the second and third conditionals: *If I had time, I would help; if I had known, I would have called.* In requests it is the polite form: *Would you like some tea? Would you mind closing the door?*

**Deduction and refusal.** — *Will* and *would* also draw conclusions: *That will be the postman* means "I conclude, with confidence, that it is." *Would* is its more cautious cousin: *That would be the postman, I suppose.* And *won't* frequently personifies a thing that refuses to work: *The car won't start.* Tennyson's Ulysses holds two modal forces side by side: "I cannot rest from travel: I will drink / Life to the lees" — inability in *cannot*, determination in *will*.

## Shall and should

**Shall.** — *Shall* was once the ordinary first-person future (*I shall be late; we shall see*), and it remains in Britain in offers and suggestions: *Shall I open the window? Shall we go?* It carries a strong note of the speaker's will in commands and undertakings — *You shall not be forgotten; the tenant shall pay the rent* — and in legal and contractual prose it states a binding rule: *The meeting shall be held on the first Monday.* In the King James Bible and in Shakespeare it marks decree: "Shall be my brother; be he ne'er so vile."

**Should.** — *Should* is the modal of advice, duty, and expectation. It recommends (*You should see a doctor*), states a mild obligation (*You should apologize*), and predicts with confidence (*The train should arrive by six* — that is, it is reasonable to expect it). In a subordinate clause after a verb of suggestion it appears in place of a subjunctive: *I suggest that he should leave* or *that he leave.* With a perfect participle it expresses unfulfilled duty or regret: *I should have studied harder.* It also supplies a formal, tentative condition: *If you should see him, tell him I called.*

**The shall/will rule is a prescription.** — In 1762 Robert Lowth set down what became the classic rule: simple future uses *shall* with *I* and *we* but *will* with *you*, *he*, *she*, *it*, and *they*; determination reverses this, giving *I will* and *you shall*. Later grammarians taught it as a law of the language. It is not one. It is a style preference, strongest in southern British English and largely abandoned in North America, where *will* serves every person. Modern usage keeps *shall* mainly in questions, offers, and legal texts. The history of this "rule" is a clear case of prescription outrunning description.

## Must

**Obligation and necessity.** — *Must* states a necessity, and its force usually comes from the speaker: *You must stop smoking; I must go now.* It is stronger than *should* and more personal than *have to*. Its negative, *mustn't*, states a **prohibition**: *You mustn't touch the stove* means "you are forbidden to." Shakespeare's Hamlet packs a paradox into it: "I must be cruel, only to be kind."

**Certainty, or deduction.** — *Must* is also the modal of confident inference. *You must be exhausted; that must be his car* report a conclusion the speaker regards as near-certain. This is the epistemic *must*. It has no past tense of its own; for a past deduction English uses *must have* + participle: *She must have missed the bus.*

**Mustn't and needn't are not opposites.** — A common error is to treat *mustn't* as the negative of obligation. It is not. *You mustn't go* forbids the action; *you needn't go* and *you don't have to go* remove the necessity but leave you free to go. The opposite of *must* (obligation) is therefore *needn't* or *don't have to*, while *mustn't* is the opposite of *may* (permission). The same split runs through the epistemic use: the negative of *That must be true* is *That can't be true*, not *That mustn't be true*.

## Ought to

**Duty and probability.** — *Ought* is the only core-like modal that keeps *to*: *You ought to apologize; we ought to leave soon.* It expresses moral duty and reasonable expectation much as *should* does: *The film ought to be good.* Its negative is *ought not to* or *oughtn't to*, and its past form is *ought to have* + participle: *I ought to have known.* Mary Shelley's creature says, "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel" — duty and identity in one modal.

**Should and ought to.** — The two overlap heavily. *Should* is the more frequent, the more subjective, and the more flexible; *ought to* is slightly more formal and often feels more objective, as if the obligation stood outside the speaker. Some older guides tried to reserve *should* for the speaker's own judgement and *ought* for an external duty, but usage does not maintain so sharp a line. Both take a perfect infinitive for the past, and both appear in the deduction sense: *She should have arrived; she ought to have arrived.*

## Modals of deduction

**A scale of certainty.** — The epistemic modals form a scale. *That must be his car* places a conclusion at the top of the scale, near certainty. *That can't be his car* is the same certainty, negated. *That may be his car*, *might be*, and *could be* place it lower, as a real possibility. *That should be his car* and *that ought to be* put it at "reasonable to expect." *That will be his car* is the confident prediction of a speaker who has already decided. The choice among them is the speaker's calibration of doubt. Jane Austen opens *Pride and Prejudice* with a confident epistemic *must* whose content is anything but certain: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The irony lives in the gap between the firm modal and the flimsy claim.

**Can't, not mustn't.** — The negative of deduction is *can't* (or *couldn't*), never *mustn't*. *That mustn't be his car* would mean "he is not allowed to own that car," a root reading, not a conclusion. This is one of the clearest places where a change of modal changes the whole kind of meaning, from what is required to what is known.

**Past deduction.** — For conclusions about the past the pattern is modal + *have* + past participle: *She must have left already; she can't have left already; she might have left already; she may have left.* Notice that *must have* and *can't have* are the two certainties, positive and negative, and that both point backwards in time.

## The past of a modal

**Modal + have + participle.** — English has no past tense for the modals as such. Instead it builds a **modal perfect** with *have* and the past participle, and the result usually looks back at an unrealized or completed possibility, obligation, or conclusion.

- *could have* — an unrealized past possibility: *I could have helped, but I did not know.*
- *should have* and *ought to have* — an unfulfilled duty, often with reproach: *You should have told me.*
- *may have* and *might have* — a past possibility: *She might have taken the wrong train.*
- *must have* — a past conclusion: *He must have forgotten.*
- *would have* — a counterfactual: *I would have come if I had known.*
- *needn't have* — an action done that was not necessary: *You needn't have waited*, compared with *you didn't need to wait*, which may mean the waiting never happened.

**The "could of" error.** — In rapid speech *could have*, *should have*, and *would have* reduce to *could've*, *should've*, *would've*, which sound like "could of," "should of," "would of." The written form *of* is an error; the auxiliary is *have*. This is a spelling and hearing mistake, not a grammatical alternative, and it is one of the most common faults in student writing.

## Semi-modals and their work

**Have to.** — *Have to* is the workhorse that supplies *must* with the forms it lacks: a past (*I had to work late yesterday*), a future (*I will have to ask*), an infinitive (*I don't want to have to choose*), and a participle (*having had to wait*). It also differs in flavour. *Must* typically expresses an obligation the speaker feels or imposes; *have to* expresses one that comes from outside — a rule, a timetable, a circumstance. *I must phone my mother* (I feel I ought to) contrasts with *I have to phone my mother* (she expects it, or it is arranged). The line is a tendency, not a wall, and the two are often interchangeable. The informal British *have got to* does the same work: *I've got to go.*

**Be going to.** — *Be going to* marks a future that is already intended or already visible in the evidence: *I'm going to visit Rome next year* (a plan) and *Look at those clouds — it's going to rain* (a prediction from present signs). It contrasts with *will*, which more often marks a decision made at the moment of speaking or a bare prediction: *I'll get the phone.* Like the modals it has full finite forms, so it survives where *will* cannot.

**Used to.** — *Used to* marks a past habit or state that no longer holds: *I used to walk to school; there used to be a shop on this corner.* It has no present form. Its negative and question are built with *did*, and the spelling loses the *d* after *did*: *I didn't use to like coffee; did you use to live here?* The form *didn't used to* is common in speech but is rejected in careful writing.

**Need to.** — *Need* is a double agent. As an ordinary verb it takes *to* and inflects: *You need to rest; she needs to know.* As a marginal modal it takes a bare infinitive, no *-s*, and *not* directly: *He need not wait; you needn't worry; need I say more?* The modal use is mostly confined to negatives and questions. *Dare* behaves in the same split way: *He dare not answer* beside *He doesn't dare to answer.*

**Other near-modals.** — *Had better* gives strong advice, stronger than *should*: *You'd better leave now*, with the negative *You'd better not leave.* *Would rather* expresses preference: *I'd rather stay.* *Be able to*, *be allowed to*, and *be supposed to* fill other gaps. The older term for this group is **semi-auxiliaries** (Randolph Quirk and colleagues, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, 1985); Huddleston and Pullum's *Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* (2002) calls them **semi-modals** and reserves "core modals" for the nine or ten central members.

## Modals and politeness

**Tentative past forms.** — The past-tense modals *could*, *would*, and *might* do not point to past time when they make requests; they create social distance and therefore politeness. *Can you help me?* is a plain request; *Could you help me?* is softer; *Would you mind helping me?* is softer again, and expects the answer "No, not at all." *May I?* and *Might I?* are the formal end of the permission scale. The mechanism is a kind of metaphor: distance in time stands for distance between speaker and hearer.

**Indirect requests.** — Politeness often arrives through an indirect frame: *I was wondering whether you could give me a hand*; *You couldn't lend me a pen, could you?* The negative question and the tag both invite refusal, which is what makes them courteous. A request that names the hearer's ability rather than their willingness — *Could you pass the salt?* — is especially common, because it leaves the hearer free to say no.

## Description, prescription, and disputed points

**Rules that are style preferences.** — Several familiar "rules" of English are not grammar at all but taste. The prohibition on the **split infinitive** (*to boldly go*) has no basis in English syntax; the construction is old and idiomatic, and the ban was imported from Latin, where the infinitive is a single word and cannot be split. The ban on **ending a sentence with a preposition** (*the person I spoke to*) ignores the fact that English verbs and prepositions form units and that the pattern is native. The ban on **beginning a sentence with *And*** or *But* is a schoolroom habit; the King James Bible and every major English prose writer begin sentences that way. Each is a preference that a writer may keep or drop, not a rule the language enforces.

**The modal prescriptions.** — The same caution applies to the modals. The *shall/will* rule is a prescription, not a description. The *can/may* distinction for permission is a matter of formality, not correctness. And **double modals** — *might could*, *may can*, *used to could* — are perfectly systematic in Scottish, Northern Irish, and Southern American English even though standard English rejects them; they are nonstandard, not illogical. What makes a modal construction wrong is usually not that it breaks a rule of syntax but that it belongs to a different register or dialect from the one the writer intends.

**What to notice in a sentence.** — To analyse a modal, ask five questions. (1) Which word is the modal, and what is its subject? (2) Does it pass the formal tests — no *-s*, bare infinitive, no *do*? (3) Is the meaning root (ability, permission, obligation) or epistemic (possibility, deduction)? (4) What time does it point to, and does it need *have* + participle to reach the past? (5) What would replace it if a finite form were required — *be able to*, *have to*, *be allowed to*? Take the sentence *That can't have been easy.* The modal is *can't*; the subject is *that*; it passes the tests; the meaning is epistemic — a past deduction; and the paraphrase is *I conclude that it was not easy.* That analysis is the whole method in miniature.

## Chronology

- **Old English (to c. 1100)** — The ancestors of the modals are **preterite-present** verbs: verbs whose present tense is formed from an old perfect. *Cunnan* ("know"), *magan* ("be able"), *motan* ("be allowed"), *sculan* ("owe"), *willan* ("wish"), and *agan* ("possess") all have full inflections, including infinitives and participles.
- **Middle English (c. 1100–1500)** — The class loses most of its inflections. *Can* shifts from "know" to "be able"; *shall* and *will* begin to serve as future markers; *must* and *ought* become fixed in old preterite forms.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* lists the auxiliaries and states the doctrine that *shall* and *will* have distinct forces according to person.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray's *English Grammar* codifies the **potential mood** — *may*, *can*, *might*, *could*, *would*, *should* — as one of five moods, and the scheme is taught in schools for a century.
- **1818** — William Cobbett's *A Grammar of the English Language* mocks "all the fanciful distinctions" of tense and insists there are only three times.
- **1891** — Henry Sweet's *A New English Grammar* classifies the modals as **anomalous verbs**, defined by their defective forms rather than by their meanings.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen's *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles* argues that English has only two tenses proper and that *shall* and *will* are modal, not a true future tense.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* includes a long entry on "shall and will," the classic battlefield of the prescription.
- **1985** — Quirk, Greenbaum, Leech, and Svartvik's *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language* describes **modal auxiliaries** and **semi-auxiliaries**.
- **1986** — F. R. Palmer's *Mood and Modality* sets out the deontic, epistemic, and dynamic categories.
- **2002** — Huddleston and Pullum's *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* distinguishes **core modals** from **semi-modals** and gives the modern descriptive account.

## Questions to carry

- Why does English have a class of verbs with no infinitive and no participle, and what does the language do to fill the gaps?
- Is *will* a future tense or a modal of prediction and volition? What turns on the answer?
- When *must* and *can't* both express certainty, why is *mustn't* not the negative of deduction?
- The shall/will rule was taught for two centuries and then faded. What does its history show about how grammatical "rules" are made?
- Where does a genuine grammatical error end and a difference of dialect or register begin — for example with *might could*, or with *can I* for permission?
