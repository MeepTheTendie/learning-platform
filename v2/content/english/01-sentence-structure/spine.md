# The sentence: subject and predicate

**Period:** English grammar, sixteenth to twenty-first centuries

## What a sentence is

**A sentence, defined.** — A sentence is the smallest unit of language that makes a statement, asks a question, gives a command, or utters an exclamation. The schoolroom definition is older than the grammars quoted in this unit. "A SENTENCE is an assemblage of words, expressed in proper form, and ranged in proper order, and concurring to make a complete sense," wrote Robert Lowth in 1762. Lindley Murray repeated the formula a generation later, dropping the "proper order": "A sentence is an assemblage of words, forming a complete sense." The definition is circular — it tells you that a sentence is what makes complete sense, and complete sense is what a sentence makes — and it hides the real question, which is grammatical rather than logical. Henry Sweet saw the difficulty in 1891 and redefined the sentence by its *form*, not its meaning: a sentence is "a word or group of words whose form makes us expect it to express a full meaning." "We say 'expect,'" he added, "because it depends on the context whether or not any one sentence expresses a complete meaning." That is the modern view: a sentence is a grammatical construction, and completeness is a matter of form and context, not a quantity of meaning that can be measured.

**The trouble with "complete thought."** — The phrase "complete thought" survives in countless schoolbooks, but it is not a test you can apply. *Jesus wept* (John 11:35) is two words and a complete sentence. *The man who came to dinner* is six words, contains a subject and a verb, and is not a sentence: it is a noun phrase that leaves you waiting. *Come!* is one word and a sentence, though nothing is said about who is to come. Conversely, *He is coming* is formally complete but, as Sweet noted, "shows on the face of it that it is incomplete in meaning, for *he* means 'some one who has been mentioned before,' and makes us ask 'who is he?'" Meaning is supplied by context; grammatical form is what the grammarian can describe.

**The clause and the phrase.** — A **clause** is a group of words built around a subject and a finite verb. A **phrase** is a group of words that does not contain that subject–verb pair and works as a single part of speech: a noun phrase (*the last bus*), a verb phrase (*will have gone*), an adjective phrase (*very fond of cake*), an adverb phrase (*before dawn*), or a prepositional phrase (*under the stairs*). Lowth defined a phrase as "two or more words rightly put together in order to make a part of a Sentence; and sometimes making a whole Sentence." The distinction matters because a sentence is made of clauses, and clauses are made of phrases. A sentence with one clause is **simple**; the taxonomy of simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences is a taxonomy of how clauses combine.

**Subject and predicate.** — Every clause has two principal parts. The **subject** names what is being talked about; the **predicate** says something about it. Lowth, following the logic of his day, called them the **Agent** and the **Attribute**, with an **Object** as a third part. Murray used "subject," "attribute," and "object," and his example parses itself: in *A wise man governs his passions*, "a wise man is the subject; governs, the attribute, or thing affirmed; and his passions, the object." Sweet gave the same division in psychological terms: "All thoughts require at least two ideas: (a) what we think of, called the subject... and (b) what we think concerning it, called the predicate." The **simple subject** is the noun or pronoun at the centre (*man*); the **complete subject** is that noun together with all its modifiers (*a wise man*). The **simple predicate** is the verb (*governs*); the **complete predicate** is the verb with its objects, complements, and adverbials (*governs his passions*).

**The Latin model.** — Lowth, Murray, and Cobbett all worked from Latin grammar, where the sentence was analysed into subject and predicate and the parts of speech were defined by their behaviour in Latin. English, which lost most of its case endings and depends instead on word order and prepositions, does not fit that mould cleanly. Lowth himself noticed that English "determines the cases in Nouns" by order rather than inflection. The mismatch explains several schoolroom rules: the ban on the split infinitive (Latin *amare* cannot be split), the ban on the final preposition (Latin prepositions precede their objects), and the insistence that *It is I* is more correct than *It is me* (Latin *ego sum*). Each rule imports a Latin fact into English, where it need not hold.

**Finding the subject.** — The subject is what the verb agrees with, and the question test is to ask *who?* or *what?* before the verb: *Birds sing* — who sings? *Birds*. The subject normally precedes the verb, but English allows several inversions. In questions the auxiliary moves first: *Was it he?* In *there*-sentences the grammatical subject follows the verb: in *There was a man*, the subject is *a man*, and *there* is a dummy element with no referent. In poetry and rhetoric the order may be reversed for emphasis: "Upon thy right hand did stand the Queen" (Psalm 45:9). Imperative sentences have an implied subject, *you*: *Go, thou traitor!* names it, but *Close the door* does not. And in the **passive voice** the object of the active sentence becomes the subject: *Alexander conquered the Persians* becomes *The Persians were conquered by Alexander*. Lowth recorded all these patterns, and they are still the ones that trip up students who assume the first noun is always the subject.

**Subjects that are not what they seem.** — Several English constructions put a word in the subject position that does not name the actor. **Dummy *it*** fills the subject slot for weather and time (*It is raining. It is late*) and for **extraposition**, where a clause is moved to the end (*It is certain that she will come*, from *That she will come is certain*). **Existential *there*** does the same work (*There is a problem*), with the notional subject after the verb. **Cleft** and **pseudo-cleft** sentences divide a single proposition into two clauses for emphasis: *It was the rain that stopped us*; *What stopped us was the rain.* In each case the grammatical subject is not the logical subject, and an analyst who asks only "what is the first noun?" will get the wrong answer.

**The predicate.** — The predicate is everything the clause says about the subject, and it always contains a verb. What follows the verb determines the sentence pattern. A verb that needs nothing to complete it is **intransitive**; one that needs an object is **transitive**; one that needs a word describing the subject is **linking**. These are properties of the verb *in a particular sentence*, not permanent labels. *Sing* is intransitive in *The birds sing* and transitive in *The choir sang a hymn*. *Run* is intransitive in *She ran* and transitive in *She runs a bakery*. The old grammarians often treated the classification as fixed, which is why their rules misfire; modern descriptive grammarians treat transitivity as a property of the verb-plus-complement construction.

**How to analyse a clause.** — A practical procedure: (1) Find the finite verb; it is the head of the predicate. (2) Ask *who?* or *what?* before it to find the subject. (3) Ask whether the verb needs anything after it. If it needs nothing, the pattern is SV. If it needs an object, find the direct object by asking *what?* or *whom?* (SVO). (4) If two objects follow, test whether the first can become a *to* or *for* phrase (SVOO). (5) If the verb is a form of *be* or another linking verb, what follows is a subject complement (SVC). (6) If a word after the object describes that object, it is an object complement (SVOC). (7) Mark every remaining phrase as an adverbial or modifier. The procedure is not infallible — verbs like *make* and *find* are ambiguous — but it forces the analyst to give a reason for each label.

## The five basic sentence patterns

**Why five.** — English clauses can be described by the sequence of their core elements: subject (S), verb (V), object (O), and complement (C). Five patterns cover the great majority of simple English clauses. The scheme is a teaching device, not a law of nature. Sweet, Jespersen, and later grammarians such as Quirk and Huddleston all proposed different inventories. Quirk's *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language* (1985) lists seven clause types, and Huddleston and Pullum's *Cambridge Grammar* (2002) refuses the scheme altogether; but the five patterns are useful because they turn the vague question "what kind of sentence is this?" into a set of concrete tests.

**1. Subject–verb (SV).** — The verb is intransitive and the clause is complete with subject and verb alone: *Birds sing. The baby slept. Time flies.* Wordsworth opens "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud" with an SV clause, *I wandered*, then adds an adverbial, *lonely as a cloud*; the adverbial is not a core element, so the pattern is still SV. The shortest complete sentence in the King James Bible, *Jesus wept* (John 11:35), is SV.

**2. Subject–verb–object (SVO).** — The verb is transitive and takes a direct object, the thing affected by the action: *The early bird catches the worm. God created the heaven and the earth* (Genesis 1:1). The direct object answers *what?* or *whom?* after the verb. A useful test: the object of an active clause can usually become the subject of a passive one — *The worm is caught by the early bird* — while the SV clause *Birds sing* has no passive.

**3. Subject–verb–complement (SVC).** — The verb is a linking verb (*be, seem, become, appear, feel, taste, sound, remain, grow*), and what follows describes or identifies the subject. That word is the **subject complement**: an adjective (*The soup tastes salty*), a noun (*He became a doctor*), or a noun phrase (*The Lord is my shepherd*, Psalm 23:1). The test for a subject complement is identity: the complement and the subject refer to the same thing, so they can swap places — *My shepherd is the Lord*. Dickens built the most famous English sentence on this pattern: *It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.*

**4. Subject–verb–indirect object–object (SVOO).** — Some verbs take two objects: a **direct object** (what is given, sent, told, or shown) and an **indirect object** (to whom or for whom). *She gave the dog a bone. I told him the truth. He sent his mother a letter.* The indirect object can usually be rephrased as a prepositional phrase after the direct object: *She gave a bone to the dog.* This is the test that separates SVOO from SVO with an adverbial.

**5. Subject–verb–object–complement (SVOC).** — The verb takes a direct object and then a word that describes or names that object. That word is the **object complement**: *They elected him president. She painted the door red. The court found the man guilty.* The object complement is not the same as an indirect object. *She made him a cake* is SVOO (she made a cake for him); *She made him a star* is SVOC (she caused him to be a star). The meaning of *make* decides which pattern is in play.

**Complements and adjuncts.** — Not everything after the verb is part of the pattern. A **complement** is required by the verb and completes its meaning; an **adjunct** (or adverbial) is optional and can be removed without destroying the clause. In *She put the book on the table*, the phrase *on the table* looks like an ordinary prepositional phrase, but *put* requires it: **She put the book* is not English. In *She read the book on the table*, the same phrase is an adjunct — she might have read it elsewhere. The distinction between complements and adjuncts is one of the hardest in English grammar, and it is the reason the five patterns describe the core and leave the periphery to the adverb.

## Direct and indirect objects, and complements

**Direct and indirect objects.** — The **direct object** is the person or thing directly affected by the verb. The **indirect object** is the recipient or beneficiary. Only certain verbs — *give, send, tell, show, lend, offer, promise, teach, buy, bring, make* — take both. The order is normally indirect then direct, but when the direct object is a pronoun and the indirect object is long, English prefers the prepositional form: *Give it to the woman in the blue coat* rather than *Give the woman in the blue coat it*. Two pronouns can occur (*Give me it*), though many speakers prefer *Give it to me*. Passivization reveals the difference: either object can become the subject of a passive — *The dog was given a bone* and *A bone was given to the dog* — but the direct-object passive is the more usual.

**Subject and object complements.** — Both are complements, but they complete different things. A **subject complement** follows a linking verb and describes the subject (*The sky grew dark*). An **object complement** follows the object of a transitive verb and describes that object (*The rain made the sky dark*). The distinction turns on whether the word after the verb relates back to the subject or forward to the object. Old grammars called the subject complement the "predicate nominative" or "predicate adjective"; the object complement had no settled name until the twentieth century.

**Transitive, intransitive, linking.** — A **transitive** verb takes a direct object; an **intransitive** verb does not; a **linking** verb joins the subject to a complement rather than acting on an object. The categories overlap and shift. *Feel* is linking in *I feel tired* and transitive in *I feel the rough cloth*. *Taste* is linking in *The soup tastes good* and transitive in *She tasted the soup*. A common error is to use an adverb where a linking verb demands an adjective: *The soup tastes badly* is wrong because *tastes* is linking and takes the adjective *bad*. But *She tasted the soup carefully* is right, because *tasted* is transitive and the adverb modifies the verb. The same verb, two patterns, two different modifiers.

## Sentence types by function

**Declarative.** — A declarative sentence makes a statement: *The cat sat on the mat. I wandered lonely as a cloud.* The subject precedes the verb, and the sentence ends with a full stop. It is the default pattern of English prose.

**Interrogative.** — An interrogative sentence asks a question. There are four main kinds. **Yes/no questions** invert subject and auxiliary: *Was it he? Did Alexander conquer the Persians?* **Wh-questions** begin with an interrogative word: *What did you see? Who wrote this?* **Alternative questions** offer a choice: *Tea or coffee?* **Tag questions** append a short reversed auxiliary: *You came, didn't you?* Lowth noticed that the interrogative "depends intirely on the order of the words" — a statement becomes a question by moving the auxiliary, and modern grammar agrees.

**Imperative.** — An imperative sentence gives a command, makes a request, or offers an invitation. Its subject is the implied *you*, which is normally omitted: *Close the door. Go, thou traitor! Do thou go.* The negative imperative uses *do not* or *don't*: *Don't touch that.* Lowth observed that *let* supplies a first- or third-person imperative: *Let us be gone. Let him try.* Imperatives are the clearest case of a sentence whose subject is present in the grammar but absent from the page.

**Exclamatory.** — An exclamatory sentence expresses strong feeling and usually ends with an exclamation mark. Some are fixed formulas (*What a mess! How strange!*); others are ordinary declaratives or imperatives given exclamatory intonation (*You came!*). The category overlaps with the others, which is why many grammarians treat it as a matter of tone rather than a distinct sentence type.

**Minor sentences.** — Not every utterance has a subject and a verb. *Thanks. Good morning. Three sugars. Out!* are **minor sentences** or **sentence-words**. Sweet called them "one-word-sentences" and observed that the subject may be "self-evident," as in *come!* = "I command you to come." Modern grammarians call the phenomenon **ellipsis** when the missing words are recoverable from context, and treat minor sentences as a normal part of the language rather than a failure of grammar.

## Sentence types by structure

**Simple.** — A simple sentence contains exactly one clause: *The baby slept. She gave the dog a bone. He became a doctor.* Lowth and Murray both defined the simple sentence as having "but one Subject, and one Finite Verb." A long simple sentence is still simple if it has only one clause: *In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth* is simple, however many words it has.

**Compound.** — A compound sentence joins two or more independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*) or with a semicolon. *Life is short, and art is long.* *I came, I saw, I conquered.* Each clause could stand alone as a sentence. The comma before the conjunction is conventional; the semicolon is the alternative when the clauses are closely related and no conjunction is used: *Life is short; art is long.* Lowth's definition — "Two or more Simple Sentences, joined together by one or more CONNECTIVE WORDS, become a Compounded Sentence" — is still the working one, though modern grammar distinguishes **coordination** (clauses of equal rank) from **subordination** (one clause inside another).

**Complex.** — A complex sentence contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. A **dependent** or **subordinate** clause has a subject and verb but functions as a part of the larger sentence, introduced by a subordinating conjunction (*because, although, when, if, since, while*) or a relative pronoun (*who, which, that*). *When the rain stopped, we went out.* *The man who came to dinner stayed a week.* Sweet and Jespersen analysed subordination more finely than the school tradition did, distinguishing the clause that modifies a noun from the clause that modifies the whole main clause. The traditional test is simple: if a clause cannot stand alone and make sense, it is dependent.

**Three kinds of dependent clause.** — A dependent clause does the work of a noun, an adjective, or an adverb. A **noun clause** fills a subject or object slot: *That she lied surprised no one*; *I know what you did.* An **adjective (relative) clause** modifies a noun: *The man who came to dinner stayed.* A relative clause may be **restrictive** (*The book that I bought is lost*) or **non-restrictive** (*The book, which I bought yesterday, is lost*); the comma marks the difference between identifying and merely adding information. An **adverbial clause** modifies the main verb or the whole clause: *When the rain stopped, we went out*; *Although it was raining, we went out*; *We went out because the rain stopped.* The traditional labels are worth knowing because they predict punctuation: a non-restrictive relative clause is set off by commas, and a restrictive one is not.

**Compound-complex.** — A compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. *Although it was raining, we went out, and we stayed until dark.* It is the pattern of much formal prose and of the long periodic sentences of the eighteenth century. It is not a fifth kind of clause; it is simply a compound sentence one of whose clauses contains a dependent one.

## Fragments and run-ons

**Fragments.** — A **fragment** (or incomplete sentence) is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but lacking an independent clause. Three kinds are common. The **missing-subject fragment**: *Went to the store.* The fix is to supply the subject: *She went to the store.* The **missing-verb fragment**: *The man in the blue coat.* The fix is to add a verb: *The man in the blue coat waved.* The **dependent-clause fragment**: *Because the rain stopped.* The fix is to attach it to a main clause (*We went out because the rain stopped*) or to drop the subordinator (*The rain stopped*). Fragments are not always errors. Advertisers, poets, and novelists use them deliberately: *"Curiouser and curiouser!" cried Alice.* In exposition, a fragment usually signals that the writer has lost control of the clause; in dialogue and description it may be a deliberate effect.

**Run-ons and comma splices.** — A **run-on** (or fused sentence) joins two independent clauses with no punctuation at all: *I came I saw I conquered.* A **comma splice** joins them with only a comma: *I came, I saw, I conquered* is acceptable because the clauses are short and parallel, but *The rain stopped, we went out* is a comma splice, because a comma is not strong enough to join two independent clauses. There are four standard repairs: a full stop (*The rain stopped. We went out.*), a semicolon (*The rain stopped; we went out.*), a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (*The rain stopped, so we went out.*), or subordination (*When the rain stopped, we went out.*). The comma splice is not a grammatical error in every tradition — the eighteenth-century prose of Addison and Swift contains many — but modern edited English treats it as one.

**Prescription and description.** — Grammar has two jobs, and they are often confused. **Description** records how speakers and writers actually use the language. **Prescription** recommends how they ought to. The distinction matters because several rules taught as grammar are really style preferences, and some are myths. The rule against the **split infinitive** (*to boldly go*) was imported from Latin, where the infinitive is a single word and cannot be split; English has always split it, and the prohibition dates only from the nineteenth century. The rule against **ending a sentence with a preposition** is older. Lowth in 1762 wrote that separating the preposition from its relative "is an Idiom which our language is strongly inclined to; it prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with the familiar style in writing," but that placing the preposition first "is more graceful." Lowth was making a stylistic observation, not a grammatical law; later schoolbooks turned it into one. The rule against **beginning a sentence with *And* or *But*** is not found in Lowth or Murray, and the King James Bible begins hundreds of sentences with *And*. All three "rules" have a place in a style guide, but none is a fact about English grammar. A grammarian's job is to describe what English does; a writer's job is to choose among the options with an eye to clarity and effect.

## Chronology

- **c. 1586** — William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar*, the first grammar of English written in English, adapts the Latin model to English.
- **1762** — Robert Lowth, *A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, defines the sentence, its principal parts (Agent, Attribute, Object), and its simple and compound forms.
- **1795** — Lindley Murray, *English Grammar*, popularises Lowth's scheme for schools and fixes the "subject, attribute, object" terminology for a century.
- **1818** — William Cobbett, *A Grammar of the English Language*, teaches sentence-making to soldiers, sailors, and plough-boys and insists on the practical use of points.
- **1891** — Henry Sweet, *A New English Grammar, Logical and Historical*, defines the sentence by form and analyses subject and predicate, subordination, and coordination.
- **1909–1949** — Otto Jespersen, *A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, studies English usage descriptively and questions the Latin-based subject–predicate model.
- **1926** — H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, codifies and sometimes mocks the prescriptive rules of the schoolroom.
- **1952** — C. C. Fries, *The Structure of English*, analyses recorded conversation and argues that the traditional definition of the sentence does not fit actual speech.
- **1957** — Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures*, shifts grammatical analysis from the sentence's parts to the rules that generate it.
- **1985** — Randolph Quirk and others, *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, describes seven clause types and the full range of English complementation.
- **2002** — Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey K. Pullum, *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, rejects the traditional five-pattern scheme and the schoolroom rules.

## Questions to carry

- Why did grammarians define the sentence by "complete sense," and what does Sweet's definition by form do better?
- In *There was a man*, what is the subject, and why is the answer not obvious?
- Which of the five patterns does *She made him a star* belong to, and how does the test distinguish it from *She made him a cake*?
- Lowth called the final preposition a familiar idiom but preferred the fronted form. Is his position descriptive, prescriptive, or both?
- When is a sentence fragment a mistake, and when is it a deliberate choice?
