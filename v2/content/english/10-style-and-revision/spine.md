# Style and revision

**Period:** English prose, nineteenth to early twentieth centuries

**Two ways to say one thing.** — Herbert Spencer, writing in 1852, asked his readers to compare a sentence with a gesture. "Leave the room," he observed, is a weaker instruction than a finger pointed at the door; a shrug says more than a paragraph about indifference. The words are not wrong. They are simply slower than the body, and in that slowness Spencer found the whole problem of prose. Every sentence asks the reader to spend a little effort before the meaning arrives, and whatever effort the words absorb is taken from the meaning itself. Now take a smaller revision, of the kind any writer makes: "There was a decision made by the committee to reject the plan" becomes "The committee rejected the plan." The facts are identical. The second sentence is not merely shorter. It has moved the actor to the front, turned a noun back into a verb, and dropped the vague "there was" that began it. A reader who meets the first sentence must hold an empty subject in mind while waiting for the real one; a reader who meets the second meets the committee at once. The revision has changed the thought the sentence carries, because it has changed where the reader's attention goes. This unit's organizing question is how that happens — how revising a sentence changes the thought it carries.

**Style is not ornament.** — The first thing to unlearn is the metaphor of clothing. Style is not a coat of paint applied to a finished idea, nor a set of graces laid over plain sense. Spencer's argument is mechanical and, in its own way, radical: language is "an apparatus of symbols for the conveyance of thought," and the reader has only a limited amount of mental power at each moment. Part of that power goes to recognizing the symbols, part to combining the images they suggest, and only what remains can be spent on the thought itself. On this account a word is a cost, and style is the management of cost. Walter Pater, writing in 1889, put the emphasis elsewhere but reached a similar conclusion. For Pater the form and the idea cannot be separated at all: "it is impossible to detach the form from the idea, for the idea only exists by virtue of the form." A thought that has not found its sentence is not yet a thought; it is a pressure without a shape. Between Spencer's economy and Pater's inseparability lies the working claim of this unit: style is the thought, made visible and made precise.

**The one revision that changed an argument.** — The clearest case of a revision altering a thought is also one of the most famous sentences in English. In June 1776 Thomas Jefferson drafted the Declaration of Independence. His rough draft read: "We hold these truths to be sacred & undeniable; that all men are created equal & independant, that from that equal creation they derive rights inherent & inalienable, among which are the preservation of life, & liberty, & the pursuit of happiness." When the Continental Congress took up the draft, it struck "sacred & undeniable" and put in its place "self-evident." The change is small enough to miss. It is also decisive. "Sacred and undeniable" grounds the claim in divine authority: the truths are true because God has made them so, and to deny them is impiety. "Self-evident" grounds the claim in reason: the truths are true because any reasoning person can see them, and to deny them is to contradict oneself. The same sentence now argues from a different foundation. Jefferson had written a theological proposition; the Congress turned it into a rational one. Nothing about the political claim changed. Everything about why the claim should be believed changed, and the change lives in three words. This is what it means to say that revising is not repainting.

**Diction and register.** — Diction is the choice of words, and register is the level at which they are pitched — the difference between the language of a legal brief, a lecture, and a letter to a friend. The two are inseparable, because a word carries not only its meaning but the company it keeps. Spencer noticed that English has two vocabularies, one Germanic and one Latinate, and that the older, shorter words tend to strike the reader with more force. A child says "I have," not "I possess"; he wishes, he does not desire; he thinks, he does not reflect. The Latin synonyms are learned later and so are bound less tightly to the ideas they name. Hence "It is sour" moves the reader faster than "It is acid," though the two mean the same. Spencer added a qualification that keeps the rule from becoming a slogan: a long word can be stronger than a short one when its very bulk suggests the thing it names. "Magnificent" is more than "grand"; "stupendous" more than "vast"; "disgusting" more than "nasty." The point is not length but fit. Robert Louis Stevenson, in an essay of 1885, called this "the apt choice and contrast of the words employed," and described the writer's task as taking the "blocks" of ordinary speech — made for the market and the bar — and by "tact of application" touching them "to the finest meanings and distinctions." Pater's Flaubert carried the same idea to its extreme: among all the expressions in the world there is only one that will do, and the writer's labour is to find it.

**The plain style and the grand style.** — Register is not a ladder from bad to good. It is a set of instruments, and the choice of instrument is a choice about what the sentence is doing. The opening of Edward Gibbon's *The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776) is a specimen of the grand style: "In the second century of the Christian Æra, the empire of Rome comprehended the fairest part of the earth, and the most civilized portion of mankind." The inverted order ("comprehended the fairest part"), the balanced pairs, and the unhurried clause give the sentence the weight of a monument, which is exactly what Gibbon wants: he is beginning a work of many volumes and is telling the reader to settle in. A modern plain paraphrase — "In the second century, Rome ruled the best part of the world" — says roughly the same thing and loses the ceremony. Neither is a failure. Gibbon's register belongs to the eighteenth-century historian addressing an educated public; the plain version belongs to a report. The fault to avoid is register that does not fit the occasion: a joke told in the language of a treaty, or a serious argument delivered in the language of an advertisement. Jane Austen's opening to *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) shows register used as a weapon: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The sentence wears the costume of a philosophical maxim, and the reader is meant to hear the gap between that costume and the small social truth inside it. The irony is not stated anywhere. It is carried entirely by the style.

**Concreteness and the active verb.** — An abstract noun is a sentence's sleeping pill. "The implementation of the policy resulted in an improvement in conditions" asks the reader to unpack three nominalizations before anything happens. "The policy improved conditions" is not just shorter; it puts an actor and an action on the page. The preference for the concrete and the active is old and widely taught. William Strunk Jr., whose *The Elements of Style* was privately printed in 1918 and revised by E. B. White in 1959, made "omit needless words" and "use the active voice" into household rules; George Orwell, in "Politics and the English Language" (1946), argued that vague, pretentious diction both expresses and encourages muddy thought, and urged writers to prefer the concrete image and the short word. Both are prescriptive, and both have been criticized. The passive voice is sometimes the right instrument, as when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately hidden: "the records were destroyed" tells the truth about what the writer can say, and "mistakes were made" is a way of naming an action while refusing to name the actor, which may be cowardice or may be diplomacy. The rule to carry away is not "never passive" but "know who is doing what, and say so unless you have a reason not to." The active verb is a discipline because it forces the writer to find the actor, and finding the actor is often the same act as finding the thought. Notice, too, that the concrete noun is not the same as the simple noun. "A vehicle" is simple and vague; "a hearse" is specific and tells the reader what kind of scene this is. Concreteness is a form of information.

**Figures of speech are economy, not decoration.** — Spencer treats metaphor, simile, and the other figures under the same law of economy. A figure lets the writer suggest in one stroke what literal statement would spell out over several sentences. "The pen is mightier than the sword" compresses an argument about force and persuasion into six words, and it does so by making the abstractions concrete: the reader sees two objects instead of two ideas. Spencer's example is the simile of a row of columns that seem to crowd together as they recede, used to explain why the great events of the past look denser than the events of the present. To build that picture by literal explanation would take many sentences, and "the first elements of the picture would become faint while the imagination was busy in adding the others." The comparison saves the effort and delivers the image whole. Figures have a cost as well as a benefit. A metaphor that has been used until it is invisible — "the foot of the mountain," "the arm of the chair" — no longer gives the reader a picture, and so no longer earns its keep. A mixed metaphor, in which the two images belong to different worlds, asks the reader to hold two incompatible pictures at once: "to take arms against a sea of troubles" is technically incoherent, and has survived because the feeling of helpless struggle holds it together. The rule against mixed metaphor is therefore a rule about coherence of thought, not about grammar.

**Sentence rhythm and variety.** — A paragraph of sentences all built the same way drones. Stevenson heard prose as music in time: "each phrase of each sentence, like an air or a recitative in music, should be so artfully compounded out of long and short, out of accented and unaccented, as to gratify the sensual ear." He described the well-made sentence as a knot that is tied and then solved: "each sentence, by successive phrases, shall first come into a kind of knot, and then, after a moment of suspended meaning, solve and clear itself." That suspension — the moment when the reader does not yet know where the sentence is going — is the engine of the periodic style, in which the main clause waits until the end. Its opposite, the loose sentence, states its point first and then adds qualifications. Neither is better in itself; monotony is the only fault. A long sentence earns its length by carrying a complex thought; a short one lands a conclusion. The writer who alternates them gives the reader both rest and surprise. Gibbon's long opening is followed, in the same paragraph, by shorter sentences that comment on it; Lincoln's Gettysburg Address moves from the long, rolling "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" to the blunt "The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here." The rhythm is part of the argument: the first sentence performs the labor of founding, the second performs the modesty of a speaker at a cemetery.

**Parallelism.** — When two or more parts of a sentence are given the same grammatical shape, the reader hears them as belonging together. "Government of the people, by the people, for the people" works because the three phrases are identical in form and rising in force. Parallelism is not only an ornament of sound; it is a claim about meaning. To put two ideas in the same frame is to assert that they are comparable; to break the frame — to let one clause run long and the next snap short — is to assert a difference. Stevenson called the antithesis a "common figure" and warned that it can be too neat: the writer must be "infinitely various," changing the stitch while keeping the pattern. The fault to avoid is the false parallel, in which the grammar promises a likeness the sense does not deliver. "She liked sailing, hiking, and to read" offends the ear because the list breaks its own contract. Repaired — "sailing, hiking, and reading" — it also becomes a clearer statement, because the writer has had to decide that the three really are the same kind of thing. Parallelism, used honestly, is an instrument of analysis.

**The paragraph as a unit of thought.** — A sentence carries a thought; a paragraph carries a movement of thought. The paragraph break is a signal that the writer has finished one stage and is beginning another, and a paragraph that wanders has usually not decided what its stage is. Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866) is the book that fixed the now-familiar rules — one topic to a paragraph, the topic stated at the start, coherence within — and the rules have lasted because they describe how a reader's attention actually works. A paragraph should be able to be summarized in a sentence, even if that sentence never appears in the text; if it cannot, the writer has probably combined two paragraphs or none. Stevenson thought of the paragraph as the largest unit of the "web" he described, the pattern that runs from phrase to sentence to paragraph to whole work. Revising at the level of the paragraph is different from revising at the level of the sentence: it asks not "is this well said?" but "does this belong here, and is this the moment to say it?" A well-made paragraph can often be rearranged without loss; a paragraph that resists rearrangement has usually found its necessary order, which is a sign that the thought is doing real work. Bain's rule that the topic sentence comes first is a description, not a law: many strong paragraphs delay their point in order to earn it, and the delay is the point.

**Clarity and deliberate ambiguity.** — Clarity is the default virtue of prose, but it is not the only one. A sentence may be clear and false, or obscure and true. Spencer's whole theory aims at the least possible effort for the reader, and for most expository writing that is right. Pater, however, prized what he called "the signs of discovery, of effort and contention towards a due end," and defended the elaborate period when it is "right in its elaboration." There is a difference between ambiguity that comes from not knowing what you mean and ambiguity that comes from meaning more than one thing at once — the first is a failure of revision, the second may be its achievement. Henry James's late sentences, with their trailing qualifications, are difficult on purpose: the difficulty is the record of a mind refusing to simplify. The legal "and/or" is difficult by accident, and a court must decide what it means. The test is whether the writer could state the alternatives plainly if asked. If yes, the ambiguity is a choice; if no, it is a symptom. Clarity, on this view, is not the same as simplicity. A clear sentence can be complex, so long as the complexity belongs to the thought rather than to the grammar.

**Revising is not proofreading.** — These are two different acts, and confusing them is the commonest mistake in a writing life. Proofreading attends to accidents: spelling, punctuation, agreement, the wrong word that the fingers typed. Revising attends to thought: what the sentence claims, what it leaves out, whether it belongs, whether it says what the writer now means. Spencer made the distinction almost in passing when he said that a knowledge of the principles of style is useful "as facilitating revision" — not correction, but the reconsideration of what has been made. Pater's Flaubert, at the end of his labour, listed the stages: "Afterthoughts, retouchings, finish," and insisted they are "of profit only so far as they too really serve to bring out the original, initiative, generative, sense." That last phrase is the key. Revision is not the addition of polish; it is the recovery of the sense that was trying to get out. Proofreading is done when the page is correct. Revision is done when the thought is. A writer who proofreads a draft that argues the wrong thing has cleaned a house built on the wrong lot. The two passes can be done in either order, but they cannot be done at the same time, because the attention they require is different: one looks at the surface, the other through it.

**Cutting.** — The most reliable test of a sentence is what happens when you try to shorten it. If nothing is lost, the cut words were not carrying thought. "Omit needless words" is Strunk's rule, and its deeper form is Pater's "jealous exclusion of what does not really tell." Cutting is not the same as compressing. Compressing squeezes the same meaning into fewer words; cutting decides that some of the meaning was not needed. The first is a skill of the hand, the second of the mind. When a writer cannot cut a passage, the reason is often that the passage is doing two jobs at once and neither is finished; separating the jobs makes the cut obvious. Spencer's rule about the order of clauses is a rule about cutting in advance: put the words where the reader will need them, and the words that were only there to hold a place disappear. A draft is not improved by being long, and it is not improved by being short. It is improved by being exact, and exactness is what cutting finds. The writer should also notice what cutting destroys. A qualifier that seems like clutter may be the only thing keeping a claim honest; to cut "in most cases" from a sentence is to make a promise the writer cannot keep. Cutting is a judgment about meaning, which is why it belongs to revision and not to proofreading.

**Writing is a way of thinking.** — The oldest and most misleading picture of composition is that thought comes first, complete, and writing merely records it. The evidence of the essays in this unit is the opposite. Pater's Flaubert, who believed there was one word for each thing, wrote to a friend that he grew "so hard to please as a literary artist" that he despaired of writing another line; the search for the word was the search for the thought, and it was agonizing because the thought did not exist until the word was found. Edgar Allan Poe, in "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846), claimed to have built "The Raven" backward from its intended effect, choosing every detail for its contribution to a single impression — an account later critics doubt in its details but accept in its principle, that design precedes and shapes the work. William Wordsworth, at the other pole, called poetry "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings," yet added that it takes its origin from "emotion recollected in tranquillity," which is to say from feeling worked over. The two accounts are not as far apart as they look. Writing is thinking in a medium that resists: the sentence that looked right in the mind turns out, on the page, to say something else, and the writer must decide which was meant. Revision is the name for that decision, made over and over. This is why the organizing question is not a trick. To revise a sentence is not to repaint it. It is to find out what it thinks.

**The nineteenth-century moment.** — These ideas about style were not timeless; they were argued in a particular market. Spencer's essay appeared in the *Westminster Review* in 1852, at a moment when cheap print, a widening readership, and a professional class of reviewers had made prose a trade as well as an art. Stevenson's "On Some Technical Elements of Style in Literature" first appeared in the *Contemporary Review* in 1885, when the essay had become a form of public performance and the "man of letters" a recognised figure. Pater's "Style" was published in the *Fortnightly Review* in 1888 and collected in *Appreciations* in 1889. Alongside these essays came the schoolroom tradition: Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866) codified the paragraph, and by the end of the century composition was being taught as a subject with rules. The twentieth century reacted against the rules with rules of its own — Strunk's in 1918, Orwell's in 1946 — and the reaction against those rules is still going on. What has not changed is the situation the essays describe: a writer alone with a sentence, trying to decide whether it says what it means. The tools have multiplied — the typewriter, the word processor, the spell-checker — but none of them has taken over the decision, because the decision is about meaning, and meaning is not mechanical.

## Chronology

- **1776** — Jefferson drafts the Declaration; the Congress changes "sacred & undeniable" to "self-evident."
- **1800** — Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* calls poetry the "spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" and adds that it takes its origin from "emotion recollected in tranquillity."
- **1813** — Austen publishes *Pride and Prejudice*, whose opening sentence carries its irony in its style.
- **1846** — Poe publishes "The Philosophy of Composition," describing a poem built backward from a single intended effect.
- **1852** — Herbert Spencer's "The Philosophy of Style" appears in the *Westminster Review*.
- **1863** — Lincoln's Gettysburg Address gives parallelism its most quoted English example.
- **1866** — Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* sets out the topic sentence and the rules of the paragraph.
- **1885** — Robert Louis Stevenson's "On Some Technical Elements of Style in Literature" appears in the *Contemporary Review*.
- **1888** — Walter Pater's "Style" is published in the *Fortnightly Review*; collected in *Appreciations* the next year.
- **1918** — William Strunk Jr. privately prints *The Elements of Style* for his students at Cornell.
- **1946** — George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" argues that clear prose requires clear thought.
- **1959** — E. B. White's revised *The Elements of Style* becomes a standard handbook.

## Questions to carry

- If a sentence is an "apparatus for conveying thought," as Spencer says, what happens to a thought that will not fit the apparatus?
- Spencer preferred short Saxon words; Pater's Flaubert sought the one exact word, however long. When do the two rules conflict, and which would you follow?
- Is deliberate ambiguity ever a virtue in expository prose, or only in fiction and poetry?
- If revising changes the thought, at what point does the writer stop having a plan and start discovering one?
- Why would a writer cut a sentence that is clear, correct, and true?
