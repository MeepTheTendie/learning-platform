# Narrative and the short story

**Period:** The nineteenth century

## The order of events

**The king and the queen.** — E. M. Forster, lecturing at Trinity College, Cambridge, in the spring of 1927, set two sentences side by side and asked his audience to feel the difference between them. The first was "The king died and then the queen died." That, he said, is a story. The second was "The king died and then the queen died of grief." That is a plot. The words are nearly identical; the difference is a cause. In the first sentence the two deaths merely follow one another, and the listener asks only what happened next. In the second, one death explains the other, and the listener asks why. Forster's pair is the clearest statement we have of what narrative does that a chronicle does not: it arranges events, and the arrangement carries meaning. Reverse the order — the queen dies of grief, and only then do we learn that the king has died — and the same two facts become a mystery rather than a consequence. The facts have not changed. The order has, and with it everything.

**The question this unit carries.** — Why does the order in which we learn things change their meaning? The question governs every decision a storyteller makes: where to begin, what to hold back, through whose eyes to see, and when to let the reader understand what a character does not. The nineteenth century is the period in which those decisions became a self-conscious craft. The short story, as Poe and Maupassant and Chekhov practised it, is the laboratory in which the decisions are easiest to see, because a few pages expose every choice about order. A novel can conceal its construction in length; a story of a thousand words cannot.

**Aristotle on arrangement.** — The oldest surviving analysis of narrative is Aristotle's *Poetics*, written in the fourth century BCE. Aristotle was describing tragedy, not the short story, but the terms he fixed are still in use. He called the arrangement of incidents the *mythos* — usually translated "plot" — and ranked it above character, arguing that tragedy imitates not men but action and life. Two of his terms name the effects that order produces. *Peripeteia* is a reversal, a change of fortune into its opposite; *anagnorisis* is a recognition, a change from ignorance to knowledge. In the strongest plots, Aristotle said, recognition and reversal arrive together, as when a messenger comes to comfort Oedipus and reveals instead who he is. The point to carry forward is his: the same events, differently arranged, produce terror and pity, or produce nothing at all. Plot, for Aristotle, is not the events but their arrangement.

**Story and plot.** — Forster's distinction is a version of Aristotle's, sharpened for the novel. A story, Forster wrote in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), is "a narrative of events arranged in their time-sequence." A plot is also a narrative of events, but "the emphasis falling on causality." The story satisfies curiosity; the plot demands memory and intelligence, because the reader must keep earlier details in mind to understand why later ones matter. Forster added a third sentence to show how far the principle can be pushed: "The queen died, no one knew why, until it was discovered that it was through grief at the death of the king." Here the cause is withheld and then revealed, and the withholding itself becomes the plot. The reader is handed a question before an answer, and the interval between them is where suspense lives.

**A concrete case of order.** — Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" shows what a small reordering can do. Its events, put in chronological order, are: a woman with a weak heart is wrongly told that her husband has died; she grieves, retreats to her room, and discovers that she is free; her husband returns, alive; she dies. Told in that order, the story is a sequence of misfortunes. Told in Chopin's order, with the news arriving in the first sentence and the word "free" arriving only after a page of careful preparation, the story becomes a study in a feeling the reader is made to share before it is named. The last line — the doctors' verdict that she died of "heart disease — of the joy that kills" — is the masterstroke. It is a cause assigned by people who do not know the real cause, and the reader does.

**Fabula and sjuzhet.** — A generation of Russian critics, writing in the 1910s and 1920s, split the same idea in two and gave the halves names. Viktor Shklovsky and Boris Tomashevsky distinguished the *fabula* — the events of a story in their natural chronological order, as they might have happened — from the *sjuzhet*, the order and manner in which those events are actually presented in the text. A murder, an investigation, and an arrest have a fabula order: murder, then investigation, then arrest. A detective story usually begins with the discovery of the body, so its sjuzhet order is investigation first, and the murder is recovered in flashback. The gap between the two orders is where a storyteller works. Shklovsky also argued, in "Art as Technique" (1917), that art exists to make the familiar strange — he called the effect *ostranenie*, defamiliarization — and that narrative order is one of the devices by which a routine event is made to be seen again.

## Who speaks

**Author, narrator, implied author.** — A story is not told by its author. It is told by a narrator, a constructed voice that may share the author's views or may not, and the difference is not a technicality. Wayne C. Booth, in *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961), called the version of the author that a work implies its "implied author," and separated that figure from both the real writer and any narrator the writer invents. The distinction matters because a narrator can be wrong, and the reader can know better. When a narrator is a murderer, the reader's task is not to believe him but to judge him, and the story supplies the evidence for the judgment.

**First person.** — In first-person narration a character inside the story says "I" and can report only what that character witnessed, remembers, or claims to know. The limit is the point. Edgar Allan Poe's "The Cask of Amontillado" (1846) is spoken by Montresor, a nobleman who has murdered his acquaintance Fortunato and is recounting the act fifty years later. He opens: "The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could; but when he ventured upon insult, I vowed revenge." He never says what the insult was. He insists on his own calm and justice: "I must not only punish, but punish with impunity." He smiles in Fortunato's face, and the reader is told that the smile "was at the thought of his immolation." Everything comes through Montresor, and everything is coloured by him.

**Third person limited.** — In third-person limited narration the teller stands outside the action but enters the mind of one character, usually the protagonist, and reports only what that character perceives and thinks. Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894) stays with Louise Mallard from the moment she hears of her husband's death. The reader learns the news as she does, follows her into her room, and learns what she learns there, including the word she whispers "over and over under her breath: 'free, free, free!'" The narration does not enter the minds of Josephine or Richards, so when the ending arrives — Brently Mallard walks in alive — the reader shares Louise's shock and understands its meaning before the doctors do. Chekhov's "The Lady with the Dog" (1899) works the same way with Gurov. Its late turn — "And only now when his head was grey he had fallen properly, really in love—for the first time in his life" — is a piece of plain telling that depends on pages of showing to earn it.

**Omniscient narration.** — The dominant mode of the nineteenth-century novel was omniscient narration, in which the teller knows everything, moves freely among minds, and may comment on the action from a vantage no character shares. George Eliot's *Middlemarch* (1871–72) moves among the minds of Dorothea Brooke, Tertius Lydgate, and a dozen others, and steps forward to address the reader directly, as when the narrator compares Dorothea to Saint Theresa and laments that no epic life was possible for her. Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina* (1878) enters the thoughts of Anna, Vronsky, Levin, and Karenin within a few chapters, and lets the reader see what none of them can. Thackeray's *Vanity Fair* (1848) has a showman-narrator who manages the puppets and comments on the performance. The omniscient narrator is a source of knowledge and of judgment; the reader is told what to think. The reaction against that habit — the demand that a story show rather than tell — is one of the great shifts of the period.

**The unreliable narrator.** — Booth gave a name to a device as old as storytelling: the unreliable narrator. A narrator is reliable, in Booth's account, when the account agrees with the norms the work as a whole establishes; unreliable when it does not. The reader of an unreliable narration must reconstruct a truth the narrator cannot see. Montresor is the type. He is a murderer who presents himself as a wronged man and a rational one, and the reader is asked to read against him. His final line is the giveaway: "My heart grew sick—on account of the dampness of the catacombs." A man who blames his horror on the weather is not telling the whole truth about himself. Poe never states that Montresor is self-deceived. He arranges the evidence so that the reader concludes it, and the conclusion is more disturbing than any statement would be.

## Showing and telling

**The dramatic method.** — The phrase "show, don't tell" is a workshop slogan of the twentieth century, but the argument behind it is older. Henry James, in "The Art of Fiction" (1884), replied to Walter Besant's lecture on the craft of fiction and insisted that the novel must be "a direct impression of life," and that the novelist's business is to catch the colour and pressure of existence rather than to explain it. James's late prefaces, written between 1907 and 1909 and collected as *The Art of the Novel*, describe what he called the "scenic" method: the important turns of a story are rendered as scenes, with dialogue and gesture, rather than summarized. Percy Lubbock, in *The Craft of Fiction* (1921), gave the contrast its familiar names — "picture" and "drama," "showing" and "telling" — and argued that the novel had been moving steadily toward drama.

**Scene and summary.** — The distinction is useful and also oversimple. Every story is a weave of scene and summary, and the art lies in where the thread changes. Poe shows the trowel produced from beneath the cloak — "It is this," I answered, producing a trowel from beneath the folds of my *roquelaire* — and also tells us outright that the murder is planned "with impunity." Chekhov shows Gurov's vanity through his talk of women as "the lower race," and then tells us flatly that Gurov has fallen in love for the first time. The telling lands because the showing prepared it. Summary is not a failure of craft; it is how a story covers time. The question is always which moments deserve to be rendered at full speed and which can be passed over in a sentence.

## Setting, conflict, crisis

**Setting.** — Setting is not backdrop. Poe's catacombs are damp, encrusted with nitre, and lined with the bones of the Montresors; above them the carnival is loud and drunken, and below, the vaults are silent. The trowel and the mortar that wall Fortunato in are not introduced as symbols. They are the materials of the family vault, and the setting supplies them. Chopin's setting is a single room and an open window onto a spring square; the "delicious breath of rain," the twittering sparrows, and the patches of blue sky do the work of the freedom Louise is beginning to feel. Chekhov's setting is two cities and a sea: Yalta in the heat, with its promenade and its "lady with the dog," and Moscow in the snow, where Gurov has a wife, a bank, and a club.

**Conflict and crisis.** — Conflict is the opposition of forces, and it may be external or internal. Montresor against Fortunato is external: a man and his victim, moving through the vaults. Louise's grief against her self-assertion is internal, and so is Gurov's public life against his secret one. Crisis is the moment at which the conflict must be decided. Gustav Freytag, in *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863), diagrammed the shape of a play as a pyramid: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and denouement. The diagram is a useful teaching tool and a poor description of many stories. Chekhov refuses the pyramid. "The Lady with the Dog" reaches its crisis of feeling and then stops, with no falling action and no denouement, ending on the words "the most complicated and difficult part of it was only just beginning." The refusal is the point.

## The epiphany and the modern short story

**The epiphany.** — The word for a sudden moment of insight in fiction comes from James Joyce. In *Stephen Hero*, the early draft of *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man*, Joyce defined an epiphany as "a sudden spiritual manifestation," a moment when a common object or scene reveals its "whatness." *Dubliners* (1914) is built of such moments. A boy in "Araby" stands in a darkened hall and sees his romantic errand as vanity; Gabriel Conroy in "The Dead" watches the snow fall and understands something about the living and the dead. The epiphany is not a moral or a summary. It is a shift in perception, and it is delivered as a scene.

**Chekhov and the story without a twist.** — Chekhov is the other great source of the epiphanic story, and the writer who did most to loosen the form. He wrote several hundred stories for newspapers and journals while training and practising as a physician, and he dropped the mechanical twist ending that had made the form of Poe and Maupassant so satisfying. Instead he thinned the plot, let the meaning gather in the gaps, and ended where a conventional story would only be beginning. "The Lady with the Dog" does not tell us whether the affair will be resolved; it tells us that the two lovers have understood their situation and that the hardest part lies ahead. The story's power comes from what it declines to settle.

**Frank O'Connor on the form.** — In *The Lonely Voice* (1963), the Irish writer Frank O'Connor argued that the short story is the form of "submerged population groups" and of the isolated individual. The novel, he wrote, can create a society and a hero; the short story has no hero, only "little men" whose loneliness the form exposes. O'Connor's Chekhov is a writer who refuses to resolve, who leaves his characters where he found them and yet changed. The argument is a critic's case rather than a rule — many stories do have heroes, and many are not about loneliness — but it identifies something true about the form: the short story tends to find its subject in a single consciousness at a moment of pressure.

## A life in a few pages

**The unity of effect.** — The most distinctive power of the short story is compression: a life, or the turning point of a life, packed into a few pages. Poe made the principle explicit. Reviewing Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* in 1842, he argued that the prose tale, unlike the novel, can be read at a single sitting, and that this allows it to aim at "a single effect" — one dominant impression, to which every sentence contributes. In "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) he described his own method of working backward from the desired effect. The principle is not the only way to write a story, but it explains the form's economy.

**Compression at work.** — Chopin compresses a marriage, a grief, a revelation, and a death into roughly a thousand words and about an hour of clock time; the whole story happens between the arrival of the news and the opening of the front door. Chekhov compresses an affair of months into a handful of scenes and passes over the years of secret meetings in a sentence. Poe compresses fifty years of the narrator's life into the single evening of the murder, then dismisses the half-century that follows in one line: "For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them." In each case the compression is not a shortage of space. It is a way of deciding what matters, and the decision about what to omit is as much a part of the plot as the decision about what to include.

**The nineteenth-century market.** — The form did not begin in the nineteenth century, but it acquired its modern shape there. The tale is ancient; Boccaccio's *Decameron* (c. 1353) and Cervantes's *Exemplary Novels* (1613) are ancestors. What the nineteenth century added was the magazine: cheap print, a mass readership, and a market for fiction that could be read in one number. Washington Irving's *The Sketch Book* (1819–20) gave American readers "Rip Van Winkle" and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow"; Hawthorne collected *Twice-Told Tales* in 1837; Poe reviewed it and made the case for the form. In France, Guy de Maupassant published "The Necklace" in 1884 and hundreds of other stories, most of them built on a single reversal. In America, Kate Chopin wrote "The Story of an Hour" in 1894, some years before her novel *The Awakening*. By the end of the century the short story was not a lesser novel but a distinct instrument, with its own laws of order and omission.

**Reading for order.** — The way to test Forster's distinction is to read a story twice: once as it is written, and once with the events put back into chronological order. What is lost when the detective is introduced before the corpse? What is gained when we learn the queen's cause of death only after the king's? The answer is usually the same: the story is not the events but the path through them. A reader who watches for that path — who notices what is held back and when it is released, and whose eyes the story uses — is reading narrative as a craft and not only as a report.

## Chronology

- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle's *Poetics* defines plot (*mythos*) as the arrangement of incidents and names reversal and recognition.
- **c. 1353** — Boccaccio's *Decameron* collects a hundred tales within a frame narrative.
- **1613** — Cervantes publishes the *Exemplary Novels*, an early landmark of the European short story.
- **1819–20** — Washington Irving's *The Sketch Book* appears, including "Rip Van Winkle."
- **1837** — Hawthorne collects *Twice-Told Tales*.
- **1842** — Poe reviews Hawthorne and argues for the short tale's "unity of effect."
- **1843** — Poe publishes "The Tell-Tale Heart."
- **1846** — Poe publishes "The Cask of Amontillado" and "The Philosophy of Composition."
- **1863** — Gustav Freytag's *Die Technik des Dramas* diagrams dramatic structure as a pyramid.
- **1871–72** — George Eliot's *Middlemarch* appears, a major work of omniscient narration.
- **1878** — Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina* is published.
- **1884** — Henry James's "The Art of Fiction" argues for fiction as a direct impression of life; Maupassant publishes "The Necklace."
- **1894** — Kate Chopin publishes "The Story of an Hour" in *Vogue*.
- **1899** — Chekhov publishes "The Lady with the Dog."
- **1914** — Joyce's *Dubliners* appears, built on moments of epiphany and paralysis.
- **1917** — Shklovsky's "Art as Technique" introduces defamiliarization; Constance Garnett's translation of Chekhov's stories appears.
- **1921** — Percy Lubbock's *The Craft of Fiction* codifies showing and telling.
- **1925** — Tomashevsky's "Thematics" distinguishes *fabula* from *sjuzhet*.
- **1927** — Forster's *Aspects of the Novel* distinguishes story from plot.
- **1961** — Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* names the implied author and the unreliable narrator.
- **1963** — O'Connor's *The Lonely Voice* argues for the short story as the form of the isolated individual.

## Questions to carry

- If a story is the events and a plot is their arrangement, which one does a reader actually remember — the events or the path through them?
- What does a first-person narrator gain by telling a story years after it happened, and what does the reader lose?
- Chopin's narrator stays inside Louise's mind; Poe's narrator is Louise's opposite, a murderer. How does the choice of whose eyes we borrow decide what the story can mean?
- Chekhov ends "The Lady with the Dog" before the problem is solved, and Poe ends "The Cask of Amontillado" long after it is. What does each ending ask the reader to do?
- If showing is more vivid than telling, why do some of the most memorable moments in these stories come as plain statements?
