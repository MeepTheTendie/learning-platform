# Reading a poem closely

**Period:** English poetry, sixteenth to nineteenth centuries

**The poem as a made thing.** — A poem is not a window through which the reader watches the poet's feelings; it is an arrangement of words that someone made, and close reading begins by asking how the arrangement works before asking what it means. Take the first line of Shakespeare's Sonnet 73: "That time of year thou mayst in me behold." The line is not a statement of fact but an invitation to look, and the invitation comes before the reader knows what is to be seen. Close reading is a set of habits rather than a doctrine: notice every word, ask why this word and not a near-synonym, ask what the order of the words does that a different order would not. The method was codified in the twentieth century, but its practitioners never claimed to have invented attention. I. A. Richards, in *Practical Criticism* (1929), handed Cambridge students poems with no author named and no date, and then read their written responses. The students invented biographies for poems they could not identify, praised lines for the wrong reasons, and often agreed on an interpretation that the words did not support. Richards concluded that meaning is not poured from author to reader but built out of the marks on the page, and that even able readers need a discipline for testing what they think they see. That discipline is close reading.

**Denotation and connotation.** — A word has a denotation, the meaning a dictionary records, and connotations, the associations it has gathered from use. "Choirs" denotes a group of singers, or the part of a church where singers sit. In Sonnet 73 the "bare ruin'd choirs" are, first, the branches of a tree stripped of leaves, where birds once sang; but the word also carries the memory of church music and of the monastery choirs left roofless when Henry VIII's commissioners dissolved the abbeys between 1536 and 1541. A reader who stops at the dictionary meaning loses most of the word; a reader who chases every private association loses the poem. The rule is that connotation must be paid for by the rest of the text. "Choirs" can mean the singers, the building, and the branches at once because the poem has already put birdsong, cold, and ruin in the same quatrain. Denotation is what the word must mean; connotation is what it can be made to mean; the poem decides how far the second is licensed by the first.

**The speaker and the poet.** — The "I" of a lyric is a voice the poem constructs, and it is not the same thing as the historical person who wrote the lines. Emily Dickinson's "I heard a fly buzz when I died" is spoken by someone already dead, a situation no biography can explain and no deathbed report can verify. John Donne's "The Flea," published in 1633, is spoken by a seducer who argues that a flea which has bitten both lovers has already joined them, so that to kill it would be self-murder; Donne was by then Dean of St Paul's, and the poem's speaker is not the Dean. A dramatic monologue makes the gap obvious, but every lyric has it: the poet may share the speaker's view, oppose it, or use it to expose what the speaker cannot see. When a poem says "I," the reader should ask who is speaking, to whom, in what situation, and with what degree of self-knowledge. Confusing the speaker with the poet is the commonest error in first readings, and it turns a made character into a confession.

**Tone and dramatic situation.** — Tone is the speaker's attitude toward the subject and toward the hearer; dramatic situation is the scene the words imply, including who is addressed, where, and why. The two depend on each other. Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 addresses a beloved, usually taken to be the young man of the sequence, and asks him to love the speaker more because the speaker is aging. Read the poem as a general complaint about growing old and it becomes self-pity; read it as an argument made to one person in one room, and the closing line, "To love that well, which thou must leave ere long," becomes a request with force behind it. The same words change meaning when the situation changes. Tone is carried by small things: by whether a line ends with a full stop or runs on, by whether the diction is formal or plain, by what the speaker assumes the hearer already knows. A poem's tone is rarely a single color. Sonnet 73 is tender and calculating at once, and the calculation is part of the tenderness.

**Reading for the literal sense first.** — Before asking what a figure means, the reader should make sense of the sentence as a sentence. "That time of year thou mayst in me behold / When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang" is a comparison, but the grammar has to be parsed before the comparison can be felt: the beloved is to behold, in the speaker, the time of year when leaves hang on the boughs. The literal reading also catches mistakes. A summary that calls the poem wintry misses that the season is late autumn, the leaves not gone but going, and that "or none, or few" corrects itself twice in three words. A reader who jumps at once to "autumn equals old age" may never notice that the second and third images are a day and a fire, not a season at all. Figurative language is built on literal statement, and the literal statement is usually stranger and more exact than the paraphrase that replaces it.

**Line breaks, enjambment, and the turn.** — English verse is measured in metrical feet, and the usual line of the English sonnet is iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five stresses, each foot an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one. Sonnet 73 rhymes ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, three quatrains and a closing couplet. A line whose sense finishes with the line is end-stopped; a line that runs on into the next without a pause is enjambed. "When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang / Upon those boughs which shake against the cold" is enjambed: "hang" cannot complete its sense without "Upon those boughs," so the reader is held across the break exactly as the leaves are held on the branch. The sonnet's turn, or volta, is the point at which the argument or feeling changes. In the English sonnet it commonly falls at line 13, where the couplet begins, and in Sonnet 73 the turn is unmistakable: after twelve lines of images, "This thou perceiv'st" gathers them and draws a conclusion. The turn is not a pause in the metre; the beat continues. It is a change in what the poem is doing, from showing to concluding, and the reader who feels it has understood the poem's architecture.

**The sound of the line.** — Metre is a pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, and scansion is the act of marking it. The commonest English foot is the iamb, an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, as in "to-day" or "the cold"; the trochee reverses it, "DAILY"; the anapest adds a syllable, as in "in the west" when the sense leans forward; the spondee sets two stresses side by side, as in "black night." A line of five iambs is iambic pentameter, the measure of the sonnet, of blank verse, and of most English dramatic poetry. Metre is not a metronome. Poets vary the pattern to put weight where they want it, and a reader who scans a line discovers which syllable the line is pressing. Rhyme is the other sound structure: perfect rhyme matches the stressed vowel and what follows it, as "day" matches "May," while slant rhyme matches only part. Around the metre run alliteration, the repetition of initial consonants, and assonance, the repetition of vowels; "Bare ruin'd choirs" and "sweet birds sang" both do part of their work by sound. Reading a poem aloud is not decoration. It is one of the ways the reader finds where the poem's stresses and pauses fall, and a line that is hard to say is often a line the poem wants the reader to stumble over.

**Image, simile, metaphor, personification.** — An image is a piece of sensory detail; a simile compares two things using "like" or "as"; a metaphor identifies them without the connective; personification gives a thing human qualities. Sonnet 73 uses all four. "Yellow leaves" is an image. The comparison of the speaker to a time of year is a metaphor, stated through the phrase "in me." "Death's second self" is a compressed metaphor for night. "The death-bed, whereon it must expire" personifies the fire as a dying body. These names are useful because they let a reader say precisely what a figure is doing, but the names are not the reading. A metaphor is not a code with one hidden term; it is a proposal that two things be seen together, and the reader's task is to test how far the proposal holds. The fire in Sonnet 73 is like a dying person, but it is also unlike one, and the unlikeness is where the poem's feeling lives.

**The danger of paraphrasing a poem away.** — Cleanth Brooks, in *The Well Wrought Urn* (1947), called the reduction of a poem to its prose sense the "heresy of paraphrase." His argument was that a poem's meaning is not a statement that the verse decorates, but something the verse does, so that any paraphrase that is faithful is incomplete and any paraphrase that is complete has stopped being faithful. Reduce Sonnet 73 to "I am old, so love me while you can," and the order of the images is gone, the three repetitions of "In me thou see'st" are gone, the fire that is consumed by the fuel that fed it is gone, and the turn is gone. A summary can be true and still be useless as a reading, because a poem is not a statement dressed in metre; the metre and the sequence are part of the meaning. William Empson, in *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930), showed how a single phrase can carry several meanings at once and how the pressure of the verse holds them together instead of choosing among them. That holding-together is exactly what a paraphrase flattens. The test of a reading is not whether it could replace the poem but whether it sends the reader back to the words with more to see.

**How to annotate.** — Annotation is a record of attention, and it should slow the eye to the speed of the line. Number the lines in the margin. Write the rhyme scheme at the right edge, letter by letter, so the pattern of quatrains and couplet becomes visible. Mark the stresses of the first line, and perhaps of the first line of each quatrain, to feel the pulse the words are set to. Underline repeated words and phrases, since repetition is where a poem usually puts its weight. Circle words whose connotations need checking, and look them up. Draw a line across the page where the turn comes. In the margin write questions, not summaries: Who is "thou"? Why does the poem begin with a verb of seeing? Is "choirs" a building, a group of singers, or a sound? Why does the fire consume what nourishes it? A good annotation should be legible a week later and should never stand in for the poem. Its purpose is to make the reader able to say not what the poem means but what the poem does, line by line, and why. For Sonnet 73 the margin of a first reading might hold a handful of questions: line 1, "behold" — why begin with looking?; line 4, "choirs" — singers, building, or branches?; line 5, "In me" again — the same gesture, a new image; line 13, the turn; line 14, "leave" — who leaves whom? None of these is yet an interpretation. Each is a question the poem has raised, and together they are the raw material from which one can be built.

**A worked reading: Shakespeare's Sonnet 73.** — The poem was first printed in the 1609 quarto of *Shake-speares Sonnets*. It is fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, and it is spoken to a single hearer, "thou." Its argument is that the speaker is near the end of life and that the beloved should therefore love him more, not less.

The first quatrain runs: "That time of year thou mayst in me behold / When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang / Upon those boughs which shake against the cold, / Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang." The opening verb is "behold," a verb of looking, so the poem begins by asking the beloved to see rather than by stating a feeling. The season is late autumn, not winter: the leaves are yellow, or nearly gone, or few. The phrase "or none, or few" corrects itself as it goes, enacting the stripping of the tree, and "do hang" is enjambed so that the sense, like the leaves, is suspended until "Upon those boughs." "Bare ruin'd choirs" is the hinge of the quatrain. Literally the branches are bare and the birds' singing places are ruined; but "choirs" is also the part of a church where singers sat, and the ruins of the dissolved monasteries were still visible in Shakespeare's England. "Where late the sweet birds sang" puts the song in the past: "late" means recently, and the birds stand for the speaker's youth.

The second quatrain repeats the opening gesture: "In me thou see'st the twilight of such day / As after sunset fadeth in the west; / Which by and by black night doth take away, / Death's second self, that seals up all in rest." The image shifts from a year to a day. It is not sunset itself but the afterglow that the poem compares to the speaker, which is a more exact figure for age: the sun has gone and the light remains a little. "By and by" is plain, almost casual speech, and the plainness makes the coming of night sound ordinary and certain rather than dramatic. "Death's second self" is a metaphor for night, night being death's twin or deputy, and "seals up all in rest" suggests a legal sealing, a final closing. The quatrain ends in a full stop; the night is complete.

The third quatrain repeats the gesture once more: "In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire, / That on the ashes of his youth doth lie, / As the death-bed, whereon it must expire, / Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by." The image shrinks again, from a day to a fire on a hearth. The fire lies on the ashes of its own youth, so that the speaker's age is fed by what his youth consumed. The last line is the strangest: the fire is extinguished by the very fuel that kept it alive. There is a grammatical surprise in "his youth," which makes the fire a person, and in "death-bed," which makes the fire a body. The three images have moved from the largest unit to the smallest, and each is shorter-lived than the one before: a season, a day, a fire. The shrinking is itself part of the meaning, since the poem is about a life running down.

The couplet turns: "This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong, / To love that well, which thou must leave ere long." "This" gathers the three images; "thou perceiv'st" changes the poem from showing to concluding. The argument is that because the beloved sees the speaker's decline, his love will grow stronger, since he must soon lose what he loves. The last line names the beloved's loss rather than the speaker's death, so the poem's final subject is the survivor. "Leave" is ambiguous: the beloved leaves the speaker, or the speaker leaves the beloved, or the beloved loses the speaker to death. The ambiguity is not a flaw. It lets one word carry both griefs, and it is the kind of thing a paraphrase cannot reproduce.

**Two more turns: Herbert and Blake.** — The turn is not exclusive to the sonnet, and two short poems show what it can do elsewhere. George Herbert's "The Collar," printed in *The Temple* in 1633, is spoken by a man who chafes against his calling and resolves to break free. The poem runs in irregular lines and impatient rhythms, and then, near the end, it turns on a single word: "But as I rav'd and grew more fierce and wilde / At every word, / Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe: / And I reply'd, My Lord." The turn is a change of addressee and of heart at once, and the poem's whole movement is carried by the interruption. William Blake's "The Tyger," from *Songs of Experience* (1794), turns by refusing to turn. Its questions — "What immortal hand or eye / Could frame thy fearful symmetry?" — are asked and re-asked, and the poem ends by repeating its opening question with one word changed, from "Could frame" to "Dare frame." The repetition is the point: the poem does not resolve its wonder, it sharpens it. Both poems show that the turn can be a reversal, as in Herbert, or a refusal of reversal, as in Blake, and that in either case the reader has to locate the exact word where the change happens.

**What the poem does that a summary loses.** — The poem is not "about old age" in the way a summary is. It builds three images that grow progressively smaller and more domestic; it repeats "In me thou see'st" so that the beloved is made a witness; it suspends the reader across enjambments; it holds its argument until the couplet, so that the reader is made to see before being told what to conclude. A paraphrase keeps the conclusion and throws away the seeing. The poem's work is to make the conclusion feel earned, and the earning is the poem. This is why close reading is not a preliminary to interpretation but a form of it, and why the best test of a reading is whether it can point to the words that produced it.

**Practical criticism and its critics.** — The method described here was shaped by a particular group of twentieth-century readers. I. A. Richards's classroom experiment at Cambridge produced *Practical Criticism* in 1929 and showed how unreliable untrained response could be. His student William Empson pushed the analysis of ambiguity further in *Seven Types of Ambiguity* in 1930. In the United States, Cleanth Brooks and Robert Penn Warren built a teaching practice around the "close reading" of poems as self-contained verbal objects, and Brooks's *The Well Wrought Urn* in 1947 gave the practice its most famous slogan, the heresy of paraphrase. Later scholars objected that this method treated poems as if they had no history, no patrons, no readers, and no author, and they read the same texts through biography, politics, and the conditions of printing. The objection has force. Yet the discipline of attending to the words survived the argument about context, because no historical or political reading can be tested without it. Helen Vendler's *The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets* (1997) is a late example: it reads each sonnet as a designed structure of speech, and it treats the order of the words, not the biography of the poet, as the evidence.

## Chronology

- **c. 1580–1595** — Sir Philip Sidney writes *An Apology for Poetry*; it is printed in 1595, after his death.
- **1609** — *Shake-speares Sonnets*, including Sonnet 73, is printed in London by Thomas Thorpe.
- **1633** — The poems of John Donne and of George Herbert are published posthumously; Herbert's *The Temple* includes "Easter Wings" and "The Collar."
- **1794** — William Blake publishes *Songs of Experience*, including "The Tyger."
- **1798** — Wordsworth and Coleridge publish *Lyrical Ballads*.
- **1800** — The second edition adds Wordsworth's Preface, with "the real language of men" and "emotion recollected in tranquillity."
- **1846** — Edgar Allan Poe publishes *The Philosophy of Composition*, his account of how he wrote "The Raven."
- **1890–1896** — Emily Dickinson's *Poems* appear in three series, edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson.
- **1929** — I. A. Richards publishes *Practical Criticism*.
- **1930** — William Empson publishes *Seven Types of Ambiguity*.
- **1947** — Cleanth Brooks publishes *The Well Wrought Urn*.
- **1997** — Helen Vendler publishes *The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets*.

## Questions to carry

- What does Sonnet 73 do in its first twelve lines that its closing couplet could not do alone?
- Dickinson's "I heard a fly buzz when I died" is spoken by a dead person. What does that impossible speaker let the poem say that a living speaker could not?
- Where is the line between a connotation the poem supports and one the reader supplies?
- If a paraphrase is a heresy, how can a reader ever say what a poem means without committing it?
- Wordsworth says poetry is "emotion recollected in tranquillity." Does Sonnet 73 read as recollection, or as speech made in the moment? What in the words decides?
