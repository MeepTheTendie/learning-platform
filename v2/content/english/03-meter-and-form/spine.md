# Meter and form

**Period:** English poetry, sixteenth to nineteenth centuries

**The same words, twice.** — Say "To be, or not to be, that is the question" as flat prose and it is almost a shrug. Say it as Shakespeare arranged it and the stresses gather: to BE, or NOT, to BE, that IS, the QUES-tion. Nothing has been added but the placing of beats, and the sentence has changed its weight. English meter is the deliberate arrangement of stress into a repeating pattern, and the question this unit returns to is how that pattern changes what a line says. The pattern is not a frame laid over the meaning; it is one of the means by which the meaning is made. Move a beat and the words survive, but the emphasis does not.

## The beat in the language

**Stress, not length.** — Greek and Latin verse was quantitative: it counted long and short syllables, and a syllable was long or short by rules that had little to do with how emphatically a speaker pronounced it. English has no such stable quantities. What it has is stress, the prominence a syllable receives from loudness, length, and pitch, and what it has above all is a rhythm that swings between stressed and unstressed syllables. English is a stress-timed language: the beats tend to fall at roughly equal intervals, and the syllables between them are crowded or stretched to fit. Say "the boy on the burning deck" and the ear hears three beats and a tumble of weaker syllables between them. English meter takes that natural swing and regularises it into a pattern a reader can predict. The result is a convention, not a law of speech: the pattern is abstract, the reading is a performance, and the interest of verse lies in the distance between the two.

**The line.** — A poem is written in lines, and the line is the unit the ear counts. The line break is not a full stop; it is a pause, a turn, and a promise. Where the line ends determines how the rhythm is felt, and the poet may satisfy the expectation the break sets up or frustrate it. This is why the same words can be verse in one arrangement and prose in another. It is also why a reader who ignores the line endings is not reading the poem. The line is the smallest repeating unit of meter, and the foot is the smallest unit inside it.

**The foot and its five common shapes.** — Prosody borrowed its vocabulary from Greek, and the names still describe English feet even though the quantities do not transfer. A foot is a small group of syllables built around one stress. The iamb is an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one: "to BE," "the SUN," "aGAIN." It is the commonest foot in English because it matches the article-noun and preposition-noun habits of the language. The trochee is the reverse, stressed then unstressed: "NEV-er," "DOUB-le," "TY-ger." The anapest is two unstressed syllables and then a stress: "in the NIGHT," "on the ROAD," "to the WOODS." The dactyl is a stress and then two unstressed: "MER-ri-ly," "CAN-non to," "THIS is the." The spondee is two stressed syllables together: "HEART-break," "DARK night," "old AGE." The spondee is rare as a whole foot because English tends to alternate, and it usually appears as a substitution, a momentary stiffening of the line. Prosodists also name the pyrrhic, two unstressed syllables, but it is almost always a way of describing two weak syllables inside a larger foot rather than a foot in its own right. The names matter less than the habit they train: a reader who can hear an iamb can hear when a poet refuses one.

**Line lengths.** — Lines are named by how many feet they contain, another inheritance from Greek. Monometer has one foot; dimeter two; trimeter three; tetrameter four; pentameter five; hexameter six; heptameter seven; octameter eight. English practice concentrates on a few of these. Iambic tetrameter drives Wordsworth's "I wandered lonely as a cloud" and most English hymns and ballads. Iambic pentameter is the workhorse of the tradition: it carries Shakespeare's sonnets ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"), Pope's couplets, and the whole body of blank verse. Iambic hexameter, the alexandrine, is slower and more ceremonial; Pope mocked it in a couplet that performs its own complaint, "A needless alexandrine ends the song / That like a wounded snake, drags its slow length along." The old fourteener, an iambic heptameter, was the meter of sixteenth-century narrative verse before pentameter displaced it. Trochaic and anapestic lines are also common: Blake's "Tyger Tyger, burning bright" is trochaic tetrameter, and Clement Clarke Moore's "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (1823) is anapestic tetrameter, which is why it gallops. Dactylic hexameter, the meter of Homer and Virgil, fits English awkwardly, and Longfellow's *Evangeline* (1847) is the famous attempt: "This is the forest primeval. The murmuring pines and the hemlocks."

**Ballad meter, or common measure.** — One pattern deserves its own name because it runs through centuries of popular verse. Ballad meter, also called common measure, alternates iambic tetrameter with iambic trimeter and rhymes the second and fourth lines, an abcb scheme. John Newton's "Amazing Grace" is the standard example: "Amazing grace, how sweet the sound / That saved a wretch like me." The short third line pulls the stanza up, and the fourth settles it. The same measure underlies ballads, hymns, and much of Emily Dickinson, who loosened it with dashes and slant rhymes until it could hold an entire private cosmology.

**Scansion, and why two readers disagree.** — Scansion is the practice of marking a line's meter, usually with a breve or "u" for an unstressed syllable, a slash for a stress, and a vertical stroke between feet. Scan "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" and the pattern is clean: shall I | comPARE | thee TO | a SUM- | mer's DAY. But scansion is not a science, and honest readers scan the same line differently. Stress is relative, not absolute: "the" is weak in "the sun" and can be promoted in a phrase like "the very sun." A pair of stresses can be called a spondee or split into a trochee and an iamb depending on where the scanner draws the boundary. Elision, the squeezing of two syllables into one ("th' ambitious," "o'er"), lets a poet keep the count while the spelling suggests more. The disagreements are not a failure of the method; they are evidence that meter is a contract between poet and reader, and the contract is always being renegotiated. Paul Fussell, in *Poetic Meter and Poetic Form* (1965; revised 1979), argued that this contract is what makes meter meaningful: because the reader expects a pattern, a poet can make meaning by fulfilling the expectation, delaying it, or breaking it. Derek Attridge, in *The Rhythms of English Poetry* (1982), went further and argued that the foot itself is a misleading fiction, an import from Greek that obscures the real rhythm of English, which is better described as a sequence of beats and offbeats in a stress-timed language. The two positions are still argued, and they are not merely technical: they decide whether a substitution is an event in the poem or a quirk of the description.

**Variation: the pattern against the voice.** — No good poet writes a metronome. The interest of iambic pentameter lies in how it is bent. The commonest variations have names. Initial inversion replaces the first iamb with a trochee, as in Keats's "SEA-son of mists and mellow fruitfulness," which opens by leaning on the noun. A feminine ending adds an unstressed syllable after the fifth stress, so the line ends on a falling, unclosed note; "To be, or not to be, that is the question" is a pentameter with a feminine ending, and the extra syllable on "question" is part of why the line sounds unresolved. A masculine ending closes on the stress. The caesura is a pause inside the line, often marked "||," and its position controls the line's gait: "Know then thyself, || presume not God to scan" divides at the centre and balances the two halves. Enjambment runs the sense past the line break, so that the metrical pause and the syntactic pause fall in different places; this is the engine of Milton's long paragraphs and of Shakespeare's most agitated speeches. The reverse, the end-stopped line, closes both at once. Gerard Manley Hopkins (1844–1889) pushed variation to a system he called "sprung rhythm," in which the poet counts stresses but not the unstressed syllables between them, so that the line can lurch and spring. Traditional prosody treats such effects as substitutions within a pattern; Attridge treats the pattern itself as a way of talking about beats. Either way, the effect is the same: the reader hears the norm and the departure at once, and the departure is where much of the expression lives.

## Rhyme, stanza, and the shapes of poems

**Rhyme at the line's end.** — Rhyme is the matching of sounds, usually from the last stressed vowel to the end of the word. End rhyme places the match at the ends of lines, and a rhyme scheme is written in letters: abab means the first and third lines rhyme, the second and fourth. Rhyme pleases by repetition and by the slight surprise of the match; it also closes a unit, which is why rhymed couplets tend to sound finished and why a poet who wants to keep moving may avoid them. A masculine rhyme falls on a single stressed syllable ("day" / "way"); a feminine rhyme carries an unstressed syllable after the stress ("MEAS-ure" / "PLEAS-ure"); a triple rhyme stretches to three ("merrily" / "verily"). Rhyme can be exact or approximate, and the approximate kinds are as old as the exact ones.

**Rhyme inside, and rhyme that only looks like rhyme.** — Internal rhyme puts the match inside a line rather than at its end. Coleridge's "The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew, / The furrow followed free" is a braid of internal and end rhyme, and the sound enacts the speed of the ship. Edgar Allan Poe's "Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary" rhymes "dreary" with "weary" before the line is over. Slant rhyme, also called near rhyme or off rhyme, matches some but not all of the sound: Emily Dickinson rhymes "soul" with "all" and "me" with "Immortality," and Wilfred Owen built his war poems on pararhyme, where the consonants agree and the vowels do not ("strange" / "strong," "killed" / "cold"). Eye rhyme matches the spelling but not the sound. Christopher Marlowe's "Come live with me and be my love, / And we will all the pleasures prove" pairs "love" and "prove," which looked like a rhyme to later readers long after the pronunciation had drifted; "stone" / "none" and "wind" (air) / "mind" are the same trick. Eye rhyme is a fossil of sound change, and it is a reminder that a reader hears a poem with an accent the poet may not have shared.

**The stanza.** — A stanza is a group of lines arranged in a repeating pattern of length, rhyme, and sometimes refrain. The couplet is the smallest common unit; the closed heroic couplet, two rhymed iambic pentameters with the sense completed at the second line, was perfected by Dryden and Pope. Pope's "Know then thyself, presume not God to scan; / The proper study of mankind is man" shows the form's gift for aphorism. The quatrain is the commonest stanza in English, and its rhyme schemes range from the ballad's abcb to the cross-rhymed abab of Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" (1751). Larger forms are often imports. Rhyme royal, a seven-line iambic-pentameter stanza rhyming ababbcc, was used by Chaucer in *Troilus and Criseyde* and later by Shakespeare in *The Rape of Lucrece*. Terza rima, Dante's interlocking aba bcb cdc, was adapted into English by Wyatt and later by Shelley in "Ode to the West Wind." Ottava rima, eight lines of abababcc, came from Italian romance and carried Byron's *Don Juan*. The Spenserian stanza, invented by Edmund Spenser for *The Faerie Queene* (1590–96), has nine lines, the first eight iambic pentameter and the ninth an alexandrine, rhyming ababbcbcc; the long final line draws the stanza to a close like a held breath. Tennyson's *In Memoriam A.H.H.* (1850) used a stanza of its own name, four iambic tetrameters rhyming abba, and the enclosing rhyme gives the quatrain its slow, circular grief.

**The sonnet.** — The most durable stanza in English is fourteen lines of iambic pentameter. The Petrarchan or Italian sonnet divides into an octave (abbaabba) and a sestet (often cdecde), and the division is usually a movement from problem to answer, or from statement to turn. Sir Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, brought the form into English in the 1530s. Surrey's version, developed by Shakespeare, rearranges it into three quatrains and a final couplet, rhyming abab cdcd efef gg; the couplet becomes a hinge where the poem turns or sums up. Spenser's variant links the quatrains with interlocking rhyme, abab bcbc cdcd ee. Shakespeare's Sonnet 18, "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?", is the familiar English shape, and its closing couplet is the turn the form trains a reader to expect.

## Blank verse

**Unrhymed pentameter.** — Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter. It was introduced into English by Surrey, whose translation of Books II and IV of Virgil's *Aeneid* was published in the 1550s, and it reached the stage in *Gorboduc* (1561), the first English tragedy written in the meter. Christopher Marlowe made it the instrument of the public theatre, and Ben Jonson, in his elegy for Shakespeare printed in the 1623 First Folio, called it "Marlowe's mighty line." Shakespeare inherited it and stretched it: he varied the pause, added feminine endings, broke lines in two when a thought failed, and let a speech run for thirty lines without a full stop. The two soliloquies in *Hamlet* show the range. "O what a rogue and peasant slave am I!" begins in regular iambic and then breaks into short exclamations ("For Hecuba?"), lists, and a final couplet, so that the meter registers the mind's disorder. "To be, or not to be" moves more evenly, and its pauses fall where the thought hesitates.

**Milton's verse paragraph.** — John Milton chose blank verse for *Paradise Lost* (1667) because, as he wrote in a note on the verse, rhyme was "the troublesome and modern bondage of rhyming" and no necessary ornament of a long poem. Where earlier blank verse tended to move line by line, Milton built paragraphs. He placed the caesura late or early, ran the sense across line endings, and suspended the verb until the end of a long clause, so that the verse enacts the sweep and delay of the argument. The opening is the model: "Of Man's first disobedience, and the fruit / Of that forbidden tree, whose mortal taste / Brought death into the world, and all our woe" — the syntax spills past two line breaks before it reaches its verb. Milton's practice taught later poets that blank verse could be an epic instrument, not merely a dramatic one.

**Blank verse after Milton.** — Rhyme returned to fashion after the Restoration, and Dryden's heroic couplets dominated satire and argument, but blank verse never died. It suited the meditative poem and the long narrative. Wordsworth wrote *Tintern Abbey* (1798) and *The Prelude* (published 1850) in it, using it for the quiet accumulation of reflection. Tennyson used it for *Idylls of the King*, and Robert Frost, in the twentieth century, made it sound like New England speech in "Mending Wall" (1914) and "Birches" (1916). The meter's persistence is easy to explain: it is close enough to the rhythms of English speech to seem natural, and regular enough to be heard as verse; it offers the line as a unit without the closure of rhyme. It is the form a poet chooses when the argument is long and the voice must keep talking.

## Free verse

**Whitman's long line.** — Walt Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* (1855) broke with the metrical tradition. "Song of Myself" has no repeating foot, no rhyme scheme, and no fixed line length. Its lines are long, often longer than a breath, and they are held together by other means: by repetition and parallelism ("You shall possess ... You shall no longer ... You shall not ... You shall listen"), by the catalogue, by the sweep of a single sentence across several lines, and by the line break itself, which falls where Whitman wants the reader to pause. The rhythm is not absent; it is carried by syntax and breath rather than by a scansion of feet. Whitman's example mattered because it showed that a poem could be rhythmical without being measurable, and that the long line could hold a democratic sweep that the closed couplet could not.

**What replaced meter.** — The French *vers libre* of the 1880s, associated with Gustave Kahn and Jules Laforgue, gave the movement a name. In English the term "free verse" was taken up by the Imagists around 1912–14, and poets such as Ezra Pound, H.D., and Amy Lowell wrote short, unrhymed, cadenced lines that trusted the image and the line break. Pound's "In a Station of the Metro" (1913) is two lines long. T. S. Eliot's "Reflections on Vers Libre" (1917) argued that the phrase was a misnomer — there is no verse without some rhythm, only a rhythm too simple to scan — and his own work mixed metrical and unmeasured passages. William Carlos Williams later proposed the "variable foot," a three-part line in which each part is measured by the ear rather than by syllables; "The Red Wheelbarrow" (1923) is the famous example. What replaced meter, then, was not the absence of rhythm but a different set of organizing devices: the line as a unit of attention, the cadence of the sentence, repetition, and the tension between where the syntax wants to stop and where the line actually breaks.

**The argument over free verse.** — Whether free verse is verse at all has been argued since it appeared. Robert Frost compared writing it to playing tennis without a net, meaning that it removed the constraint that made the game worth watching. Charles O. Hartman, in *Free Verse: An Essay on Prosody* (1980), answered that free verse has a prosody of its own, built on the line break and the interaction of syntax with lineation, and that a reader can learn to hear it. Marjorie Perloff, in *The Poetics of Indeterminacy* (1981), traced how modern poets used the open line to resist closure itself. The debate is unresolved because the two sides want different things from the word "prosody": one wants a system of measurement, the other a description of how a poem moves.

## How rhythm carries meaning

**The sound an echo to the sense.** — The oldest claim about meter is that it can imitate the thing it describes. Alexander Pope made the claim a doctrine in *An Essay on Criticism* (1711): "The sound must seem an echo to the sense." He then demonstrated it in a couplet about the effort of hurling a rock and the speed of a runner — "When Ajax strives some rock's vast weight to throw, / The line too labours, and the words move slow; / Not so, when swift Camilla scours the plain, / Flies o'er th' unbending corn, and skims along the main." The heavy stresses slow the first line; the light ones hurry the third. Tennyson's "The Charge of the Light Brigade" is the standard later case. Its falling rhythm and short, broken lines are usually heard as hoofbeats and headlong motion, and the refrain "Rode the six hundred" returns like a count of the dead. The pattern does not merely accompany the meaning; a reader who heard the poem read in a different rhythm would hear a different poem.

**Where rhythm argues with the sense.** — The effect is often subtler than imitation. A feminine ending leaves a line unclosed, so a speech of doubt can end on a syllable that refuses to settle. A caesura placed against the sense breaks a thought where the meter does not expect it, and the reader feels the strain. Enjambment sets the metrical pause and the syntactic pause at odds, so that the line break interrupts a phrase and the sense runs on; Milton used the device to keep a reader suspended across a whole paragraph, and Whitman used the line break to isolate a single image or clause. Repetition, whether of a foot, a phrase, or a refrain, creates expectation, and the satisfaction or defeat of expectation is itself a kind of meaning. Rhythm also carries feeling that the words alone would not: the iambic drum of a hymn consoles by its regularity, and the broken meter of a grief-stricken lyric unsettles by refusing to console.

**The contested part.** — How much of this is in the poem and how much is supplied by the reader is disputed. W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley, in "The Concept of Meter" (1959), argued that meter is an abstract pattern and that the attempt to scan the sound of actual speech confuses two different things; on their account the pattern is real but the "expressive" effects are inferred. Morris Halle and Samuel Jay Keyser, in work beginning in 1966, tried to derive the rules that generate metrical lines, treating meter as a grammar. Attridge's beat theory and Fussell's account of the metrical contract both assume that the reader's expectation is part of the poem's machinery. Helen Vendler, in *The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets* (1997), reads the sonnets line by line and treats every metrical and rhyming choice as a move in an argument. The disagreement is not about whether rhythm matters — everyone agrees it does — but about where the meaning resides: in the pattern, in the performance, or in the transaction between them. That question is why scansion is worth learning. A reader who can mark a line can also ask what the marking leaves out, and the gap between the diagram and the voice is where most of the poetry is.

## Chronology

- **c. 1380s** — Chaucer writes *Troilus and Criseyde* in rhyme royal, ababbcc.
- **1530s** — Sir Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, introduce the sonnet into English; Surrey begins blank verse with his *Aeneid*.
- **1557** — Tottel's *Miscellany* prints Wyatt's and Surrey's poems, spreading both forms.
- **1561** — *Gorboduc*, the first English tragedy in blank verse, is performed.
- **c. 1587** — Marlowe's *Tamburlaine* establishes blank verse as the medium of the public stage.
- **1590–96** — Spenser publishes *The Faerie Queene* in the nine-line Spenserian stanza.
- **c. 1600–1601** — Shakespeare writes *Hamlet*; his soliloquies show blank verse bent to the movement of thought.
- **1667** — Milton publishes *Paradise Lost* in blank verse, with a note defending unrhymed verse.
- **1711** — Pope's *An Essay on Criticism* declares that "the sound must seem an echo to the sense."
- **1798** — Wordsworth and Coleridge publish *Lyrical Ballads*; Wordsworth's blank verse and Coleridge's rhymed ballad stand side by side.
- **1823** — Moore's "A Visit from St. Nicholas" popularises anapestic tetrameter.
- **1842** — Tennyson publishes "Break, Break, Break" and other poems.
- **1850** — Tennyson's *In Memoriam* uses the abba quatrain; Wordsworth's *The Prelude* appears in blank verse.
- **1854–55** — Tennyson writes and revises "The Charge of the Light Brigade."
- **1855** — Whitman publishes *Leaves of Grass*, including "Song of Myself."
- **1880s** — French *vers libre* is named and practised by Kahn, Laforgue, and others.
- **1912–14** — The Imagists adopt the term "free verse" and write short unrhymed cadenced poems.
- **1917** — Eliot publishes "Reflections on Vers Libre."
- **1923** — Williams publishes "The Red Wheelbarrow," an early "variable foot" poem.

## Questions to carry

- Where does a poem's meter end and its spoken rhythm begin, and can a reader ever tell the two apart?
- Why did unrhymed iambic pentameter become the standard line for English drama and epic, and what did it let writers do that rhyme did not?
- If free verse abandons the foot, what holds a free-verse line together, and is that enough to call it verse at all?
- How much of what a reader hears as expressive rhythm is in the poem, and how much is supplied by the reader's expectation?
- What is lost when a line is "regularised" in editing or translation, and what does the loss reveal about what the meter was doing?
