# Water, rivers, and oceans

**Region:** The whole earth

**A river that reaches the sea, and one that does not.** — The Amazon pours about 209,000 cubic metres of water into the Atlantic every second, roughly a fifth of all the fresh water that runs off the land into the world ocean. The Colorado, in the dry American West, is usually taken dry by dams and diversions before it reaches the Gulf of California. Both are water obeying gravity; their fates differ because of what people have built at their banks. The question running through this unit is how moving water makes and remakes the land.

## The water cycle and the world's water budget

**One ocean, one cycle.** — Nearly all water on Earth is salt water in the sea: about 1.335 billion of the roughly 1.386 billion cubic kilometres that exist, or 96.5 per cent. Fresh water is the other 2.5 per cent, and most of it is unavailable too: about 68.7 per cent of the fresh water is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers and another 30 per cent lies underground. Less than one per cent sits in lakes, rivers, and swamps, and the atmosphere holds about 0.001 per cent. The cycle is powered by the sun: solar energy evaporates water, the vapour rises, cools, and condenses, and rain or snow falls back, returning to the sea through rivers or seeping into the ground. The ocean supplies about 86 per cent of evaporation, but land receives more rain than it gives up, and the difference — the runoff — feeds every river on Earth.

**The budget, and residence times.** — Across the planet, precipitation and evaporation balance. The useful numbers are residence times: how long a molecule stays in one place. In the atmosphere, about nine days; in a river, days to weeks; in shallow groundwater, decades to centuries; in deep aquifers, thousands of years; in the ocean, about three thousand years; in the ice sheets, tens of thousands. A pollutant in a river is flushed within weeks; the same pollutant in a deep aquifer may remain for millennia.

## Drainage basins and watersheds

**The basin.** — A river drains a catchment, or drainage basin: the land from which water flows to it, bounded by a divide, the ridge separating one basin from the next. The Mississippi basin covers about 3.2 million square kilometres and drains all or part of thirty-one American states. The Continental Divide runs along the Rockies and separates rivers reaching the Pacific from those reaching the Atlantic and Arctic.

**Stream order.** — Geomorphologists order streams by their tributaries. A headwater with none is first order; two first-order streams join into a second-order channel; two second-order streams into a third. Low-order channels make up most of a network's length but carry little water; the high-order trunk carries most. Robert E. Horton set out the principles in 1945 and Arthur Strahler refined the ordering in 1952. Floods, sediment, and pollution move down the network from the small channels.

**Rivers as boundaries, rivers as lifelines.** — Because a river is a natural line across the land, people have used it as a border. The Rio Grande forms about two thousand kilometres of the United States–Mexico boundary; the Danube touches ten countries and four capitals; the Mekong passes through six. A river border is convenient and unstable, because rivers move. When the Rio Grande shifted at El Paso in the 1860s, it moved land between countries, and the Chamizal dispute took a century to settle. Treaties often fix the line at the thalweg, the deepest channel, and distinguish slow erosion, which moves the boundary, from sudden avulsion, which does not. Rivers are also lifelines. The Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus, and the Yellow River each watered an early dense farming society, and the rhythm of their floods set the calendar of the state. Egypt still takes almost all its water from the Nile.

## The work of rivers

**Erosion.** — A river cuts by hydraulic action, the force of water prising rock apart; by abrasion, the grinding of its load against the bed; and by solution, the chemical removal of soluble rock. It cuts headward and vertically into the bed, until the channel nears base level. Where hard rock follows soft, the bed steps down and a waterfall forms. Niagara Falls, about fifty-one metres high, is the lip of the Niagara Escarpment and has retreated upstream for twelve thousand years as the softer rock beneath is undermined. A gorge is a deep, narrow valley cut where a river keeps downcutting as the land rises. The Grand Canyon, cut by the Colorado, is 446 kilometres long and up to 1.8 kilometres deep; the river began carving it perhaps five to six million years ago, though the dating is contested.

**Transport.** — A river's load travels as dissolved load in solution, suspended load of silt and clay, and bedload of sand, gravel, and boulders rolled along the bed. Which particles move, and whether they are eroded or deposited, depends on the water's speed; the Swedish geographer Filip Hjulström mapped the relationship in 1935 in the curve that still bears his name. The Yellow River carries more sediment than any other, on the order of a billion tonnes a year, washed from the loess plateau it crosses; its bed has risen above the surrounding plain, and it has changed course many times — the reason it is called "China's Sorrow."

**Deposition, meanders, and floodplains.** — Where a river slows, it drops its load. The finest sediment settles on the floodplain, the flat land the river covers when it floods, building rich alluvium. A lowland river wanders: the outer bank of a bend, where water runs fastest, is cut away as the cut bank, while the inner bank collects a point bar of sand and gravel. The bend migrates outward and downstream into broad meanders, and when a meander is cut through at its neck the abandoned loop becomes an oxbow lake. On the lower Mississippi, engineers cut off meanders deliberately in the 1930s to shorten the navigation channel.

**Deltas.** — Where a river meets standing water, it slows abruptly and dumps its load, building a delta — a fan of sediment named for the triangular Greek letter because the Nile's distributaries recalled its shape. Deltas are among the most fertile and densely settled lands on Earth. Their shape varies with the balance of river, waves, and tide. A delta exists only while the river supplies sediment. If dams trap the sediment upstream, the delta sinks and erodes; the Mississippi delta has lost roughly 4,900 square kilometres of land since the 1930s.

**The graded river, and a debate about it.** — A river tends toward a form in which it can just move the water and sediment delivered to it — a graded, or equilibrium, profile. William Morris Davis argued in 1899 for a "geographical cycle," in which a landscape passed once through youth, maturity, and old age. Grove Karl Gilbert and, later, Luna Leopold rejected that one-way story. In *Fluvial Processes in Geomorphology* (1964), Leopold, M. Gordon Wolman, and John P. Miller described the river as a self-adjusting system: discharge, slope, width, depth, velocity, and sediment load are linked, and a change in one produces compensating changes in the others. How far a river is ever truly in balance remains an active argument.

## Flooding and flood control

**The flood.** — Every river floods. Bankfull discharge just fills the channel to the top of its banks and does most of the work of moving sediment and shaping the channel; on many rivers it recurs every year or two. Larger floods are described by recurrence interval: a "hundred-year flood" has a one per cent chance of occurring in any given year. The Yellow River's floods have killed in the hundreds of thousands and more; the 1927 flood of the lower Mississippi covered some seventy thousand square kilometres and broke the policy of relying on levees alone.

**Levees and their cost.** — A levee does not remove a flood; it confines it. Water that would have spread across the floodplain is held between walls, so it runs higher and faster, and when a levee breaks the water enters suddenly and deeply. The American geographer Gilbert F. White argued in *Human Adjustment to Floods* (1945) that flood-control works encourage people to build on land that floods, so each round of protection raises the eventual loss — the "levee effect." Modern management tries to make room for the river by setting levees back and restoring wetlands. The Mississippi's Birds Point–New Madrid Floodway, designed in the 1920s, was opened in 2011 to spare Cairo, Illinois, at the cost of flooding farmland in Missouri.

**Dams.** — A dam stores the flood and releases it in the dry season, generates electricity, and supplies cities and irrigation. It also traps the sediment that would have built the delta and replenished the floodplain, and blocks fish that migrate upriver to spawn. The Hoover Dam on the Colorado, built 1931–1936 and 221 metres high, created Lake Mead, the largest reservoir in the United States, and made Las Vegas, Phoenix, and Los Angeles possible. The Aswan High Dam on the Nile, completed in 1970, ended the annual flood and trapped almost all the river's sediment; without it the Nile delta is retreating and the eastern Mediterranean sardine fishery collapsed. The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze, completed in 2012 and the largest power station in the world at 22,500 megawatts, displaced more than a million people. Every large dam is a bargain in which benefits are immediate and costs spread over decades and downstream.

## Groundwater and aquifers

**Water below the ground.** — Rain that does not run off soaks into soil and rock, filling pore spaces. The top of the saturated zone is the water table; below it, an aquifer is rock or sediment porous and permeable enough to yield water in useful quantity. Recharge is the rate at which an aquifer refills, and it is usually far slower than the rate at which people pump. That mismatch is the central fact of groundwater.

**Mining the Ogallala.** — The Ogallala, or High Plains, aquifer underlies about 450,000 square kilometres from South Dakota to Texas. It made possible the wheat and cattle economy of the American High Plains after the Second World War, but recharge there is only about a centimetre a year in places, while pumping for irrigation has lowered the water table by tens of metres across wide areas. The aquifer is being mined, not used, and parts of Texas and Kansas will soon be unable to irrigate. The Nubian Sandstone aquifer beneath the eastern Sahara holds fossil water that fell in wetter ages; Libya pipes it to the coast in the Great Man-Made River.

**Subsidence and salt.** — Removing groundwater can make the ground itself sink as emptied pore spaces compact. Mexico City, built on the drained bed of Lake Texcoco, has sunk several metres in a century and parts continue to drop by tens of centimetres a year, cracking buildings and distorting sewers. On coasts, pumping can pull salt water inland into the freshwater lens that floats on denser sea water, poisoning wells. In Jakarta, subsidence and salt intrusion together are among the reasons Indonesia decided to move its capital.

## Lakes and inland seas

**How a lake is made, and how it ends.** — A lake is standing water in a basin with no outlet to the sea, or too small an outlet to drain it quickly. Basins form by tectonics, glaciers, volcanic craters, meanders, and by people who build reservoirs. A lake traps sediment and eventually fills, so most are geologically young. The Great Lakes hold about twenty-one per cent of the world's surface fresh water. Where a basin has no outlet at all — an endorheic basin — water leaves only by evaporation, and salts concentrate: the Dead Sea, the Great Salt Lake, and the Caspian Sea are of this kind.

**The Aral Sea.** — The clearest lesson in diversion is the Aral Sea in Central Asia. Once the fourth-largest lake, about 68,000 square kilometres, it was fed by the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya. From the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted those rivers to irrigate cotton, and the lake shrank. By the 2000s it had lost about ninety per cent of its volume and split into basins; the fishing town of Moynaq now sits beside a dry seabed littered with rusting trawlers. The northern part has partly recovered since a dam was built in 2005, but the larger southern sea remains a salty remnant. The Aral is the standard case of a water body destroyed by irrigation upstream.

## The oceans

**Salinity and temperature.** — Sea water is about 3.5 per cent dissolved salt, a salinity of roughly 35 parts per thousand. The proportions of the major dissolved ions are nearly constant everywhere — a principle established by the Swiss chemist Alexander Marcet in 1819 and confirmed by William Dittmar's analyses of *Challenger* samples in the 1870s. Temperature falls with depth: a warm surface layer, then a rapid drop called the thermocline, then cold deep water close to zero to four degrees Celsius.

**Currents.** — Surface currents are driven mainly by winds, bent by the Earth's rotation — the Coriolis effect — into great circular gyres. The Gulf Stream, which the American naval officer Matthew Fontaine Maury called "a river in the ocean" in *The Physical Geography of the Sea* (1855), carries warm water north-east across the Atlantic and keeps north-western Europe milder than its latitude would suggest. Beneath the surface runs the thermohaline circulation, a slow overturning driven by differences in temperature and salt: cold, salty water sinks in the North Atlantic and creeps through the deep ocean for a thousand years before welling up. The American oceanographer Wallace Broecker called this the "great ocean conveyor" in 1987. Where winds push surface water away from a coast, deep, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it — upwelling — and these zones are among the world's richest fisheries. When the Peruvian upwelling fails, the result is El Niño.

**Tides and waves.** — Tides are the rise and fall of the sea under the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser degree, the Sun. Because the Earth turns beneath them, most coasts have two high and two low tides a day. When Sun and Moon pull in line, at new and full moon, the range is greatest (spring tides); when they pull at right angles, least (neap tides). In a few funnel-shaped bays the tide resonates and grows enormous: the Bay of Fundy has a range of up to sixteen metres. Waves are raised by wind blowing across open water; their size depends on wind speed, duration, and fetch, the distance of open water. A wave is a moving shape, not moving water, until it reaches shallow water and breaks. Tsunamis are different, caused by undersea earthquakes, landslides, or eruptions, low and fast in the deep ocean and rising into destructive walls only at the shore.

**The deep sea and the mid-ocean ridges.** — The ocean floor is not flat. Through it runs the longest mountain range on Earth, the mid-ocean ridge system, some sixty-five thousand kilometres long, where new sea floor forms as plates pull apart. The American geologist Harry Hess proposed in 1962 that the ocean floor spreads from these ridges and is consumed at trenches, and the idea became part of plate tectonics. In 1977, scientists in the submersible *Alvin* found hydrothermal vents on the Galápagos Rift, where superheated mineral-rich water pours from the floor and supports animals — tube worms, crabs, clams — that live not on sunlight but on chemical energy. The deepest place known is the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, nearly eleven kilometres down. The ocean covers about seventy-one per cent of the surface and absorbs much of the carbon dioxide human activity releases, slowly making sea water more acidic.

## The cryosphere and glaciers

**Where the ice is.** — The cryosphere is the frozen part of the Earth: the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland, polar sea ice, mountain glaciers, snow cover, and permafrost. Antarctica's ice sheet covers about fourteen million square kilometres and holds some sixty per cent of all fresh water; Greenland's holds most of the rest. Sea ice floats, but the ice sheets rest on land, and their loss raises sea level.

**Glaciers as rivers of ice.** — A glacier is ice thick enough to flow under its own weight. It forms where snow accumulates faster than it melts; snow compacts into firn and then ice, and the ice creeps downhill. The upper accumulation zone gains mass, the lower ablation zone loses it, and the line between them is the equilibrium line. Ice is a powerful agent of erosion: it plucks and abrades rock, carries debris as moraine, and leaves sharp ridges, bowl-shaped cirques, and U-shaped valleys. When a glacial valley is drowned by the sea it becomes a fjord, like the deep inlets of Norway, Chile, and New Zealand. The largest glacier on Earth is the Lambert Glacier in East Antarctica, some four hundred kilometres long.

**Water towers.** — For hundreds of millions of people, glaciers are a reservoir. The rivers rising in the Hindu Kush and the Himalaya — the Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra, Mekong, and Yangtze — are fed in the dry season by meltwater from snow and ice, and the mountains are called the water tower of Asia. As the climate warms, most glaciers retreat; the Alps have lost about half their ice since 1900, and Kilimanjaro's ice cap has shrunk by more than eighty per cent since 1912. In the short run, melting increases river flow; once the ice is gone, flow falls. The point at which meltwater supply begins to decline is sometimes called "peak water." Melting glaciers also create lakes dammed by unstable moraine, which can burst in a glacial lake outburst flood; one such flood destroyed the Peruvian town of Huaraz in 1941.

**Ice ages.** — The Serbian mathematician Milutin Milankovitch argued in the 1920s and 1940s that variations in the Earth's orbit change the distribution of sunlight and pace the ice ages. At the height of the last glaciation, about twenty thousand years ago, ice sheets covered much of northern Europe and North America, and so much water was locked up that sea level was about 120 metres lower; Britain was joined to the continent. The ice left the Great Lakes, the fjords, the moraines, and the fertile loess soils of Europe and China. The shape of the land cannot be understood without water in its frozen form.

## Water scarcity and the great dams

**Scarcity.** — Fresh water is renewable only at the rate the cycle delivers it, and in many places people use it faster. A common rough measure is that a country with less than 1,000 cubic metres of renewable fresh water per person per year faces scarcity. The Colorado, the Yellow, the Indus, and the Nile are all heavily or fully used; several now fail to reach the sea in normal years. The causes are population, irrigation, industry, and diet — it takes far more water to grow a kilogram of meat than a kilogram of grain.

**The great dams, and the arguments about them.** — The twentieth century was the age of the great dam. Hoover Dam tamed the Colorado; the Aswan High Dam ended the Nile flood; the Three Gorges Dam became the largest power station on Earth. These works are the subject of a long argument. The German-American scholar Karl Wittfogel, in *Oriental Despotism* (1957), proposed the "hydraulic hypothesis": that the need to organize large irrigation works in dry lands produced centralized, coercive states. The archaeologist Robert McC. Adams, in *Heartland of Cities* (1981), replied that Mesopotamian irrigation was often small-scale and locally managed, and that Wittfogel read the centralized states of his own time back into antiquity; the hypothesis is now generally thought too simple. The American writer Marc Reisner, in *Cadillac Desert* (1986), argued that the great dams of the American West were built by a federal bureaucracy that had to keep building to survive. Tree-ring research later showed that the Colorado Compact of 1922 was negotiated in an unusually wet period, and the reservoirs of the Colorado have been falling for decades. Wittfogel and Reisner disagree about almost everything except this: a dam is never only an engineering work. It is also a claim on a river, and on everyone downstream.

**Treaties and the rivers that cross borders.** — Because rivers cross borders, they force neighbours to negotiate. The Colorado River Compact of 1922 divided the river between an upper and a lower basin and, by assuming more water than the river carries, set up a century of dispute. The Nile Waters Agreement of 1959 gave Egypt and Sudan almost the whole flow and left upstream states out; the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, begun on the Blue Nile in 2011, has revived the quarrel. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, divided the rivers between India and Pakistan and has survived wars.

**Rivers as lifelines.** — For all the engineering, the oldest fact about rivers remains: people live by them. The Ganges is both a water supply for hundreds of millions and, at Varanasi and elsewhere, a river so polluted that bathing in it is a hazard. The Yellow River ran dry at its mouth in most years between 1972 and 1999, including 226 days in 1997, before an allocation scheme restored some flow. The Colorado, the rivers that fed the Aral, and the Rio Grande all show the same pattern: a river can be taken apart by the people who depend on it. Whether the future holds more dams, fewer, or a different kind of management, the question stays the one this unit began with — how moving water makes and remakes the land, and what happens when people decide to make it move somewhere else.

## Chronology

- **c. 3000 BCE** — Egyptian records measure the Nile flood; nilometers mark its height.
- **1687** — Newton's *Principia* explains the tides by gravitation.
- **1819** — Alexander Marcet shows the constant proportions of salts in sea water.
- **1855** — Matthew Fontaine Maury publishes *The Physical Geography of the Sea*.
- **1869** — John Wesley Powell's party runs the Colorado through the Grand Canyon.
- **1872–1876** — The *Challenger* expedition surveys the deep ocean.
- **1878** — Powell's *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region* proposes watershed government for the West.
- **1899** — W. M. Davis publishes the "geographical cycle."
- **1922** — The Colorado River Compact is signed at Santa Fe on 24 November.
- **1927** — The lower Mississippi floods; the "levees only" policy is abandoned.
- **1931–1936** — Hoover Dam is built on the Colorado.
- **1935** — Filip Hjulström publishes his curve of sediment transport.
- **1945** — Gilbert F. White's *Human Adjustment to Floods*; R. E. Horton on stream networks.
- **1957** — Karl Wittfogel publishes *Oriental Despotism*.
- **1959** — The Nile Waters Agreement divides the river between Egypt and Sudan.
- **1960** — The Indus Waters Treaty is signed; the Aswan High Dam is begun.
- **1962** — Harry Hess publishes his hypothesis of sea-floor spreading.
- **1964** — Leopold, Wolman, and Miller's *Fluvial Processes in Geomorphology*.
- **1970** — The Aswan High Dam is completed; the Nile flood ends.
- **1977** — Hydrothermal vents are discovered on the Galápagos Rift.
- **1986** — Marc Reisner's *Cadillac Desert*.
- **1987** — Wallace Broecker names the "great ocean conveyor."
- **1994–2012** — The Three Gorges Dam is built on the Yangtze.
- **2011** — Ethiopia begins the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam; the Mississippi floodway is opened.

## Questions to carry

- A river is a thin skin of water on a huge planet. Why does so small a share of the world's water do so much of the work of shaping the land?
- Leopold described rivers as self-adjusting systems; Davis described a one-way cycle of erosion. What would each expect if a dam were built across a river, and which prediction fits the record?
- The Colorado River Compact of 1922 assumed more water than the river carries. Was that an honest mistake, a political bargain, or a deliberate overestimate — and how does the tree-ring evidence bear on the answer?
- Wittfogel argued that control of water produces centralized power; Adams replied that irrigation is often local. Which reading does the evidence from Mesopotamia, Egypt, or the American West support, and where does it fail?
- If a river is also a border, a lifeline, and a carrier of sediment, whose claim on it should decide how it is used — and what happens to the delta when the answer is "the people upstream"?
