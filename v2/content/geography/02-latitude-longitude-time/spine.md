# Latitude, longitude, and time

**Region:** The whole earth · **Period:** c. 150 BCE–1923 CE

**Two numbers for every place.** — Every point on the surface of the earth can be fixed by two numbers. Latitude is the angle between a place and the equator, measured north or south from the centre of the earth: the equator is 0°, each pole is 90°, and a degree is divided into sixty minutes. Longitude is the angle between a place and some chosen meridian, measured east or west; it says how far round the world a place lies. Latitude is a fact of astronomy. A navigator who measures the height of the sun at noon, or the height of the pole star at night, and allows for the season, can find his latitude without knowing where he is or where he sailed from. Longitude is a fact of time. The earth turns through 360° in twenty-four hours, so 15° of longitude is one hour, 1° is four minutes, and one minute of arc is four seconds of time. A navigator who knows the local time and also the time at a fixed reference place can subtract the two and read off his longitude. Latitude is a measurement; longitude is a comparison. That asymmetry — latitude easy, longitude hard — shaped the whole history of finding a position at sea.

**The grid and its inventors.** — The idea of covering the earth with a grid of intersecting circles is usually credited to the Greek astronomer Hipparchus, working on Rhodes in the second century BCE, who used the positions of stars and records of eclipses to fix places by latitude and longitude. Claudius Ptolemy, writing his *Geography* at Alexandria about 150 CE, listed some eight thousand places with coordinates and drew his prime meridian through the Fortunate Islands — the Canaries, the westernmost land he knew. The grid he and Hipparchus used is the one still in service: parallels of latitude, which are true circles around the pole, and meridians of longitude, which are half-circles running from pole to pole. The only natural lines in the whole system are the poles and the equator, which are fixed by the earth's axis of rotation. Everything else is convention. The equator divides the globe into equal halves; a degree of latitude is almost the same length everywhere, about 111 kilometres, varying slightly because the earth bulges at the equator and flattens at the poles. A degree of longitude, by contrast, shrinks as you go north or south: it is about 111 kilometres at the equator and zero at the poles, because the meridians converge. Sailors turned that fact into a unit of distance. One minute of latitude is one nautical mile, 1,852 metres, and the nautical mile remains the ordinary measure of distance at sea.

**The tilt and the seasons.** — The earth's axis is not perpendicular to the plane of its orbit; it leans by about 23.44°, and it keeps pointing in the same direction as the earth goes round the sun. That single lean produces the seasons and the great circles of the tropics. The tropics are the two parallels where the sun stands directly overhead at a solstice: the Tropic of Cancer at 23.44° north, where the sun is overhead at the June solstice, and the Tropic of Capricorn at 23.44° south, where it is overhead at the December solstice. The polar circles lie at 66.56° north and south, the complement of the tilt, and mark the latitudes where the sun does not set at the summer solstice and does not rise at the winter solstice. Between the tropics the sun passes overhead twice a year; within the polar circles there is at least one day of continuous daylight and one of continuous darkness. The noon height of the sun depends on both latitude and season. On the equinoxes the sun's declination is zero and noon altitude equals 90° minus the latitude; at other times the sun's declination must be added or subtracted. In the northern hemisphere, with the sun to the south, latitude equals 90° minus the observed altitude plus the declination. This is the arithmetic that turned the sun into a position line. The seasons are caused by the tilt and not by distance from the sun: the earth is in fact closest to the sun in early January, during the northern winter. The Greek geographer Eratosthenes, librarian at Alexandria about 240 BCE, used the summer solstice to measure the size of the earth. He knew that at Syene, near the first cataract of the Nile, the sun at noon on the solstice shone straight down a well, while at Alexandria, some five thousand stadia to the north, it stood about 7° from the vertical. Assuming the two places on the same meridian and the sun's rays parallel, he reckoned the circumference at about 250,000 stadia — within a few per cent of the modern figure, if the stadium he used was the one historians think it was. The result was a piece of reasoning from a shadow, and it stood for centuries.

**Reading the sky at sea.** — The oldest and simplest way to find latitude at sea is to measure the height of the pole star. The celestial pole stands above the horizon at an angle equal to the observer's latitude, and Polaris sits close enough to that pole to serve as a marker. It is not exactly on the pole, however: it circles the pole once a day in a small arc, so a correction is needed. In the 1520s the Italian chronicler Antonio Pigafetta, who sailed with Magellan, set down the rule. If the two Guard stars of the Little Bear stood on the western arm, the pole star was one degree above the pole; if on the line, three degrees thirty minutes below; if on the eastern arm, one degree below. The observed altitude was corrected accordingly. The sun offered a second method, available by day. A navigator measured the sun's altitude at local noon, looked up the sun's declination for that date in an almanac, and worked the sum. Pigafetta gave seven cases for the different combinations of hemisphere and season. The instruments that made these measurements were simple. The cross-staff, a graduated staff with a sliding cross-piece, measured the angle between the horizon and a heavenly body; the mariner's astrolabe, a heavy brass ring with an alidade, was used by Portuguese navigators from the late fifteenth century; the quadrant measured angles up to 90°. All of them required the observer to look at two things at once, the body and the horizon, and all suffered from the same difficulty: at sea the horizon is not steady, and the eye must judge a moving line. The backstaff, or Davis quadrant, invented in 1594, let the observer turn his back on the sun and read its shadow instead, which was easier and safer than staring at it. In 1731 John Hadley in England and Thomas Godfrey in America independently built the reflecting quadrant, which used two mirrors to bring the image of the sun or star down to the horizon and let the observer see both in the same field of view. Its descendant, the sextant, perfected in the 1750s, took its name from the 60° arc of its frame; because the angle is doubled by reflection, it could measure angles up to 120°, and a good instrument read to ten seconds of arc. The sextant did not solve longitude, but it made the lunar-distance method and the checking of chronometers possible, and it remained the navigator's chief instrument into the age of satellites. Alongside these instruments went dead reckoning: the ship's speed was estimated by throwing a log over the side on a line knotted at intervals and timing it with a half-minute glass, the course was taken from the compass, and the day's run was plotted on a chart. The compass itself was unreliable, because the magnetic pole is not the true pole; the angle between magnetic north and true north, the variation, had to be measured and allowed for, and it changed from place to place.

**The longitude problem.** — Latitude could be found from the sky, but longitude could not, because longitude is a difference of time. Every method that worked depended on comparing local time with the time at a known place. The first attempts used events in the sky that happen at the same instant everywhere: an eclipse of the moon, for instance, or the eclipses of Jupiter's moons, which Galileo proposed as a celestial clock in the 1610s. These were useless on a moving ship, because a telescope cannot be held steady at sea. The practical method of the eighteenth century was the lunar distance. The moon moves against the stars by about thirteen degrees a day, fast enough that its position is a kind of clock hand. A navigator measured the angle between the moon and the sun, or the moon and a bright star, at a known local time, then compared the measurement with tables giving the moon's position as seen from Greenwich at each moment. The difference gave the time at Greenwich, and the time difference gave the longitude. It was accurate enough to be useful and difficult enough to be hated. The calculations took hours, and an error in the tables or the arithmetic threw the result out. Nevil Maskelyne published the *Nautical Almanac* in 1767 to supply the tables, and the method became standard for decades. Its weakness was its cost in labour and its dependence on clear skies for both moon and horizon.

**The disaster and the prize.** — The cost of not knowing longitude was measured in ships. On the night of 22 October 1707 a Royal Navy fleet under Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell was returning from the Mediterranean and ran onto the rocks of the Isles of Scilly, off the south-west of England. Four ships were lost, including the flagship *Association*, and between 1,400 and 2,000 men drowned. The navigators believed they were clear of the islands; their dead reckoning had drifted, and the charts and compasses were faulty. The shock pushed Parliament to act. In July 1714 it passed the Longitude Act, which set up a Board of Longitude and offered a series of rewards: £10,000 for a method that could find longitude to within one degree, £15,000 for two-thirds of a degree, and £20,000 for half a degree — half a degree at the equator being thirty nautical miles. The Act did not say how the problem should be solved, only how accurately. Sir Isaac Newton, consulted on the scheme, doubted that a watch could ever keep time well enough at sea: "when longitude at sea is lost," he wrote, "it cannot be found again by any watch." The Board waited, and paid smaller sums for promising ideas.

**Harrison's clocks.** — The man who answered the Act was a Lincolnshire carpenter and clockmaker named John Harrison, born in 1693 at Foulby in Yorkshire and raised at Barrow upon Humber. He had taught himself horology and built wooden clocks of great precision before he turned to the sea. His first marine timekeeper, H1, was finished in 1735; it was a large machine of brass and wood with two connected balances, and it was tested on a voyage to Lisbon and back. H2 followed in 1741, then H3 in 1757, each an attempt to correct the flaws of the last. Then Harrison changed his approach. Instead of a great machine, he built a large watch. H4, completed in 1759, was about thirteen centimetres across and weighed some 1.45 kilograms, and it was a completely different kind of object: compact, portable, and accurate. In 1761 it was sent to Jamaica on HMS *Deptford* in the care of Harrison's son William. The voyage lasted eighty-one days, and when the ship reached Kingston the watch was found to be about five seconds slow — an error of roughly one nautical mile in longitude. The Board was not satisfied and demanded a second trial. In 1764 H4 sailed to Barbados on HMS *Tartar*, where it kept time to within about thirty-nine seconds, an error of less than ten miles. Maskelyne, who sailed on the same voyage to test the lunar method, achieved about thirty miles. The watch had plainly won. But the Board, now including Maskelyne as Astronomer Royal, argued that the accuracy might be luck and that a watch taking years to build was not practical. The dispute dragged on for a decade. Parliament paid Harrison £10,000 in 1765, on condition that he explain the watch and hand it over, and in 1773, after King George III had tested a later watch himself, it granted him a further £8,750. He received about £23,065 in all for his work, but never the full £20,000, which was never awarded to anyone. A copy of H4 made by Larcum Kendall, known as K1, sailed with Captain James Cook on his second and third voyages, and Cook's charts of the Pacific, laid down with its help, were among the most accurate yet drawn.

**The prime meridian and the date line.** — Longitude needs a zero, and for centuries there was none that everyone accepted. Ptolemy counted from the Canaries; French charts counted from Paris; British charts from Greenwich; other nations used Cadiz, Ferro, or their own observatories. A ship's position meant different numbers on different charts, and a difference of a few degrees could put a vessel on the wrong side of a reef. By the later nineteenth century the growth of railways, telegraphs, and steamships made the confusion intolerable, and in October 1884 an International Meridian Conference met in Washington at the invitation of the President of the United States. It adopted a series of resolutions. The first held that a single prime meridian should be adopted for all nations. The second proposed the meridian through the centre of the transit instrument at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich. The vote was twenty-two in favour, one against — San Domingo — with Brazil and France abstaining. Later resolutions counted longitude east and west from Greenwich up to 180°, proposed a universal day, fixed that day as a mean solar day beginning at midnight on the initial meridian and counted from zero to twenty-four hours, and expressed the hope that the astronomical and nautical days would also begin at midnight. France kept its own Paris time until 1911, and the meridian opposite Greenwich, 180°, became the ordinary place for the date to change. The International Date Line is not a straight line, because it must not cut a single country or island group in two. It bends east to pass around eastern Siberia and the Aleutians, swings far east to enclose Kiribati, and passes between Samoa and American Samoa. The line has been moved more than once for reasons of trade. The Philippines, reached from Mexico across the Pacific, lay on the American side of the line and so ran a day behind Asia until 1844, when the governor-general ordered that Tuesday, 31 December 1844 be removed from the calendar. Alaska changed sides when the United States bought it from Russia in 1867, and the transfer ceremony was dated Friday, 18 October 1867, repeating a day. Samoa, which had been on the American side since 1892, skipped Friday, 30 December 2011 to move to the western side and align its working week with Australia and New Zealand. Kiribati removed Saturday, 31 December 1994 so that its eastern Line Islands could share a date with the rest of the country. The result is that the islands of the Line Islands keep UTC+14, the earliest hour on earth, while Baker and Howland Islands, a few hundred miles away, keep UTC−12, the latest.

**Standard time and its distortions.** — Time zones are a nineteenth-century invention, and their first engineers were railway companies. Before the 1840s every town kept its own local time, set by the sun; noon in one town came minutes after noon in the next. That was harmless when travel was slow, but fatal when trains ran to a timetable on a single track. In November 1840 the Great Western Railway in Britain began running its trains on Greenwich time, and other companies followed; the practice was called railway time. In North America the problem was worse, because each railroad kept its own time and junctions served by several companies had a clock for each. On 18 November 1883, at the urging of the meteorologist Cleveland Abbe and the railway editor William F. Allen, the North American railroads divided the continent into standard zones and reset their clocks at the moment each zone reached noon. The day was nicknamed "the Day of Two Noons," because in many places the clock was turned back and noon struck twice. Within a year most large cities had adopted standard time, and Congress made it law in the Standard Time Act of 1918. The Canadian engineer Sandford Fleming had proposed a worldwide system of twenty-four zones in 1876, each fifteen degrees wide and one hour apart, and the idea became the basis of the world's time. But the zones were never purely geographical. A zone that followed the meridians exactly would cut through countries and cities, so the boundaries were bent to suit politics and commerce. China, which spans some sixty degrees of longitude, keeps a single zone, UTC+8, so that in the far west the sun reaches its highest point near three in the afternoon. India keeps UTC+5:30 and Nepal UTC+5:45, the only offsets of their kind. Newfoundland keeps UTC−3:30. Spain, though the prime meridian passes through it, keeps Central European Time, an inheritance of the Second World War, so that its western city of Vigo can see solar noon after two in the afternoon. Nome, in western Alaska, keeps a time more than two hours ahead of the sun. Daylight saving, first proposed in 1907 and adopted as a wartime measure in 1916, adds a further political hour to the summer. A time zone, in other words, is a decision, not a fact of nature.

**The day, the year, and the calendar.** — The units of time are geographical facts. The day is the earth's rotation. A sidereal day, measured against the stars, is 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds; the solar day, measured from one noon to the next, is about four minutes longer, because the earth must turn a little further to bring the sun back to the meridian while it moves along its orbit. The solar day is not quite constant either: the earth's orbit is elliptical and its axis tilted, so apparent solar time runs fast or slow against mean solar time by up to about sixteen minutes over the year. The difference is the equation of time, and it is the reason a sundial and a clock disagree. The year is the earth's orbit. The tropical year, from one March equinox to the next, is 365.2422 days, which is not a whole number, and no calendar can make it one. The Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar in 46 BCE, assumed a year of 365.25 days and added a leap day every fourth year. That overcounted by about eleven minutes a year, and by the sixteenth century the calendar had drifted ten days out of step with the equinoxes, which mattered because the date of Easter depended on the spring equinox. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII, acting on the work of the astronomer Aloysius Lilius and the mathematician Christopher Clavius, reformed the calendar. Ten days were dropped — Thursday, 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday, 15 October — and the leap rule was changed so that century years would be leap years only when divisible by 400. The Gregorian mean year is 365.2425 days, close enough that the error accumulates by only about a day in three thousand years. Protestant and Orthodox countries adopted the reform slowly, and the gap between the calendars widened. Britain and its colonies changed in 1752, dropping eleven days; Russia did not change until 1918, and Greece not until 1923. The calendar is thus a negotiated compromise between the earth's two motions, its spin and its orbit, and the date line is the seam where the compromise is stitched together. To fix a point on a featureless ocean, a navigator needed the stars for latitude, a clock for longitude, and a calendar to know what day it was — three ways of reading the same turning earth.

## Chronology

- **c. 240 BCE** — Eratosthenes measures the circumference of the earth from the solstice shadow at Syene and Alexandria.
- **c. 150 BCE** — Hipparchus sets out the grid of latitude and longitude and uses eclipse records to compare places.
- **c. 150 CE** — Ptolemy's *Geography* lists thousands of places with coordinates and a prime meridian through the Canaries.
- **c. 1524** — Pigafetta, who sailed with Magellan, writes a treatise on finding latitude by the pole star and the sun.
- **1594** — John Davis invents the backstaff, letting the navigator face away from the sun.
- **1707** — A Royal Navy fleet is wrecked on the Isles of Scilly; between 1,400 and 2,000 men die.
- **1714** — The Longitude Act sets up the Board of Longitude and offers up to £20,000 for a method accurate to half a degree.
- **1731** — Hadley and Godfrey independently build the reflecting quadrant, ancestor of the sextant.
- **1735–1759** — John Harrison completes the marine timekeepers H1, H2, H3, and finally the watch H4.
- **1761–1762** — H4 sails to Jamaica and keeps time to about five seconds over eighty-one days.
- **1764** — H4 is tested to Barbados; Maskelyne tests the lunar-distance method on the same voyage.
- **1767** — Maskelyne publishes the first *Nautical Almanac*, supplying the tables for lunar distances.
- **1773** — Parliament grants Harrison a further £8,750; the full prize is never awarded.
- **1772–1779** — Captain Cook carries Kendall's copy of H4, K1, on his second and third voyages.
- **1840** — The Great Western Railway runs its trains on Greenwich time, the beginning of railway time.
- **1876** — Sandford Fleming proposes a worldwide system of twenty-four hourly time zones.
- **1883** — North American railroads adopt standard time on 18 November, "the Day of Two Noons."
- **1884** — The International Meridian Conference adopts Greenwich as the prime meridian, 22 votes to 1.
- **1911** — France abandons Paris time for Greenwich.
- **1918** — The United States Congress makes standard time law in the Standard Time Act.
- **1923** — Greece becomes the last European state to adopt the Gregorian calendar for civil use.

## Questions to carry

- Latitude can be found from the sky alone, but longitude cannot. Why does the difference between the two turn on time?
- Pigafetta's rules for the pole star and the sun are arithmetic. What must a navigator already know, and what must he trust, before the arithmetic can work?
- Harrison's watch won its trials but not its prize. Was the Board of Longitude resisting a machine, a man, or a method?
- The 1884 conference chose Greenwich by a vote of 22 to 1, yet the world did not change at once. What does the delay say about how standards are made?
- The calendar and the time zone both impose order on the earth's motions. Where does geography end and politics begin?
