# Population and migration

**Region:** The whole earth

**Eight billion, and three questions.** — In November 2022 the United Nations put the number of living people at eight billion. It had taken the whole of human history to reach one billion, about 1804; two billion came by 1927, three by 1960, four by 1974, five by 1987, six by 1999, seven by 2011, eight by 2022. The annual rate of increase has been falling since the early 1960s, when it peaked near 2.1 per cent, and now stands below 1 per cent; the absolute number keeps rising all the same, because one per cent of eight billion is eighty million people. Whether the total levels off, and when, depends on births and deaths that no one can observe in advance, and on choices made in households no census enters. What makes a population grow, move, or stabilize is really three questions — how many are born, how many die, and who arrives and leaves — and each has a different answer at the scale of a village, a country, and the earth.

## Distribution: where the eight billion live

**The ecumene.** — Geographers use the Greek word ecumene for the part of the earth that people permanently inhabit. Roughly nine-tenths of the population occupies about one-tenth of the land, and the concentration follows three old rules: lowland beats highland, coast beats interior, and the middle latitudes of the northern hemisphere hold more than their share. The great clusters are East Asia, South Asia, Europe, and the eastern United States, with a newer and fast-growing cluster in West Africa and the Nile valley. The empty lands are the cold — Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland — the dry — the Sahara, Arabia, the Australian interior — and the high, such as Tibet and the Andes above the tree line.

**Density, and what it measures.** — Density is a ratio: people divided by land area. Bangladesh packs about 1,100 people into each square kilometre of its delta; Singapore, a city-state, holds more than 8,000; Mongolia holds about two, and Greenland fewer than one. But a country's average density can hide everything that matters, because people are not spread evenly. Egypt's average is about 110 per square kilometre, yet nearly the whole population lives along the Nile and its delta, on about 5 per cent of the land; the rest is desert. Canada's average is about four, but most Canadians live within a hundred miles of the United States border. A better measure for some purposes is physiological density — people per unit of arable land — which asks how many mouths each farmed hectare must feed, and which is far higher in Egypt than its raw density suggests.

**Concentration and its causes.** — The coastal share is striking: a large fraction of humanity lives within a few dozen kilometres of a sea or a navigable river, because water carries goods and people cheaply. The Chinese population is often described as living along a line drawn from Heihe to Tengchong — east of it the crowded plains and coasts, west of it mountains and deserts with a small fraction of the people. Brazil shows the same pull toward the Atlantic seaboard, and Australia toward its south-eastern coast. Rivers, harbours, coal fields, and later railways and oil fields have all drawn settlement, and the map of population is a palimpsest of each layer of technology in turn.

## Counting people

**The census.** — A census is an official count of a population at a moment in time, and it is a political act before it is a statistical one. Rulers have counted subjects for taxation, conscription, and tribute since antiquity: Egypt and China kept registers, the Han dynasty recorded something near sixty million people in 2 CE, and Rome held a census of citizens for the tax rolls. The modern census is regular and public. Sweden began continuous registers in 1749; the United States, whose constitution requires a decennial count for apportioning seats in the House of Representatives, took its first in 1790; Britain, fearing resistance, took its first in 1801 under John Rickman and has counted every ten years since. The questions a census asks — age, sex, occupation, birthplace, language, religion, household — decide what the state can see and therefore what it can plan.

**Vital registration.** — Births and deaths are counted separately, by continuous registration rather than by a periodic headcount. In England the London Bills of Mortality were published weekly from the sixteenth century, and in 1662 John Graunt used them to estimate the city's population and to construct one of the first life tables, showing how many of a thousand children might survive to each age. Parish registers kept by clergy gave way to civil registration — England and Wales began it in 1837 — and a country's vital statistics now come from registering each birth and death as it happens. Where registration is incomplete, as in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, demographers fall back on sample surveys, such as the Demographic and Health Surveys, and on indirect methods that infer fertility and mortality from the ages of living children.

**What a census misses.** — A count is only as good as its coverage. The homeless, the nomadic, the undocumented, the very poor, and people who fear the state are all easy to miss, and every census has an undercount that statisticians try to estimate and correct. Ages are often misreported, a problem Thomas Welton examined in 1876 when he compared the English censuses of 1851 and 1861 and found people piling up at round numbers — "age heaping" — and women understating their years. The category itself can be contested: who counts as a resident, a citizen, a household? The 1790 American census counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation, a rule that shaped the count itself. A census is evidence about the state's categories as much as about the people behind them.

## Births, deaths, and natural increase

**The crude rates.** — The crude birth rate is the number of live births in a year per thousand people; the crude death rate is the same for deaths. Subtract one from the other and you have the rate of natural increase, which can be negative. "Crude" is a technical term, not an insult: the rates are crude because they take no account of age structure, so a country full of old people can have a low birth rate even if its young families are large. Birth rates run from roughly 45 per thousand in Niger to under 8 in Japan; death rates run from the high teens in the poorest countries to over 12 in some ageing ones, where deaths are many because the population is old. The difference between births and deaths, plus net migration, is the whole of population change.

**Fertility and mortality in finer grain.** — Because crude rates can mislead, demographers use the total fertility rate, the number of children an average woman would bear if she lived through her childbearing years at the rates of a given year. Replacement is about 2.1 children per woman, allowing for children who die before reproducing. Niger's rate is near 6.7; South Korea's fell below 0.8 in the early 2020s, among the lowest ever recorded; the world average is around 2.3 and falling. Mortality is measured by life expectancy at birth and by the infant mortality rate, deaths before the first birthday per thousand live births. Global life expectancy is about 73 years — it fell during the COVID-19 pandemic, the first worldwide drop in decades — and ranges from the mid-fifties in parts of Africa to the mid-eighties in Japan. Infant mortality is the sharpest single indicator of a society's material conditions: under 3 per thousand in Iceland, over 70 in the worst-affected countries. These measures are estimates with error bars, not readings off a dial.

## The demographic transition

**The model.** — In 1929 the American demographer Warren Thompson noticed that industrializing countries passed through a characteristic sequence, and in 1945 Frank Notestein gave the sequence its name: the demographic transition. The model has four stages, sometimes five. In stage one, birth and death rates are both high and roughly cancel, so the population is nearly stable and young. In stage two, death rates fall — better food, clean water, sewers, vaccination, antibiotics, peace — while birth rates stay high, so the population grows fast. In stage three, birth rates begin to fall as children survive, women are educated, cities grow, and contraception spreads. In stage four, both rates are low and the population is stable but much older. A fifth stage describes populations that begin to shrink.

**The model at work.** — England's transition is the classic case. Its death rate fell from the eighteenth century onward, its birth rate stayed high into the late nineteenth century, and the result was the rapid growth that filled industrial cities and sent emigrants overseas; births then fell through the twentieth century. Mauritius, an island sugar economy, moved through the transition with extraordinary speed in the 1960s after a malaria campaign and a family-planning programme. Kerala, a state in southern India, reached replacement fertility not through wealth but through high female literacy and public health.

**The critics.** — The transition is not a law, and its stages are not inevitable. Many countries have stalled: death rates have fallen but birth rates have not followed on the expected schedule, so populations grow faster and longer than the model's European template implied. The HIV/AIDS epidemic in southern Africa pushed death rates back up and set transitions into reverse for a generation. Some governments have tried to hurry the transition and some to stop it, with results that show how much policy matters and how little it controls. Others argue the model smuggles in a European history as a universal rule, and that countries may age before they grow rich, so that stage four arrives without the wealth that Europe had at the same point. Even the model's defenders now speak of transition paths, plural.

## Population pyramids

**Reading a pyramid.** — A population pyramid plots each age group as a horizontal bar, males on one side and females on the other. Its shape is a compressed biography of the population. A broad-based, steep-sided pyramid — Niger's — means high birth rates and many children, with few old people; a column, narrow at the base — Japan's — means low birth rates and a large elderly share; a shape that bulges in the middle and narrows at the top is a workforce with dependants at both ends. Sharp notches mark catastrophes: the Russian pyramid still shows the missing cohorts of the Second World War and the low birth years that followed; Ireland's shows the mark of the famine and of the emigration that continued for generations after it. A bulge moving upward over decades is a baby boom ageing in place.

**Dependency and sex.** — From a pyramid demographers read the dependency ratio: the number of children and old people, who are often not in paid work, divided by the number of working-age adults. A high youth dependency ratio, as in much of Africa, puts pressure on schools; a high old-age dependency ratio, as in Japan and Italy, puts pressure on pensions and health care. The sex ratio at birth is normally about 105 boys to 100 girls, and skews at older ages toward women, who live longer almost everywhere. China's and India's reported sex ratios at birth have been skewed by son preference and sex-selective abortion, a distortion visible in their pyramids as missing young women. A pyramid is not a prediction, but it does constrain the future: the people who will be old in 2050 are already born.

## Malthus and his critics

**The essay.** — In 1798, anonymously, Thomas Robert Malthus published *An Essay on the Principle of Population*. His argument was arithmetic. Population, when unchecked, tends to increase geometrically — 1, 2, 4, 8 — while the food supply can be increased only arithmetically — 1, 2, 3, 4 — so the power of population is indefinitely greater than the power of the earth to produce subsistence. The gap is closed by what he called checks: the positive checks of famine, disease, and war, which raise the death rate, and the preventive checks of delayed marriage and restraint, which lower the birth rate. Malthus was not a prophet of doom so much as a critic of perfectibility: he argued against the French writers who thought society could be remade into universal comfort, and he thought that charity and poor relief, by encouraging early marriage, could make things worse.

**Boserup's reversal.** — A century and a half later the Danish economist Ester Boserup turned the argument around. In *The Conditions of Agricultural Growth* (1965) she argued that population pressure is not simply a brake on food supply but a cause of agricultural change: as land grows scarce, farmers abandon long fallows and shifting cultivation for shorter fallows, then for permanent cultivation, irrigation, and multiple cropping, each more labour-intensive and more productive per hectare. Necessity, as Malthus himself had written, is the mother of invention. On this reading, more people can mean more intensification, not more famine — though at a cost in labour and, often, in soil.

**The debate since.** — The twentieth century gave evidence to both. The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, in which Norman Borlaug's dwarf wheat and rice varieties multiplied yields in Mexico, India, and elsewhere, showed that food production could outrun population for decades, and the share of people who are hungry fell even as the total rose. Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb* (1968) predicted mass famine in the 1970s and 1980s that did not come; Julian Simon's *The Ultimate Resource* (1981) argued that human ingenuity, not raw materials, is the binding constraint. Yet hunger persists, and the reason is less often an absolute shortage of food than poverty, distribution, conflict, and the difficulty of getting grain to the people who need it. Malthus and Boserup are not simply right or wrong; they describe different mechanisms, and which one dominates depends on time, place, and institutions.

## Migration

**Ravenstein's laws.** — In two papers in the 1880s the geographer Ernest George Ravenstein drew general rules from British census data: most migrants move only a short distance; migration proceeds step by step toward growing towns; each stream produces a counter-stream; rural people migrate more than urban; young adults dominate; and women move more than men over short distances, men over long ones. These are tendencies, not laws in the sense of physics.

**Push and pull.** — Migrants are usually described by the forces that move them. Push factors drive people out: poverty, unemployment, land shortage, drought, persecution, war, and the absence of hope. Pull factors draw them in: higher wages, jobs, safety, freedom, family already settled, schools, and the promise of a better life. The two are not symmetrical. Push is often a matter of survival and pull of opportunity, and a single migration may combine both: a young person leaves a village with too little land to work (push) for a city where a cousin has found work (pull). Distance, cost, borders, and law mediate between them, which is why the poorest people often cannot move at all.

**Voluntary and forced.** — The line between voluntary and forced migration is real but blurry. A worker who moves for higher wages is a voluntary migrant; a family fleeing bombardment is a refugee. In between stand people driven by drought or by a government that makes their life impossible without formally expelling them. Under the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol, a refugee is a person outside their own country with a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group, and who cannot or will not return; a country that grants protection grants asylum. People displaced within their own borders are internally displaced persons, or IDPs, and are not covered by the Convention, though they outnumber refugees. By the end of 2023 the United Nations counted more than 110 million people forcibly displaced — refugees, asylum-seekers, and IDPs together — a figure that has roughly doubled in a decade. Turkey, Iran, and Colombia have each hosted millions, most of them from Syria, Afghanistan, and Venezuela respectively; Sudan's war and Ukraine's added millions more.

**Remittances and brain drain.** — Migrants send money home. The World Bank estimated remittances to low- and middle-income countries at about $656 billion in 2023, far more than the world's governments gave in development aid and, in many countries, more than foreign investment. India received the most in absolute terms, over $120 billion; for small states the sums are transformative, and in Tonga remittances have at times exceeded a third of national income. The same flow has a cost. When a country educates doctors and nurses and they leave to work abroad, it loses the money it spent training them and the services they would have provided; this is what is loosely called brain drain. The Philippines has for decades trained nurses and seafarers expressly for export; small island states and poor African countries lose a large share of their health workers to richer ones, even as those workers send money back. Whether migration is a drain or a lifeline depends on who leaves, who returns, and what the remittances do.

## Rural to urban

**The urban century.** — In 2007, for the first time, more than half of humanity lived in towns and cities; the United Nations projects about two-thirds by 2050, with almost all of the increase in Asia and Africa. Cities grow from three sources at once: natural increase among urban residents, the redrawing of boundaries that swallows villages, and rural-to-urban migration. Migration is often the smallest of the three, but it is the most visible and the most studied. The movement is usually stepwise — village to market town, town to city — and often circular, with workers returning home for harvests and festivals, rather than a one-way ticket.

**Why people move to cities.** — The countryside pushes and the city pulls. Farmland is divided among heirs until plots are too small to support a family; mechanization reduces the demand for labour; drought and flood destroy a season's income. The city offers wages, even in the informal economy, and services that villages lack. The results can be overwhelming. Lagos has grown from a few hundred thousand people at Nigerian independence in 1960 to an estimated fifteen million or more; Dhaka and Delhi are larger still; Shanghai and the Pearl River Delta cities have absorbed hundreds of millions of internal migrants. Much of the growth lands in informal settlements that lack secure title, piped water, and sewers, and governments have responded with policies ranging from slum clearance to sites-and-services schemes. China's household registration system, the hukou, ties access to schooling and health care to a person's registered place, so that the hundreds of millions who moved to the cities as workers remain, in law, villagers — a vast internal migration that the census records as rural. A city is a machine for concentrating people; whether that concentration becomes opportunity or misery depends on housing, transport, water, and work.

## Ageing populations

**The old world.** — Where the transition reaches stage four, populations stop growing and begin to age. Japan is the furthest along: about 29 per cent of its people are 65 or older, the highest share of any large country, and its population has been shrinking since 2010. Italy, Germany, Portugal, and Greece are close behind; South Korea, with the world's lowest fertility rate, is ageing faster than any of them. China is a special case: the one-child policy, in force from 1979 until 2015, cut births far faster than the economy or the pension system could adapt, and China now faces the prospect of growing old before it grows rich.

**What ageing does.** — An older population changes almost everything. The old-age dependency ratio rises, so fewer workers support each pensioner; health spending shifts from infectious disease to chronic illness and long-term care; the labour force shrinks unless productivity, immigration, or later retirement fills the gap. Governments have tried pronatalist incentives — baby bonuses, paid parental leave, cheap childcare — with modest and uncertain results; South Korea, Hungary, and Japan have all spent heavily for small changes in births. Immigration can relieve a labour shortage more quickly than births can, but it raises political questions about who belongs, and immigrants age too. The alternative is to adapt: raise retirement ages, increase productivity, and redesign cities and health systems for an older population.

## The debate over population policy

**Control and its costs.** — If population growth is a problem, should governments act on it? China's one-child policy, introduced in 1979 and relaxed to two children in 2016 and three in 2021, prevented hundreds of millions of births by fines, pressure, and sometimes coercion; it also produced a skewed sex ratio, an ageing crisis, and a generation of only children. India under Indira Gandhi ran a mass sterilization campaign during the Emergency of 1975–77, when millions of men were sterilized, some without real consent; the backlash set family planning back for years. Romania's dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu did the opposite: in 1966 he banned most contraception and abortion to raise the birth rate, and the unwanted children who followed were warehoused in orphanages. Coercion can move numbers and wreck lives at the same time.

**Education and its effects.** — The countries that lowered fertility most durably often did so without compulsion, by changing the conditions in which people decide. Kerala, in India, reached replacement fertility with high female literacy, later marriage, and good public health; Thailand's family-planning programme, led by Mechai Viravaidya, made contraception ordinary and cut growth sharply; Mauritius combined malaria control with schooling and family planning. The 1994 International Conference on Population and Development in Cairo marked a turn in the argument: instead of setting population targets, governments agreed to treat reproductive health and the status of women as ends in themselves. The evidence since suggests that where women are educated, employed, and able to choose, birth rates fall — and that where they are not, no target is met for long. The question of what makes a population grow, move, or stabilize turns out to be, in large part, a question about households, and about what they are able to choose.

## Chronology

- **1662** — John Graunt publishes *Natural and Political Observations* on London's Bills of Mortality, an early life table.
- **1749** — Sweden begins continuous population registers.
- **1790** — The first United States census, taken for apportionment in Congress.
- **1798** — Malthus publishes *An Essay on the Principle of Population*.
- **1801** — The first census of Great Britain, organised by John Rickman.
- **1837** — Civil registration of births, marriages, and deaths begins in England and Wales.
- **1876** — Thomas Welton analyses age errors in the English censuses of 1851 and 1861.
- **1885, 1889** — E. G. Ravenstein publishes "The Laws of Migration."
- **1929** — Warren Thompson describes the sequence later called the demographic transition.
- **1945** — Frank Notestein names and formalizes the demographic transition.
- **1951** — The UN Refugee Convention is adopted; the 1967 Protocol extends it.
- **1965** — Ester Boserup publishes *The Conditions of Agricultural Growth*.
- **1966** — Romania's Decree 770 bans abortion and contraception.
- **1968** — Paul Ehrlich publishes *The Population Bomb*.
- **1971** — Wilbur Zelinsky publishes "The Hypothesis of the Mobility Transition."
- **1979** — China introduces the one-child policy.
- **1981** — Julian Simon publishes *The Ultimate Resource*.
- **1994** — The Cairo conference shifts population policy toward reproductive health.
- **2007** — For the first time, more than half the world's people live in cities.
- **2015–2021** — China relaxes to two children, then three.
- **2022** — The United Nations estimates the world population has passed eight billion.

## Questions to carry

- A census is both a count and a political instrument. Which of its numbers are evidence about people, and which are evidence about the state that asked?
- Malthus and Boserup disagree about whether population pressure is a brake or a spur. What would count as evidence for each, and can both be true at once?
- Death rates fall before birth rates in the transition. Why should that order matter so much for how a population grows?
- Migration is often described as a choice. Who, in the cases here, had a real choice, and who did not?
- Ageing and rapid growth are often treated as opposite problems. Could a single policy address both?
