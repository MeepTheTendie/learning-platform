# Agriculture, resources, and trade

**Region:** The whole earth · **Theme:** How distance, resources, and markets decide what is grown and made where

## The agricultural revolutions

**The first farmers.** — Farming was invented many times, in places far apart. The earliest known fields lie in the hill country of the Levant, where by about 9500 BCE people cultivated emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, and flax and herded sheep and goats. Rice was cultivated in the Yangtze valley by about 8000 BCE, millet in the Yellow River basin soon after, and maize was bred from teosinte in Mexico by about 7000 BCE; potatoes came from the Andes and sorghum from the Sahel. Nikolai Vavilov, mapping the centres of origin of cultivated plants in the 1920s, showed that these hearths cluster where wild relatives were available and the climate forgiving. The change was not sudden: at Abu Hureyra on the Euphrates the evidence shows centuries in which people harvested wild rye and then sowed it, without a sharp break. V. Gordon Childe called it the Neolithic Revolution in 1936, and the phrase stuck, but the word is a convenience, not a date.

**What farming changed.** — Domesticating a plant or animal means selecting it for human use until it cannot survive without people. That gave a household a storable surplus, and storage is the root of what followed: surplus could be taxed, and food that could be taxed supported people who did not farm — potters, priests, soldiers, scribes. Settled life brought costs too: animal diseases crossed to humans and grain-heavy diets rotted teeth.

**The medieval revolution.** — Between about the eighth and thirteenth centuries northern Europe reorganized its farming. The heavy mouldboard plough, pulled by horses wearing a padded collar and iron shoes rather than by oxen yoked at the neck, could turn the wet soils north of the Alps. Open fields were rotated in three: winter grain, spring grain, and fallow, with the pasture grazed after harvest. Peas, beans, and vetch restored the nitrogen that grain removed. Watermills ground the harvest — Domesday Book counted some 6,000 in England in 1086 — and windmills followed. England's population rose from perhaps two million in 1086 to five or six million by 1300, and then the Black Death of 1347–51 killed a third or more of Europe.

**The British revolution.** — The most studied change came in England between roughly 1700 and 1850. Jethro Tull designed a horse-drawn seed drill in 1701 and set out his methods in *Horse-Hoeing Husbandry* (1731). Charles "Turnip" Townshend popularized the Norfolk four-course rotation — wheat, turnips, barley, clover — which fed animals in winter and eliminated the bare fallow. Robert Bakewell bred sheep and cattle for meat rather than for the plough or the fleece. Parliament enclosed common land by the thousand: some 4,000 acts between 1750 and 1850 fenced about seven million acres, a fifth of England. Yields rose, and the share of the English workforce in farming fell from perhaps half in 1700 to a fifth by 1850. Whether this was a revolution is disputed. Mark Overton, in *Agricultural Revolution in England* (1996), dates the decisive gains to 1750–1850; Robert Allen argues in *Enclosure and the Yeoman* (1992) that the change was smaller and later than usually claimed; Eric Kerridge put a revolution two centuries earlier. The label is a judgement, and the judges disagree.

## Subsistence and commercial farming

**Two logics.** — A subsistence farm grows chiefly to feed the household, spreads risk across many crops and animals, and measures success in stored food. A commercial farm grows to sell, specializes in the crop that pays best, and measures success in money. The distinction matters because the commercial grower is tied to prices he does not set: when the world price of coffee or cotton falls, he loses income even if the harvest is good. The "three sisters" of maize, beans, and squash in Mesoamerica, or the rice-and-fish paddies of southern China, were risk-spreading arrangements as much as diets.

**Intensive rice.** — Wet-rice farming is the most labour-hungry of the great cereal systems and among the most productive per hectare. Seedlings are raised in a nursery and transplanted by hand into flooded paddies, where standing water suppresses weeds. In the deltas of Asia farmers take two or three crops a year, and the terraces of the Philippine Cordillera at Banaue have held soil on steep slopes for some two thousand years. Yields of four to six tonnes of paddy per hectare are common, but each hectare absorbs an enormous amount of human labour. The Danish economist Ester Boserup argued in *The Conditions of Agricultural Growth* (1965) that this is the normal direction of history: as population grows and land runs short, people intensify. Her argument runs opposite to Thomas Malthus, who held that population presses against a fixed food supply.

**Shifting cultivation.** — In the tropical forest the cheapest way to farm is to cut and burn a patch, plant it for two or three years in a mixture of crops, then abandon it while the forest regrows. The ash fertilizes the thin soil and the long fallow restores it. The system is called swidden, or slash-and-burn; its local names include milpa in Mesoamerica and jhum in north-east India. It is sustainable when fallow is long and population thin, and destructive when land hunger shortens the fallow until the forest cannot return. Blaming the farmers gets the sequence backwards: the system fails because they have run out of forest.

**Nomadic pastoralism.** — Some environments suit no plough. On the Eurasian steppe, in the Arabian desert, and across the Sahel, people have lived by moving their herds to whatever pasture the season offers. Mongol herders followed grass and water; the Bedouin moved between wells; the Fulani drove cattle south in the dry season. Mountain peoples practised transhumance, taking flocks up to summer pasture and down in winter. Nomads were not isolated: they traded wool, hides, horses, and salt for grain, tea, and metal, and their mobility gave them military advantages settled empires feared.

## Von Thünen and the rings of cultivation

**The isolated state.** — In 1826 a Mecklenburg estate owner named Johann Heinrich von Thünen published *Der isolierte Staat* — *The Isolated State* — to ask a narrow question: if all farms faced the same soil and one market, what would each grow, and where? He imagined a single city in the middle of a uniform plain, with no river, no other town, and no trade beyond the plain's edge. Under those artificial conditions, he reasoned, the deciding cost is transport. Crops that are heavy, bulky, or perishable relative to their value must be grown close to the city; crops that are light and durable can come from far away. The result is concentric rings: market gardens and dairy nearest the town, then forest for fuel, then arable land in rotations, then grain in fallow systems, and finally ranching on the outer edge, where cattle can be walked to market alive. Von Thünen called the surplus a farm earns over its costs *land rent*, and showed that it falls with distance: rent equals yield times the difference between price and production cost, minus the cost of hauling the crop to town. His purpose was to isolate distance, as a chemist isolates a substance.

**Where it holds, and where it fails.** — Real landscapes show the pattern where the assumptions nearly hold. Before refrigeration, the land around a big city really did supply fresh milk, vegetables, and hay: New York's milk shed reached out by rail as the city grew, and market gardens ringed London. But refrigeration, canning, and fast rail let milk and meat travel thousands of kilometres, and a second market, a river, a subsidy, or a tariff distorts the rings. Later theorists rebuilt the mathematics with many markets and producers — August Lösch's *The Economics of Location* (1940) and Walter Isard's work in the 1950s — and behavioural critics such as Allan Pred argued that real farmers copy their neighbours rather than calculate rents. Von Thünen's rings survive as the clearest statement of a true idea: distance from the market shapes what the land is used for.

## Plantations and cash crops

**The sugar revolution.** — The plantation is a farm organized like a factory, growing a single export crop with imported labour on land taken from somebody else. Sugar led the way. Cane was transplanted from the Mediterranean to Brazil and then the Caribbean, and in the 1640s Barbados converted almost its whole surface to cane. By 1700 the small island was among the richest colonies on earth, worked by tens of thousands of enslaved Africans. Saint-Domingue, the French half of Hispaniola, produced about two-fifths of the world's sugar and half its coffee on the eve of the revolution of 1791.

**Cotton, rubber, and the rest.** — Cotton from the American South fed the mills of Lancashire; by 1860 the United States grew about two-thirds of the world's cotton with the labour of nearly four million enslaved people. Rubber was gathered wild in the Amazon until 1876, when seeds of the *Hevea* tree were carried to Kew and planted in Malaya; by the 1920s Malaya was the leading producer. Tea moved from China to Assam in the 1830s and to Ceylon after a coffee blight in 1869; coffee became the making of São Paulo, and by 1900 Brazil supplied about three-quarters of the world's crop. Cocoa made Ghana the largest producer by 1911, and the United Fruit Company, founded in 1899, built a banana trade so entwined with Central American governments that the phrase "banana republic" was coined. In Java the Dutch ran the Cultivation System from 1830 to 1870, requiring villages to grow export crops for the state; at its height it supplied nearly a fifth of Dutch government revenue, and its abuses were denounced in Multatuli's novel *Max Havelaar* (1860).

## The Green Revolution and its critics

**Borlaug's wheat.** — In 1944 the American plant pathologist Norman Borlaug arrived in Mexico to breed wheat for the Rockefeller Foundation. By crossing Japanese dwarf varieties, which put their energy into grain rather than stalk, he produced short, stiff wheats that could carry heavy heads without falling over and that responded richly to fertilizer and water. Mexico became self-sufficient in wheat by the mid-1950s. In the Philippines the International Rice Research Institute released the rice variety IR8 in 1966, which could double traditional yields under irrigation. India's wheat harvest grew from about twelve million tonnes in 1960 to more than a hundred million by the 2010s, and Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.

**The bill.** — The new varieties did not work without their package of inputs: chemical fertilizer, controlled irrigation, and pesticides. In the Indian Punjab the water table fell as tube wells multiplied, and soils became saline where irrigation was poorly drained. Thousands of local rice and wheat varieties, each adapted to a particular soil or flood, were abandoned for a handful of high-yielders, narrowing the genetic base of the world's food. The environmentalist Vandana Shiva argued in *The Violence of the Green Revolution* (1991) that the gains went to richer farmers and the costs to the poor and to the soil. The counter-argument is that without the higher yields, millions more hectares of forest would have been ploughed and food prices would have been higher. A large study by Douglas Gollin, Casper Worm Hansen, and Asger Wingender, published in 2021, found that the new varieties raised yields substantially but had surprisingly little effect on national income or poverty.

## Food insecurity and famine

**Malthus.** — In 1798 Thomas Robert Malthus argued in *An Essay on the Principle of Population* that population grows geometrically while food can grow only arithmetically, so that numbers will always press on the means of subsistence and be held back by famine, disease, and war. It is a powerful argument and, in its simple form, wrong: world food production has outrun population for two centuries.

**Sen's entitlement.** — The Indian economist Amartya Sen looked at famines and found that the shortage was often not of food but of the ability to get it. In *Poverty and Famines* (1981) he examined the Bengal famine of 1943, in which between two and three million people died. Food available per head in Bengal in 1943 was not far below the level of years without famine; the harvest was not catastrophic. What collapsed was what Sen called entitlements — the means by which different groups command food. Rural labourers' wages fell as food prices rose under wartime inflation; fishermen lost their livelihoods when boats were seized under a denial policy; people with no land and no savings could not buy the rice that existed. "Starvation is the characteristic of some people not having enough food to eat," Sen wrote. "It is not the characteristic of there being not enough food to eat." Famine, on this account, is a distribution problem.

**The debate.** — Sen's argument is not the whole story. The agricultural historian Mark Tauger has argued that the Bengal rice crop of 1943 was genuinely reduced by a cyclone and disease, and that food availability did fall. Cormac Ó Gráda's *Famine: A Short History* (2009) concludes that entitlements explain much but that availability still matters. The Irish famine of 1845–52 tests both views: a potato blight destroyed the food of the poor while wheat, oats, and butter continued to be exported to Britain, and about a million people died and a million more emigrated. Whether the deaths were caused by the blight, by the British government's refusal to interfere with markets, or by both is still argued. Famines are often made by people: the Indian famines of 1876–78, 1896–97, and 1899–1902 killed millions while grain moved by the new railways, and modern famines in Ethiopia, Somalia, and Sudan have been driven as much by war as by drought.

## The Industrial Revolution and the location of industry

**Coal, iron, and steam.** — Manufacture scattered among cottages and watermills gathered into factories when its power source changed. Abraham Darby smelted iron with coke instead of charcoal at Coalbrookdale in 1709; James Watt patented an improved steam engine in 1769 and gave it rotary motion in 1781; Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769), Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779), and Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785) mechanized cotton. British coal output rose from about six million tonnes in 1770 to some thirty million by 1830, and cotton became the leading export. Production shifted to the coalfields — Lancashire, Yorkshire, the Midlands, South Wales, and across the North Sea to the Ruhr and Silesia. Manchester grew from a town of perhaps 25,000 in the 1770s to a mill city of 300,000 by 1851. The same logic later moved manufacturing to the United States, Germany, Japan after 1868, and in the late twentieth century to South Korea, Taiwan, and China, where Shenzhen was made a special economic zone in 1980.

**Weber's least-cost theory.** — In 1909 the German economist Alfred Weber published *Über den Standort der Industrien* — *Theory of the Location of Industries* — to do for the factory what von Thünen had done for the farm. A firm, he assumed, chooses the place that minimizes its costs, of which three matter most: transport, labour, and the savings from clustering with other firms. Transport cost depends on weight and distance, so Weber asked whether a factory loses or gains weight in manufacture. A weight-losing industry, such as copper smelting or sugar refining, sheds most of the weight of its raw material and locates near the mine or field; a weight-gaining industry, such as bottling or baking, adds weight to a small input and locates near the market. Between these pulls the site sits where the savings on transport and labour are greatest — a calculation Weber illustrated with lines he called isodapanes, the contours of equal added cost. Pittsburgh's steel industry grew where coal and iron ore could meet. The theory also predicts its own revision: when cheap scrap and electric furnaces arrived in the 1960s, minimills moved toward the cities that generated the scrap. Critics note that real firms are not perfectly informed and are often held in place by history, habit, and government policy, but his central insight — that weight, distance, and cost decide where making happens — survives.

## Energy and mineral resources

**The map of deposits.** — Modern industry runs on materials that geology put in particular places. Coal is abundant in the United States, Russia, Australia, China, and India. Oil is concentrated in the Persian Gulf — Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, and the United Arab Emirates — with large reserves also in Venezuela, Canada, Russia, and the United States; natural gas is heaviest in Russia, Iran, and Qatar. Iron ore comes chiefly from Australia and Brazil; copper from Chile, which supplies more than a quarter of the world's output, and Peru; bauxite from Guinea and Australia; cobalt from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which produces about two-thirds of the world's supply; rare earth elements from China, which mines most and processes almost all. The map is not fixed: what counts as a resource depends on technology and price. The American geologist M. King Hubbert predicted in 1956 that American oil production would peak and decline, and it did — but hydraulic fracturing then opened shale deposits that had never been counted, and by the 2010s the United States was the world's largest producer of both oil and gas.

**The resource curse.** — A country rich in one mineral is not always lucky. The economist Richard Auty coined the phrase "resource curse" in 1993, and Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner found in 1995 that resource-rich countries tended to grow more slowly than resource-poor ones. A single export can make a government independent of its citizens' taxes, feed corruption, and draw labour away from farming and manufacturing; a sudden fall in the world price can bankrupt the state overnight. The oil embargo of 1973, when the producers' cartel OPEC cut supplies and prices quadrupled, showed both the power and the fragility of resource wealth. Some countries escape — Norway banked its oil revenue in a sovereign fund, Botswana managed its diamonds, Chile its copper — and the difference lies in institutions rather than geology.

## Core and periphery

**Dependency and world-systems.** — Why do some regions make the goods and others grow the food? One answer, dependency theory, was set out by the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch and the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America in the 1950s: the periphery exports primary products whose prices fall over time and imports manufactures whose prices rise, so trade transfers wealth to the core. Andre Gunder Frank pushed the argument further in 1966, claiming that development and underdevelopment were two sides of one process. Immanuel Wallerstein's *The Modern World-System* (1974) recast it as a single world economy with a core, a semi-periphery, and a periphery, each defined by its role in the division of labour. Critics say the theory understates local institutions and the countries that climbed the ladder, but it named a real pattern.

**The Brandt line.** — In 1980 a commission chaired by the former West German chancellor Willy Brandt published *North–South: A Programme for Survival*, which drew a line around the planet at roughly thirty degrees north to separate the rich countries of the North from the poor countries of the South. The image, soon called the Brandt line, captured the world of the 1970s: a wealthy industrial North and a poorer, commodity-exporting South. It has since been overtaken by events. South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore industrialized within a generation; China became the world's largest manufacturer; the Gulf states grew rich on oil. Today the poorest countries are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, while some countries south of the old line are richer than parts of the former communist North. The economist Branko Milanovic's "elephant curve" of global income growth between 1988 and 2008 showed the largest gains going to the middle of the world income distribution, much of it in Asia, and to the very richest, while the lower-middle of the rich countries stagnated.

## Containerization and global supply chains

**The box.** — In April 1956 a former trucking owner named Malcom McLean loaded fifty-eight metal boxes onto a converted tanker, the *Ideal X*, and sailed them from Newark to Houston. The idea was to move freight without unloading and reloading it piece by piece, and it worked. Standard sizes — the twenty-foot and forty-foot container, measured in twenty-foot equivalent units, or TEUs — were agreed internationally between 1961 and 1968, and ports, ships, and cranes were rebuilt around them. The cost of loading a tonne of cargo fell from nearly six dollars in 1956 to a few cents by the 1970s, as Marc Levinson records in *The Box* (2006). When freight became almost free, the cost of labour began to dominate, and manufacturing moved to wherever labour was cheap. Rotterdam, Singapore, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Busan became the gateways of a single production system: a car or a phone is assembled from parts made in many countries, arriving on a schedule that leaves little room for delay. The fragility of that system was exposed in March 2021, when the container ship *Ever Given* wedged across the Suez Canal and held up world trade for six days.

**The answer to the question.** — Distance, resources, and markets decide what is grown and made where. Distance raises the cost of moving goods, which is why perishable and heavy crops were once grown near the city and why cheap container freight later pulled factories toward cheap labour. Resources — fertile soil, coal, oil, ore, and the climate that suits one crop and not another — fix the raw possibilities of a place, though technology and price keep redrawing which can be used. Markets decide what is worth producing at all, and a farm or factory that ignores the market fails. When transport was dear, production sat close to the land or the mine; when it became cheap, distance mattered less and labour, skills, and institutions mattered more, and the map of who grows and makes what was redrawn again.

## Questions to carry

- Farming was invented in many places, but only a few of those hearths produced the crops that came to feed the world. What did the lucky ones have that the others lacked?
- Von Thünen assumed a single market and no rivers. Which of his rings survive when refrigeration and cheap freight are added, and which disappear?
- The Green Revolution raised yields but a 2021 study found little effect on poverty. Can both be true, and what does the gap tell you about who captures the gains from a new technology?
- Sen argues that famine is a failure of entitlement, not of food supply; Tauger and Ó Gráda argue that supply still matters. What kind of evidence would settle the disagreement, and is it the kind that survives in the record?
- Container shipping made distance cheap and pulled factories toward cheap labour. What does that suggest about where manufacturing will move next, and what could reverse it?
