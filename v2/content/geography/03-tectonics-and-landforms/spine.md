# Plate tectonics and landforms

**Region:** The whole earth · **Theme:** Deep time and the moving crust

## The earth beneath the surface

**What the waves show.** — Nearly everything known about the earth's interior comes from earthquake waves. A slipping fault sends P-waves, which compress and stretch rock in their direction of travel, and S-waves, which shake it sideways; both bend where the rock changes, and S-waves cannot cross liquid. Reading arrivals on seismographs worldwide, seismologists worked out the layers: a crust 5–10 kilometres thick under the oceans and 30–50 kilometres under the continents; a mantle of hot, creeping silicate rock to about 2,900 kilometres; a molten outer core of iron and nickel; and a solid inner core at a temperature near the sun's surface. The crust–mantle boundary was detected in 1909 by the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić, whose name it still carries.

**Heat, and the layer that moves.** — The interior is hot from the collisions that built the planet and from decaying uranium, thorium, and potassium. Heat rises and cool rock sinks, so the mantle turns over in convection cells moving centimetres a year; over geological time hot rock behaves like stiff putty. Above it lies the rigid lithosphere — crust plus uppermost mantle, about 100 kilometres thick — cracked into roughly a dozen large plates and many smaller ones, moving 1 to 10 centimetres a year, the speed of a growing fingernail, as the Global Positioning System now measures directly.

## Continental drift

**Wegener.** — The idea that the continents once fitted together is older than the theory that explains it, but Alfred Wegener (1880–1930), a German meteorologist and polar explorer, turned the curiosity into an argument. He first lectured on "the displacement of continents" in January 1912, and in 1915 published *Die Entstehung der Kontinente und Ozeane* — *The Origin of Continents and Oceans* — which he rewrote through four editions; an English translation appeared in 1924. A balloonist who set a 52-hour record in 1906, he died on his fourth expedition to Greenland.

**The evidence.** — His case was a pile of independent facts that lined up. The coastlines of South America and Africa fit like torn paper, and the fit improves when the submerged continental shelves, not the present shores, are matched. Fossils told the same story: the seed-fern *Glossopteris*, the freshwater reptile *Mesosaurus*, and the land reptiles *Lystrosaurus* and *Cynognathus* appear in Permian and Triassic rocks on now-separated southern continents, and none could have crossed a wide ocean. Mountain belts matched across the water: the Caledonian rocks of Scotland continue into the Appalachians. Permian glacial deposits — scratched pavements and a cemented rubble called tillite — cover South America, Africa, India, Australia, and Antarctica, with scratch marks pointing away from a common ice centre near the south pole. Coal of the same age lies under Antarctic ice, and tropical coal and coral are found on Arctic Spitsbergen.

**Why it was rejected.** — For four decades most geologists, and especially most American geologists, refused the theory. The objection was not the evidence but the mechanism. Wegener proposed that the continents ploughed through the ocean floor, driven by the spin of the earth and the tidal drag of the moon and sun. The English physicist Harold Jeffreys calculated that these forces were far too weak and that a continent pushing through solid rock would crumple, and Wegener's estimate that Greenland drifted up to 30 metres a year was never confirmed. To a physicist the theory failed on physics; to a field geologist it failed because it demanded young, mobile ocean basins when fossils and sediments seemed to show the oceans had been permanent for hundreds of millions of years. The Austrian geologist Eduard Suess had explained the same fossil patterns with "land bridges," now-sunken strips of continent, and most geologists preferred that to drift. The English geologist Arthur Holmes proposed in 1929 that mantle convection might drag the continents, but only a handful kept the idea alive.

## Sea-floor spreading and the magnetic record

**Hess and Dietz.** — The evidence that turned the argument came from the ocean floor. During the Second World War Harry H. Hess (1906–1969), a Princeton geologist commanding a naval transport in the Pacific, used its echo sounder to survey the sea floor between landings. In a paper circulated from 1960 and published in 1962 as "History of Ocean Basins," Hess proposed that the ocean floor is not permanent: new crust forms at the mid-ocean ridges and spreads sideways like a conveyor belt, and because the earth does not grow, an equal amount of old crust must be destroyed at the deep trenches where cold sea floor sinks back into the mantle. Robert S. Dietz of the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey reached a similar conclusion and coined the term "sea-floor spreading" in 1961. The American oceanographers Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen had been mapping the Mid-Atlantic Ridge by hand from echo soundings; Tharp's profiles revealed a continuous central rift valley, the seam where the floor splits, though Heezen at first dismissed it as impossible.

**Magnetic stripes.** — The decisive test came from magnetism. When basaltic lava cools, grains of magnetite lock in the direction of the earth's field at that moment. Bernard Brunhes in 1906 and Motonari Matuyama in the 1920s noticed rocks magnetised opposite to the present field, as if it had reversed. In the 1950s, magnetometers towed behind ships found the ocean floor striped with alternating bands of normal and reversed magnetisation, parallel to the ridges and symmetrical on either side. In 1963 two Cambridge graduate students, Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews, and independently Lawrence Morley of the Geological Survey of Canada, explained the pattern: new crust forms at the ridge, records the field of the day, and is carried away as more crust is added, so each reversal writes a new stripe. The ocean floor is a tape recorder, and its playback matched the reversal timescale that Allan Cox, Richard Doell, and Brent Dalrymple were building from dated lava flows on land.

**The clinching evidence.** — In 1968 the drilling ship *Glomar Challenger* began the Deep Sea Drilling Project, pulling cores of sediment and basalt from across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The ages were exactly as spreading predicted: youngest at the crest, steadily older to the west and east at the rate the magnetic stripes implied. In 1977 the submersible *Alvin* found hot springs and colonies of giant tube worms on the Galápagos Rift, confirming that new ocean floor was being created and cooled by seawater there. Spreading did not so much prove Wegener right as replace his mechanism: the continents do not plough through the ocean; they ride on plates that are created and destroyed.

## Plate boundaries and their landforms

**Divergent boundaries.** — Where two plates pull apart, the boundary is divergent. Under the ocean it makes the mid-ocean ridge, a submarine mountain chain more than 50,000 kilometres long, rising on average about 4,500 metres above the surrounding floor, with a rift valley along its crest where basalt erupts and hot water rises. Iceland is a rare place where the ridge stands above sea level: at Thingvellir a visitor can walk into the gap between the North American and Eurasian plates. Where divergence happens under a continent it makes a rift valley. The East African Rift, running from the Afar Triangle in Ethiopia south through the Great Lakes, is splitting Africa; its floor holds deep lakes such as Tanganyika and Malawi. The Red Sea is a rift that has already opened, and Afar, where three boundaries meet, is a triple junction.

**Convergent boundaries.** — Where plates collide, one is usually forced down, or subducted. Ocean against ocean: the older, colder slab sinks and the overriding edge buckles into a curved chain of volcanic islands — an island arc — behind a deep trench. The Mariana Trench reaches nearly 11,000 metres, and the arc behind it includes Japan, the Philippines, and the Aleutians. Ocean against continent: the slab subducts beneath the lighter land, scraping off sediment and feeding magma upward to build a volcanic chain — the Andes, the Cascades. Earthquakes along these boundaries fall on an inclined plane, the Benioff zone, dipping 40 to 60 degrees and reaching hundreds of kilometres down, direct evidence of a sinking slab. Continent against continent: neither subducts easily, and the crust is crumpled into fold mountains. The Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau rose from the collision of India with Asia beginning about 50 million years ago; India still drives north at about 5 centimetres a year, Everest stands 8,849 metres high, and the range rises a few millimetres a year even as erosion wears it down.

**Transform boundaries.** — Where two plates slide past each other the boundary is a transform fault, a long, straight, seismically active line rather than a mountain range or a trench. The San Andreas Fault runs about 1,300 kilometres through California, marking the Pacific–North American boundary, where the plates slide at about 5 centimetres a year; the land west of the fault is being carried toward Alaska. It is not a single clean cut but a braided system of segments that stick and then break, which is why California has earthquakes. On the ocean floor, transform faults offset the segments of the ridges and were one of the predictions that confirmed spreading: the movement along the fault is opposite in sense to the apparent offset of the ridge, exactly what a spreading origin requires, as J. Tuzo Wilson argued in 1965.

## Earthquakes

**What an earthquake is.** — Rock under stress bends elastically until it breaks or slips along a fault, then snaps back, releasing stored energy as seismic waves. This is the elastic-rebound theory, worked out by H. F. Reid in 1910 from surveys of the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. The point where the rupture begins underground is the focus; the point on the surface above it is the epicentre. P-waves arrive first, S-waves second, and the slower surface waves do much of the damage. The gap in arrival times at one station gives the distance to the quake; three stations give the position.

**Measuring.** — In 1935 Charles F. Richter of the California Institute of Technology devised a scale for southern California earthquakes. It was logarithmic: each whole number meant ten times the ground motion and about thirty-one times the energy released. The Richter scale was meant for local events and saturates for great ones, so since the 1970s seismologists have used the moment magnitude scale, developed by Thomas Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori, which measures the total energy released. The largest recorded earthquake is the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, magnitude 9.5; the 1964 Alaska earthquake was 9.2, and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake 9.0. The older Mercalli scale, introduced by Giuseppe Mercalli in 1902, measures intensity — what was felt and damaged. Deaths depend less on magnitude than on construction and soil: the 2010 Haiti earthquake, magnitude 7.0, killed more than 100,000 people.

**Where and why.** — Earthquakes are not scattered at random. Roughly nine in ten occur along plate boundaries, most around the Pacific rim in the "Ring of Fire," where subducting slabs generate quakes from shallow depths to about 700 kilometres. The pattern is the strongest single piece of evidence that the plates are real. Quakes far from boundaries — intraplate earthquakes — are rarer and less understood; the New Madrid earthquakes of 1811–12, which reversed the flow of the Mississippi and rang church bells in Boston, struck the middle of the North American plate. Under the sea, a sudden vertical slip on a thrust fault can displace the water column and send a tsunami racing across an ocean, as in the Indian Ocean in 2004 and at Tōhoku in 2011.

## Volcanoes and the Ring of Fire

**The Ring of Fire.** — About 75 per cent of the world's active volcanoes and 90 per cent of its earthquakes lie in a horseshoe roughly 40,000 kilometres around the Pacific, from New Zealand and Japan through the Aleutians and down the western coasts of the Americas. The reason is subduction: as an ocean slab sinks it carries water into the hot mantle above, lowering the melting point of the rock; the magma rises, gathers in chambers, and erupts through the overriding plate, building chains of volcanoes a fixed distance behind the trench. Mount Fuji, Mount Rainier, Mount St. Helens, and the Andes all belong to this system, and the Mediterranean has its own convergent belt of Vesuvius, Etna, and Stromboli. Volcanoes also occur at rifts, where the crust is pulled apart and magma rises to fill the gap, as in Iceland, and at hot spots far from any boundary.

**Kinds of eruption.** — The behaviour of a volcano depends largely on the silica and gas in its magma. Runny, low-silica basaltic lava builds broad shield volcanoes; the Hawaiian islands are a chain of them, and Mauna Loa is the largest active volcano on earth. Sticky, high-silica magma traps gas and erupts explosively, building steep stratovolcanoes and throwing out ash, bombs, and glowing avalanches called pyroclastic flows. The 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée on Martinique sent a pyroclastic flow through Saint-Pierre and killed about 30,000 people in minutes. Krakatoa in 1883 was heard nearly 5,000 kilometres away; Tambora in 1815 threw so much sulphate into the stratosphere that 1816 became the year without a summer.

## Hot spots and Hawaii

**A fixed point under a moving plate.** — In 1963 J. Tuzo Wilson suggested that some volcanoes are fed by a fixed source of heat deep in the mantle, a hot spot, over which the plate slides. The result is a chain of volcanoes that grows progressively older in the direction of plate motion. The Hawaiian–Emperor chain is the classic case: Hawaii sits over the hot spot now, with the still-submerged seamount Lōʻihi forming beside it; moving northwest, the islands grow older, and the chain bends sharply about 47 million years ago where the direction of Pacific motion changed. The same idea explains the Yellowstone hot spot, which left a trail of calderas across the Snake River Plain, and the hot spot under Iceland.

**A contested mechanism.** — The hot-spot idea assumes narrow plumes of hot rock rising from deep in the mantle, and for decades that was the standard picture. It is now disputed. The American geophysicists Gillian Foulger, Warren Hamilton, and Don Anderson have argued that many "hot spots" are not deep plumes but the ordinary result of shallow extension and cracks in the lithosphere. The debate is unresolved, which is instructive: even so successful a theory leaves questions open. What is not disputed is the trail of ages along the Hawaiian chain, which records the motion of the Pacific plate whatever lies beneath.

## The rock cycle

**Three families of rock.** — Every rock belongs to one of three families, and each can be transformed into the others. Igneous rock forms when molten material cools and crystallises: basalt from the ocean floor, granite from deep chambers. Sedimentary rock forms when erosion's products are deposited in layers and cemented — sandstone from sand, shale from mud, limestone from shells. Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is cooked and squeezed without melting: limestone becomes marble, shale slate, granite gneiss. The classification is a cycle, driven by the same plate motions that build mountains and open oceans. Rock is melted at subduction zones and ridges, erupted, weathered into sediment, buried, compressed, heated, and melted again, for billions of years. The rock cycle and the tectonic cycle are two views of one machine.

**Deep time and uniformitarianism.** — The Scottish naturalist James Hutton, standing in 1788 at Siccar Point on the east coast of Scotland, saw two sets of layers meeting at a steep angle, the lower set tilted, eroded flat, and buried by the upper. The junction, an unconformity, recorded an immense interval in which mountains had risen, worn down, and been submerged again. Hutton concluded that the earth shows "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." Charles Lyell's *Principles of Geology* (1830–33) made the method famous: the same processes acting now can explain the past. The age of the earth — 4.54 billion years, measured by Clair Patterson in 1953 from lead isotopes in meteorites — is the necessary background.

## Weathering and erosion

**Breaking rock down.** — Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place; erosion is the removal and transport of the pieces. Physical weathering breaks rock without changing its chemistry: water freezes in cracks and splits them; salt crystallises in coastal rock and pries it apart; unloading as rock is exposed makes it peel in sheets. Chemical weathering changes the minerals: slightly acidic rain dissolves limestone, carving the caves and sinkholes of karst country; oxygen and water rust iron minerals; hydrolysis turns feldspar into clay. Living things contribute — roots wedge, lichens etch, worms mix the soil. Hot, wet climates speed chemistry; cold, dry ones favour physical breakage.

**Water.** — Running water is the most important agent of erosion on land. A young river on a steep gradient cuts a narrow V-shaped valley; as the gradient lessens it wanders in meanders, cuts off oxbow lakes, and drops its load as a delta where it reaches standing water. The Colorado River has cut the Grand Canyon nearly 1.8 kilometres into the Colorado Plateau; the timing is disputed, with older estimates around 5 to 6 million years and newer work suggesting parts were carved from 17 million years or more. Niagara Falls retreats upstream as soft rock is undercut beneath the hard caprock — about a metre a year in the nineteenth century, less now that water is diverted for power. At the coast, waves cut platforms and sea stacks and move sediment by longshore drift.

**Ice.** — Glaciers are rivers of ice that form where snow accumulates faster than it melts. As they move they pluck and grind the rock beneath. Alpine glaciers carve bowl-shaped cirques, sharp ridges called arêtes, and pyramid horns — the Matterhorn is the classic horn. Continental ice sheets, such as those over Antarctica and Greenland, bulldoze vast areas; the ice ages of the Pleistocene left northern Europe and North America scoured and strewn with boulders called erratics and ridges of debris called moraines. Where a glacier reaches the sea it cuts a deep U-shaped valley that floods as a fjord, as along the Norwegian coast. At the height of the last glaciation, sea level was more than 100 metres lower than now, and the Bering Strait was dry land.

**Wind, and the pull of gravity.** — In dry regions wind becomes a major sculptor. It deflates loose material, sandblasts rock, and piles sand into dunes whose shapes record the wind — crescent barchans where sand is scarce, star dunes where it is not. The finest dust settles as loess, a blanket of wind-blown silt; the Loess Plateau of China is up to about 300 metres thick, and Saharan dust blows across the Atlantic to the Amazon. Gravity moves material downhill without river or glacier: landslides, rockfalls, and slow creep. In 1903 a slab of Turtle Mountain at Frank, Alberta, dropped onto the town in 90 seconds, killing more than 70 people; in 1963 a landslide into the reservoir behind the Vajont Dam in Italy sent a wave over the dam and killed about 2,000.

## Deep time and the shape of landscapes

**Reading time in rock.** — The Danish scientist Nicolas Steno set out in 1669 the principles that made stratigraphy possible: in undisturbed layers the oldest is at the bottom, layers are laid down horizontally, and a layer cutting across another is younger than the one it cuts. Fossils gave relative ages. Absolute dates came with radioactivity: Ernest Rutherford suggested in 1905 that decay could serve as a clock, Arthur Holmes produced the first radiometric timescale in 1911, and the modern time scale was built from uranium–lead and potassium–argon dates, cross-checked against the magnetic reversals recorded in the ocean floor. The earth is 4.54 billion years old; the oldest rocks found are about 4.0 billion years old.

**Landscapes in balance.** — A landscape is the meeting point of two forces: the tectonic uplift that raises rock and the erosion that wears it down. Where uplift is fast and young, as in the Himalaya, mountains are high and slopes steep; where uplift has long ceased, as in the Appalachians, the same kinds of rock are worn to rounded ridges after hundreds of millions of years. The crust floats on the mantle in a rough balance called isostasy: as a mountain erodes, its roots rise, which is why old mountain belts are not merely flattened but lifted and exposed. Rivers adjust to the rock they cross, cutting gorges through hard layers and widening into valleys in soft ones.

**The arrangement is not permanent.** — The continents have assembled and broken apart several times. About 1.1 billion years ago most of the land lay in a supercontinent called Rodinia; about 250 million years ago it lay in Pangaea, surrounded by a single ocean, Panthalassa. The Mediterranean is a shrinking remnant of the Tethys, closing as Africa moves north against Europe; the Atlantic is widening; India continues to drive into Asia; and the Red Sea and East African Rift may one day meet to make a new ocean. The whole cycle of assembly and breakup, named the Wilson cycle after J. Tuzo Wilson, takes roughly 300 to 500 million years.

**The answer to the question.** — A theory built from fossils, rocks, and magnetism explains mountains and earthquakes because those three kinds of evidence turned out to be records of the same motion. Fossils and matching rock belts showed that the continents were once joined; magnetism in the ocean floor showed that new crust is made at ridges and carried away; the pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes showed where the plates grind, sink, and split. Mountains rise where plates collide and crumple, or where a sinking slab feeds magma upward; earthquakes mark the places where moving rock sticks and snaps. The fit of the coastlines that struck Wegener was not a coincidence but a consequence — the torn edges of a continent still moving a few centimetres a year.

## Questions to carry

- Wegener had the fossils and the fit of the coasts. What did he lack that Hess supplied, and why did the lack matter more to physicists than to geologists?
- If new ocean floor is made at the ridges, where does the old floor go, and what evidence on the sea bed shows it leaving?
- The Himalaya and the Andes are both made by collision. Why is one a chain of fold mountains and the other a line of volcanoes?
- The Richter scale is logarithmic and saturates; the moment magnitude scale does not. Why does the choice of scale change what counts as the "largest" earthquake?
- The Hawaiian chain records plate motion, but the cause of the hot spot is disputed. What kind of evidence would settle whether plumes rise from the deep mantle?
