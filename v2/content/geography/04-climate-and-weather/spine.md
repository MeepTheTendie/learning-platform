# Climate and weather

**Region:** The whole earth · **Organizing question:** Why does it rain where it rains, and why is the equator hot?

## Weather and climate

**Two words, one atmosphere.** — Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place and a moment: the temperature, pressure, wind, cloud, and rain that an instrument records or a person feels. Climate is the same quantities tallied over decades. The World Meteorological Organization sets the standard interval at thirty years, so a climate is a distribution of weathers, with means, ranges, and the odds of extremes. The distinction is not that one is short and one long but that they answer different questions: a weather report says whether to carry an umbrella, a climate record whether the umbrella is needed more often than a generation ago. A single cold morning no more disproves a warming trend than a single warm afternoon proves one.

**The quip and the record.** — A line often credited to Mark Twain holds that climate is what we expect and weather is what we get; the attribution is doubtful, but the point is not. Climate is expectation built from record, and when a place's thirty-year means shift, the expectation shifts with them. The instruments matter: the thermometer came into wide use in the eighteenth century, the rain gauge is older, and systematic station networks cover the globe densely only from the nineteenth century. Before that, climate must be reconstructed from proxies — tree rings, ice cores, corals, lake sediments, and written notes about harvests and frosts.

## The atmosphere

**What the air is made of.** — Dry air is about 78 per cent nitrogen, 21 per cent oxygen, and 1 per cent argon, with a few hundredths of a per cent of carbon dioxide and traces of other gases. Water vapour is the wild card, ranging from almost nothing over a desert to four per cent in the humid tropics, and it is the gas that moves most in the weather. The atmosphere thins with height but has no sharp top; about half its mass lies below 5.5 kilometres and 99 per cent below 30 kilometres. Pressure at sea level averages 1,013.25 hectopascals, the unit once called the millibar, and falls by half for every 5.5 kilometres climbed.

**Layers and the lapse rate.** — Almost all weather happens in the troposphere, the lowest layer, which reaches about 8 kilometres at the poles and 16 to 18 kilometres over the equator. Within it, temperature falls with height at an average of 6.5°C per kilometre — the environmental lapse rate — because the ground is heated by the sun and the air is heated from below. Above it the temperature levels off and then rises through the stratosphere, where ozone absorbs ultraviolet light and where jet airliners cruise to avoid the turbulence below. Higher still come the mesosphere and the thermosphere, where the aurora glows. The boundary between troposphere and stratosphere, the tropopause, is where the temperature stops falling; it is higher and colder over the tropics than over the poles, and it acts as a lid that keeps most water vapour below it.

## Insolation and the greenhouse effect

**The sun's energy.** — The earth intercepts sunlight at an average intensity of about 1,361 watts per square metre at the top of the atmosphere, the solar constant. Spread over the sphere's four-times-larger surface, the average is about 340 watts per square metre. About 30 per cent is reflected straight back to space by clouds, ice, and bright surfaces — the albedo — and the rest is absorbed by atmosphere, land, and ocean. Absorbed sunlight is eventually re-radiated as infrared, and the planet must lose as much energy as it gains or it would heat without limit. The balance of incoming and outgoing energy is the planet's energy budget.

**The blanket.** — Certain gases absorb outgoing infrared and re-radiate it in all directions, including back down, so the surface stays warmer than it would be with no atmosphere at all. This is the greenhouse effect, and it is natural. Joseph Fourier described the general principle in the 1820s; John Tyndall measured in 1859 how gases such as water vapour and carbon dioxide absorb infrared; Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 that doubling or halving carbon dioxide would change the surface temperature by several degrees. Without any greenhouse effect the earth's average surface temperature would be about −18°C; with it, about 15°C. Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, carbon dioxide the one most affected by human activity, and methane and nitrous oxide matter in smaller amounts.

**Why the equator is hot.** — Sunlight arrives as a beam, and the same energy spread over a larger area is weaker. Near the equator the sun climbs almost overhead at noon, so its rays strike the surface at a high angle and concentrate their energy; near the poles the sun stays low, the same beam is smeared across a much larger patch of ground, and it passes through more atmosphere, which scatters and absorbs part of it. Snow and ice at high latitudes also reflect most of the light that reaches them, so the poles lose energy faster than they gain it. The result is a surplus of energy in the tropics and a deficit near the poles, and the machinery of wind and current moves heat from the first to the second.

**The tilt and the seasons.** — The earth's axis leans about 23.4 degrees from the vertical, and that tilt, not the changing distance to the sun, makes the seasons. As the planet orbits, the northern and southern hemispheres take turns leaning toward the sun. At the solstices one pole is tilted most directly toward the light; at the equinoxes the sun is overhead at the equator and day and night are nearly equal everywhere. The sun is actually closest to the earth in early January, during the northern winter — a reminder that distance is not the cause.

## Air pressure and winds

**Pressure and its gradients.** — Air pressure is the weight of the column of air above a point. It is not uniform: warm, less dense air rises and lowers the pressure at the surface, while cold, dense air sinks and raises it. Wind is simply air moving from higher to lower pressure, pushed by the pressure-gradient force and fastest where the gradient is steep. Isobars — lines of equal pressure on a map — crowd together where winds are strong. Evangelista Torricelli built the first mercury barometer in 1643, and in 1648 his colleague Blaise Pascal had a barometer carried up the Puy de Dôme in France and saw the mercury fall with altitude, which showed that the atmosphere has weight and a top.

**How wind turns.** — On a non-rotating planet wind would blow straight down the pressure gradient. On this one it is deflected. The deflection is the Coriolis effect, named after the French engineer Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who set out the mathematics of motion in a rotating frame in 1835. Moving air is turned to the right of its path in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, and the turning is stronger at high latitudes and for fast winds, and vanishes at the equator. The rule that wind blows with low pressure on the left in the northern hemisphere is Buys Ballot's law, stated by the Dutch meteorologist Christophorus Buys Ballot in 1857.

**Cyclones and anticyclones.** — Because of the turning, air does not flow straight into a low. It spirals inward, counter-clockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern, and where it converges it rises. That rising air cools, its vapour condenses, and clouds and rain form: a low is usually wet and stormy. Around a high, air sinks and spirals outward, the sense reversed, and descending air warms and dries, so a high is usually clear.

## The global circulation

**Hadley's cell.** — In 1735 the English meteorologist George Hadley proposed that the trade winds are the surface return of a great convection cell. Air heated at the equator rises, flows poleward aloft, cools, sinks around 30° latitude, and returns along the surface toward the equator, deflected westward by the rotation. The rising belt is the intertropical convergence zone, the ITCZ, a band of cloud and heavy rain that migrates north and south with the seasons; the calm at its centre is the doldrums, and the sinking belt near 30° is the horse latitudes. The surface winds of the cell blow from the northeast in the northern hemisphere and the southeast in the southern: the trade winds.

**Ferrel's correction and the three cells.** — Hadley's simple cell could not explain everything. In 1856 the American meteorologist William Ferrel showed that a fourth force, arising from the atmosphere's east–west motion combined with the earth's rotation, shifts the belts, raises the pressure near 28° latitude, and lowers it near the poles. The mid-latitude cell that bears his name, the Ferrel cell, is now understood differently from a simple convection loop: it is a thermally indirect cell, driven not by direct heating but by the eddies of travelling cyclones and the poleward transport of heat and momentum they carry. Poleward of about 60° lies the polar cell, with cold dense air sinking over the pole and flowing equatorward as the polar easterlies. The three-cell picture — Hadley, Ferrel, polar — is a useful idealisation of a much messier reality, strongest over the uniform ocean and badly distorted over continents.

**Westerlies and the jets.** — Between the trade winds and the polar easterlies blow the prevailing westerlies of the middle latitudes, the winds that carried sailing ships eastward and gave the southern ocean its Roaring Forties and Furious Fifties. Along the boundaries between the cells run the jet streams: fast, narrow rivers of air near the tropopause, the subtropical jet near 30° and the polar-front jet near 60°, meandering in great waves that steer the surface weather. The polar front, where cold polar air meets warm subtropical air, is where most mid-latitude storms are born.

**Why it rains where it rains.** — The circulation explains the map of rainfall. Rain is heaviest where air rises: at the equator, where the trades converge and the ITCZ sits, and along the storm tracks of the middle latitudes. Rain is scarce where air sinks: in the horse latitudes near 30°, where the great deserts lie — the Sahara, the Arabian, the Kalahari, the Australian, and, on the western margins of continents, the Atacama and the Namib. The pattern is not perfectly symmetrical, because land and sea heat differently, and the monsoons of Asia and West Africa reverse the system seasonally. But the broad answer to why the equator is hot is that the sun is high there; the answer to why it rains there is that the hot air rises.

## Ocean currents and the thermohaline circulation

**Surface currents.** — Winds drag the surface of the ocean with them, and the Coriolis effect turns the moving water, so the great currents circulate in gyres: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, counter-clockwise in the southern. On the western side of each ocean basin the currents are narrow, deep, and fast — the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio, the Brazil Current, the East Australian — and on the eastern side they are broad, shallow, and cold, such as the California and the Peru, or Humboldt, Current. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current is the largest by volume, moving perhaps 125 million cubic metres a second.

**The great conveyor.** — Beneath the wind-driven surface lies a slower circulation driven by density. Cold water and salty water are denser, so at high latitudes where the surface is chilled and sea ice formation leaves salt behind, water sinks. It forms deep water in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica, creeps along the ocean floor for centuries, and rises again in slow upwelling. The circuit is called the thermohaline circulation, or global conveyor belt, and a full loop takes about a thousand years. The American oceanographer Henry Stommel worked out much of its physics in the mid-twentieth century, and Wallace Broecker popularised the conveyor image in the 1980s.

**Why the current matters.** — The Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carry tropical heat toward Europe, which is why Bergen and Dublin are milder in winter than Labrador at the same latitude. The conveyor also moves carbon and nutrients and ventilates the deep sea. Its strength is not fixed: evidence from Greenland ice cores and North Atlantic sediments suggests the circulation weakened abruptly during the last ice age, and the cold snap called the Younger Dryas, about 12,900 years ago, may have been triggered by a flood of fresh water that shut down deep-water formation. Whether the circulation will weaken this century is an active and contested question.

## The water cycle

**Reservoirs and fluxes.** — Water moves among the ocean, the atmosphere, the land, and living things in a continuous cycle. The sun's heat evaporates water from the sea and, through the leaves of plants, from the land in a process called transpiration. The vapour rises, cools, and condenses into cloud; the cloud releases it as rain or snow; the water runs off the land in rivers, soaks into the ground, or is taken up again by plants. The atmosphere holds only about 13,000 cubic kilometres of water at any moment — a thousandth of the ocean's stock — but it turns over every nine days, and roughly half a million cubic kilometres fall as precipitation each year. Almost nine-tenths of that falls back on the ocean.

**Where the rain comes from.** — Most rain over land is water evaporated from the ocean, but some is recycled: the Amazon basin recycles a large share of its own rainfall as water passes from soil to tree to air and back, which is one reason clearing forest there is expected to reduce rainfall downwind. Ice and groundwater are the slow reservoirs: glaciers lock up water for millennia, and the ice ages raised and lowered sea level by more than 100 metres.

## Clouds and rain

**How a cloud forms.** — A cloud is visible water: countless tiny droplets or ice crystals suspended in air. It forms when air rises, expands, and cools. Rising air cools at about 10°C per kilometre while it stays unsaturated — the dry adiabatic rate — and more slowly once condensation begins and releases latent heat, at about 6°C per kilometre. When the air reaches its dew point, vapour condenses on tiny particles called condensation nuclei — dust, sea salt, pollen, smoke. Without those specks, water vapour can cool far below its dew point and still not condense.

**Naming the clouds.** — In 1802 the English chemist and pharmacist Luke Howard published an essay that gave clouds the names still in use: cirrus for the wispy, high, ice-crystal clouds; cumulus for the heaped, cottony ones; stratus for the flat layered sheets; and nimbus for rain-bearing cloud. Combinations followed — cumulonimbus for the towering thunderstorm, cirrostratus for the veil that haloes the sun. The classification rests on shape and height, which correspond to how the air moves: cumulus grows where air rises in columns, stratus where it spreads in layers, cirrus where it is thin and high.

**Three kinds of rain.** — Rain falls when air rises, and there are three common ways to make it rise. Convectional rain comes from local heating: the ground warms, air bubbles upward, and by afternoon a thunderstorm drops heavy rain, which is why the equatorial belt has an afternoon shower almost daily. Frontal rain comes from the meeting of air masses along a front, where warm air is lifted over cold; it is the steady rain of a warm front and the violent squall line of a cold front, typical of the middle latitudes. Relief, or orographic, rain comes from air forced up a mountain slope; the windward side is drenched and the leeward side, in the rain shadow, is dry. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills of northeast India, on the windward side of the Bay of Bengal's monsoon, are among the wettest places on earth; the Atacama Desert, west of the Andes, is among the driest, because the same mountains that wring out the rain on their eastern flank leave the western coast in their shadow.

**Air masses and fronts.** — An air mass is a large body of air that takes on the temperature and humidity of the surface beneath it: maritime tropical air is warm and moist, continental polar air is cold and dry. When two masses meet, the boundary is a front. The Norwegian meteorologists Vilhelm Bjerknes and his son Jacob Bjerknes, working at Bergen during the First World War with Halvor Solberg and Tor Bergeron, built the model of the polar front and its cyclones that still structures the weather map: a warm front where warm air rides up over cold, a cold front where cold air wedges under warm, and an occluded front where the cold front catches the warm one. Their insight was that a cyclone is not a random storm but a wave on the polar front, and that it has a life cycle.

## Köppen and the classification of climates

**The first global map.** — In 1884 the German-Russian climatologist Wladimir Köppen published a map dividing the world into climate zones by the plants that grow in them, and he refined the system for the rest of his long life, issuing the full scheme in 1918 and again in 1936 with his son-in-law Rudolf Geiger. Köppen's five great groups are labelled by letter: A for tropical, B for dry, C for temperate, D for continental, and E for polar; further letters mark rainfall and temperature, so that a place can be tagged Af (tropical rainforest), BWh (hot desert), Cfb (oceanic), Dfb (warm-summer continental), or ET (tundra). The scheme is empirical: its boundaries were drawn where the vegetation changes, not derived from atmospheric physics.

**What the scheme does and does not do.** — Köppen's system is simple, uses data that many stations already had, and remains the most widely used climate classification. It is also a snapshot: its boundaries are arbitrary at the edges, it says nothing about why a climate is what it is, and a warming world is shifting the zones faster than the map is redrawn. Rival schemes exist: the American geographer C. Warren Thornthwaite proposed in the 1930s a classification based on the balance of precipitation and evaporation, closer to what plants experience.

## El Niño and the Southern Oscillation

**Walker's see-saw.** — In the 1920s the English physicist Gilbert Walker, working for the Indian Meteorological Department, searched the records for a way to predict the monsoon. He found a see-saw of atmospheric pressure between the eastern Pacific and the Indian Ocean–Indonesia region: when pressure was high at Tahiti it tended to be low at Darwin, and the reverse. He called it the Southern Oscillation and showed it correlated with monsoon rainfall and with distant weather. He had found a pattern without a cause.

**Bjerknes and the coupled ocean.** — The cause came in the 1960s from the Norwegian-American meteorologist Jacob Bjerknes, who linked the pressure see-saw to the temperature of the equatorial Pacific. Normally the trade winds pile warm water in the west and draw cold, nutrient-rich water up along the Peruvian coast, and the rising air over the warm pool drives a circulation named after Walker. In an El Niño year the trades slacken or reverse, warm water sloshes eastward, the upwelling fails, and the rains move with the warm pool — drought in Indonesia and Australia, floods in Peru and Ecuador. The name comes from Peruvian fishermen who noticed the warm current arriving around Christmas and called it El Niño, the Christ child. Its opposite, the cool phase, is La Niña. Together the ocean and atmosphere flip between states in a coupled cycle now called ENSO, and its effects reach around the world through teleconnections — the 1982–83 event brought drought to Australia and floods to the Americas, and the 1997–98 event was among the strongest recorded.

## Natural and human influences on climate

**Natural causes.** — Climate has always changed. The Serbian mathematician Milutin Milanković showed in the 1920s that slow variations in the earth's orbit — the shape of the ellipse, the tilt of the axis, and the wobble of the axis — change the distribution of sunlight by latitude and season on cycles of about 100,000, 41,000, and 23,000 years that pace the ice ages. The sun's own output varies slightly. Volcanoes can cool the planet for a year or two by throwing sulphate aerosols into the stratosphere: Tambora in 1815 caused the "year without a summer" in 1816, and Pinatubo in 1991 lowered global temperatures by about half a degree.

**Human causes.** — Since the industrial revolution, burning coal, oil, and gas has added carbon dioxide to the air, and clearing forest and draining peat have added more; the concentration has risen from about 280 parts per million before 1800 to over 420 today. The American scientist Charles David Keeling began measuring carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa in Hawaii in 1958, and the rising "Keeling curve" is the single most cited record of the change. Methane from agriculture, nitrous oxide from fertiliser, and the soot and sulphate aerosols of industry also shift the balance; aerosols cool by reflecting sunlight, masking part of the warming. In 1988 the World Meteorological Organization and the United Nations Environment Programme created the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, whose first assessment appeared in 1990.

**A shifting debate.** — The question is no longer whether the climate is changing but how much of it is human and how fast the consequences arrive. In 1878 John Wesley Powell, reporting on the arid lands of the American West, could still write that the operations of people on the surface of the earth are "so trivial" that their effects escape discernment beside the grand effects of nature; that was a defensible reading of the evidence then, and it is not one now. The American physicist James Hansen told a congressional committee in 1988 that the warming was already detectable, and his work since has argued that the rise in greenhouse gases is the main cause and that cutting the shorter-lived gases could slow the change. Others dispute the pace, the sensitivity of the climate to carbon dioxide, and the costs of action; the disagreement is real, but it is about magnitude and policy, not about whether the atmosphere responds to what is put into it.

- **2015–16** — Another strong El Niño, with record global temperatures.

## Questions to carry

- Why is the equator hot, and why does that heat make rain rather than merely warmth?
- Hadley's single cell and Ferrel's three-cell picture disagree. What did each explain, and what did each leave out?
- The deserts of the world sit near 30° latitude, but the Atacama and the Namib sit on western coasts. What does the difference tell you about relief, currents, and the limits of a general rule?
- Köppen drew his climate boundaries where the vegetation changed. What kind of explanation is that, and what kind is it not?
- Powell in 1878 judged human influence on rainfall trivial; Hansen in 1988 judged it decisive. What changed — the evidence, the instruments, or the scale of the effect?
