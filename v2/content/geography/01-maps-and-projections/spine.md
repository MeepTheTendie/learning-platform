# Maps and projections

**Region:** The whole earth · **Theme:** What a map shows and hides

## The map in the hand

**A flat picture of a round earth.** — A map is a flat drawing of some part of the earth's surface, made to a chosen scale and using agreed symbols. It cannot be complete. A map is a reduction: it takes a curved, three-dimensional, endlessly detailed surface and lays it on a plane. In the process the mapmaker settles three questions — how much to shrink the ground, how to flatten the sphere, and which marks to use — and every answer is a decision about what to keep and what to throw away. A road atlas keeps the roads and leaves out the trees. A political map keeps the borders and leaves out the hills. The organizing question is simple and hard: what does a given map let you see, and what does it hide?

**What a map leaves out is a choice, not an accident.** — The Ordnance Survey's one-inch maps of nineteenth-century Britain show parish boundaries and turnpike roads, because the state that paid for them wanted to tax land and move troops. A modern hiking map of the same ground reverses the emphasis: footpaths, contour lines, and walls appear, while the administrative boundaries that once dominated the sheet shrink to faint lines or vanish. The ground has not changed. The purpose of the map has.

**One map, one argument: the London Tube.** — The clearest case is the map of the London Underground. In 1931 Harry Beck, a draughtsman in the Underground's signals office, redrew the network using only horizontal, vertical, and forty-five-degree lines. His map abandoned true geography almost entirely. Distances are false; the centre of London is stretched and the outskirts are squeezed; the only geographical feature left is a stylised River Thames. Beck's map is a diagram of connections, not a picture of the city, and it answers a single question — which line, and where do I change? — while hiding everything that would get in the way of the answer.

**The map that would be the world.** — The point has been made by satirists. In a one-paragraph story published in 1946, Jorge Luis Borges described an empire whose cartographers made a map so exact that it covered the empire entirely; later generations, he wrote, abandoned it to the sun and the weather. Lewis Carroll had imagined the opposite: the Bellman's chart in *The Hunting of the Snark* (1876) is "a perfect and absolute blank." Alfred Korzybski's phrase of 1931, "the map is not the territory," names the limit. A useful map is never the ground; it is a selected, simplified model.

## Scale, legend, and symbol

**Scale.** — Scale is the ratio between a distance on the map and the same distance on the ground, usually written as a fraction: on a 1:50,000 map, one centimetre on the paper stands for 50,000 centimetres — half a kilometre — on the ground. The vocabulary trips people up. A *large-scale* map covers a small area in great detail, because the fraction is large (1:1,250 is larger than 1:250,000); a *small-scale* map covers a continent and shows little detail. A scale bar lets a reader measure distance without arithmetic, and it survives photocopying that a written fraction may not. The Ordnance Survey's Explorer series is drawn at 1:25,000 and its Landranger series at 1:50,000; the difference is the difference between a map on which a field is a visible shape and one on which it is a speck.

**The legend.** — No map explains itself. The key, or legend, is the small dictionary that tells the reader what the marks mean, and it is the part of the map that most plainly reveals the mapmaker's mind. A dashed line may mean a footpath, a boundary, or a projected railway; a shaded patch may mean woodland, marsh, or a built-up area. Before contour lines were introduced in the nineteenth century, relief was shown by hachures — short strokes drawn down the slope. Each convention is a decision, and each tells the reader what the mapmaker expected them to need.

**The Ordnance Survey.** — The most influential state mapping project in the English-speaking world began with a war scare. The Board of Ordnance founded the Ordnance Survey in 1791, during the French Revolutionary Wars, to produce accurate maps of southern England in case of invasion. The work rested on triangulation, perfected by the Scottish soldier William Roy in his military survey of Scotland after the 1745 rising. In 1784 Roy measured a baseline on Hounslow Heath with a theodolite built by Jesse Ramsden, and from that line the Principal Triangulation of Britain spread across the country between 1783 and 1853. The first sheet, a one-inch map of Kent, appeared in 1801. The survey later moved to Southampton; its one-inch series became the standard portrait of Britain, and in 1936 it introduced the National Grid, a single coordinate system that replaced the patchwork of county origins. The Ordnance Survey is a state map, and its history is a history of what the state wanted to know.

**Symbols carry values.** — What a map chooses to mark is a statement about what matters. A colonial-era map of an African territory might mark missions, mines, and administrative posts and leave existing settlements and trade routes unmarked; a tourist map of the same region marks hotels and viewpoints. Neither is a lie in the narrow sense; both are arguments about which features constitute the place.

## The graticule

**Latitude and longitude.** — The graticule is the grid of imaginary lines that lets any point be given a pair of numbers. Parallels of latitude run east–west and measure distance north or south of the equator, from 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles. Meridians of longitude run pole to pole and measure distance east or west of an agreed starting line, the prime meridian. Latitude is comparatively easy to find: at night the height of the pole star above the horizon gives the observer's latitude, and by day the height of the noon sun does the same with a little arithmetic. Longitude is the hard one.

**The longitude problem.** — Longitude is a problem in time. The earth turns 360° in twenty-four hours, so one hour of time difference between two places equals 15° of longitude. A navigator who knows the local time and the time at a reference meridian can find the difference, and so the longitude. The difficulty is keeping the reference time at sea. In 1714 the British Parliament passed the Longitude Act, offering a large reward for a practical method; the Board of Longitude it created eventually rewarded John Harrison, a Yorkshire carpenter and clockmaker, whose fourth timekeeper, H4, kept time well enough on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 to fix longitude to within about ten nautical miles. A rival method used the moon's position against the stars, made usable by the *Nautical Almanac*, first published under Nevil Maskelyne in 1767. The graticule on a sea chart is only as trustworthy as the longitudes behind it, and before the eighteenth century most were guesses.

**Where zero is.** — The prime meridian is a convention, and conventions are political. Ptolemy measured longitude from the Fortunate Islands, generally identified with the Canaries, at what he took to be the western edge of the world. Later European mapmakers used Ferro, Paris, or their own capitals. In October 1884 an International Meridian Conference in Washington, with delegates from twenty-five nations, resolved that the meridian of the Royal Observatory at Greenwich should be the world's prime meridian. The vote passed by a large majority, with France abstaining. The choice of zero changed nothing about the earth; it changed what every coordinate on every map would say.

**Ptolemy's table.** — The oldest surviving systematic use of the graticule is the *Geography* of Claudius Ptolemy, an astronomer working in Alexandria in the second century CE. Ptolemy's book is not really a picture; it is a gazetteer, a table of some eight thousand places, each given a latitude and a longitude. His world is recognisably the Mediterranean and its hinterland, but distorted: he stretched the known world too far east, so that the longitude of China is wildly overstated, and he had no idea of the Americas or the Pacific. The *Geography* was lost to western Europe for a thousand years, rediscovered in the Renaissance, and reprinted with new maps.

## The problem of flattening a sphere

**You cannot flatten an orange.** — A sphere cannot be flattened onto a plane without distortion. This is not a failure of skill or of instruments; it is a mathematical fact. In 1827 Carl Friedrich Gauss proved what is now called the Theorema Egregium, the "remarkable theorem": the curvature of a surface is intrinsic, built into its geometry, and cannot be changed by bending it without stretching or tearing. A globe can be peeled only by tearing the peel or by stretching it, and a map of the earth's surface is a systematic record of where the mapmaker chose to stretch.

**Four things a map might keep.** — Because something must give, mapmakers choose which property to protect. An *equal-area* (equivalent) projection keeps areas in correct proportion, so that a country twice the size of another looks twice as large. A *conformal* projection keeps local angles and shapes correct, so that small features look like themselves and a compass bearing is preserved. An *equidistant* projection keeps distances correct along certain lines, usually the meridians or the equator. A map cannot be all three at once; conformal and equal-area are mutually exclusive on a flat map of the whole sphere.

**Tissot's ellipses.** — The standard way to see distortion was devised by the French mathematician Nicolas Auguste Tissot in the 1850s and elaborated in 1881. Imagine drawing a small circle on the globe. On any flat map it becomes an ellipse, and the size and shape of that ellipse measure how much the map has stretched or squashed the ground at that point. On an equal-area map every ellipse has the same area. On a conformal map every ellipse is a circle. On a Mercator map the ellipses grow enormously toward the poles and become long from north to south, which is why Greenland and Antarctica swell on that projection.

## Mercator and the sea

**Mercator.** — Gerardus Mercator was the Latinised name of Gerard Kremer (1512–1594), a Flemish engraver and instrument-maker who worked in Louvain and later in Duisburg. In 1569 he published a large world map, printed from eighteen copper plates and titled *Nova et Aucta Orbis Terrae Descriptio ad Usum Navigantium Emendate Accommodata* — "a new and more complete representation of the terrestrial globe, properly adapted for the use of navigators." The title states the purpose exactly. Mercator was not trying to draw the world as it would look from space; he was trying to make a chart on which a ship's course could be laid out with a ruler.

**What the projection does.** — Mercator's method was to increase the spacing of the parallels as they approach the poles. Near the equator the spacing is nearly uniform; toward the poles the parallels are pushed farther and farther apart, in proportion to the secant of the latitude. The result is that at every point the scale is the same in all directions — the map is conformal — and local shapes are preserved. The cost is that area is wildly exaggerated at high latitudes. On Mercator's map the north–south distance from the equator to 60° is more than doubled; by 80° it is stretched nearly sixfold.

**Rhumb lines and the compass.** — The payoff is for the navigator. A *rhumb line*, or loxodrome, is a course that crosses every meridian at the same angle — a constant compass bearing. On a globe it spirals toward the pole and is not a great circle, a fact described by the Portuguese mathematician Pedro Nunes in 1537. On Mercator's chart, and only there, a rhumb line appears as a straight line. A navigator can therefore draw a straight line from departure to destination, read the bearing from the compass rose, and hold that course, and the ship will arrive. Mercator was not the first to exploit the idea: Erhard Etzlaub's *Rom Weg* road map of about 1500 already spaced its parallels for the purpose. The mathematics was not published until 1599, when the English mathematician Edward Wright set out the table of meridional parts in *Certaine Errors in Navigation*. Wright's book is the bridge between Mercator's practical chart and the mathematical projection that took his name.

**The lie on land.** — The same property that makes Mercator a good sea chart makes it a misleading world map. Greenland, about 2.2 million square kilometres, appears larger than Africa, about 30.4 million square kilometres — roughly fourteen times its true size. Antarctica becomes an infinite white wall along the bottom edge. The distortion grows without limit toward the poles, so no map of the whole earth on Mercator can show the poles at all. For centuries the projection was nevertheless the default world map in classrooms and atlases, which is why generations grew up with a mental image of the world in which the high latitudes were too large and the tropics too small.

**Web Mercator.** — The projection has had an unexpected second life. When online mapping services appeared after 2005, most adopted a variant called Web Mercator, in which coordinates are projected as if the earth were a sphere even though positions are recorded on an ellipsoid. It is convenient for tiling and panning, and it now underlies an enormous share of all maps produced. The most widely seen maps ever made carry the same high-latitude exaggeration as Mercator's chart of 1569, usually without any statement of it.

## Equal area and the Peters controversy

**Equal-area maps.** — The alternative to conformality is to keep areas true. Equal-area projections are older than Mercator's fame: the sinusoidal projection was used by Mercator himself in the 1570s, and in 1805 the German astronomer Karl Mollweide published the oval world projection that still carries his name. Others followed, including the Hammer projection of 1892 and the Albers conic of 1805. These maps preserve the relative size of countries, which makes them indispensable for mapping anything distributed over the whole earth — population, forests, rainfall, disease. The price is shape. On a Mollweide map the continents near the edges are stretched and slanted, and the poles are compressed into points; the world looks as though it has been printed on the inside of an egg. The American space agency uses Mollweide for many global images precisely because area is what matters there.

**Gall and Peters.** — The most public argument about projections in the twentieth century concerned a map that was not new. In 1855 the Scottish clergyman James Gall published an equal-area projection with straight, parallel parallels and meridians, now called the Gall orthographic. In 1974 a German historian and filmmaker, Arno Peters, launched a projection identical to Gall's, presented it as his own invention, and argued that it was the only fair map of the world because it gave the equatorial and southern regions their true area, in contrast to the Mercator map that had flattered Europe and North America. The area point was real: Mercator does exaggerate the north. But the projection was Gall's, 119 years older, and its equal area is bought at a heavy cost in shape — the equatorial regions are stretched vertically and the northern continents are squashed. Cartographers pointed this out, and a dispute followed through the 1980s and 1990s that was as much political as mathematical. Several North American geographical organisations passed resolutions against rectangular world maps with severe distortion. The episode is a clean demonstration that a projection is never a purely technical choice.

**The National Geographic case.** — Institutions have changed their world maps, and the changes are arguments. The National Geographic Society used the Mercator projection for its world maps from 1922. In 1988 it abandoned Mercator for the compromise projection designed by Arthur H. Robinson in 1963, and in 1998 it changed again to the Winkel Tripel projection. Each switch was explained in the magazine as a response to readers and to the society's sense of what a world map should show. The map was the same earth; the statement differed.

## Thematic maps

**The map as an argument.** — A thematic map shows the distribution of a single subject — disease, votes, rainfall, wealth. The classic case is the map of cholera deaths that John Snow published after the Soho outbreak in London in 1854. Snow marked each death with a short black bar on a street plan of the neighbourhood and saw the bars cluster around the water pump in Broad Street. The map helped him argue that cholera was spread by water, not by bad air, and the pump handle was removed. Historians now treat the story carefully: Snow's argument rested on much more than the map, the map was drawn after the outbreak had already declined, and the removal of the handle may not have been the turning point that legend makes it. What the map did was make a pattern visible that a table of addresses did not.

**Minard's flow map.** — In 1869 the French engineer Charles Joseph Minard published a flow map of Napoleon's Russian campaign of 1812. One band shows the army advancing on Moscow and shrinking as men are lost; a second shows the retreat; a line below records the temperature day by day. Six variables — place, time, direction, army size, temperature, and the river crossings — are shown in one image. The statistician Edward Tufte later called it perhaps the best statistical graphic ever drawn. It is also a piece of rhetoric: Minard was a critic of the war, and the map is built to make the cost visible.

**Choropleth and its traps.** — The most common thematic map is the choropleth, in which areas are shaded by a value. Whether the value is a count or a rate changes everything: a map of the number of disease cases will simply track population, while a map of cases per thousand people will not. The choice of class breaks — where the shading changes from one shade to the next — can make the same data look calm or alarming. And because the units are fixed in advance, the pattern can depend on the boundaries rather than on the underlying reality. Stan Openshaw and Peter Taylor named this the modifiable areal unit problem in 1979: the same data, grouped into different sets of areas, can produce different-looking maps. A related trap is the ecological fallacy, described by William S. Robinson in 1950, the mistake of reading a relationship between areas as a relationship between the individuals inside them. A cartogram answers the problem by redrawing each area in proportion to its value — a country's size becomes its population, or its emissions, or its votes — so that the map stops lying about magnitude.

## Every map is an argument

**Harley and the map as text.** — In 1989 the British geographer J. B. Harley published an essay, "Deconstructing the Map," that changed how many people thought about cartography. Harley argued that maps are not neutral records of fact but texts that carry and reproduce power. Following Michel Foucault, he treated cartography as a form of knowledge tied to authority, and he urged readers to look for what maps silence — the places and peoples left off, the features made invisible — as well as for what they show. The essay has its critics, who say that Harley imported literary theory into a practical craft and sometimes read politics into decisions that were merely technical. But his central point, that a map has a point of view, is now common ground.

**Monmonier and the necessity of distortion.** — The American geographer Mark Monmonier made the same case from the technical side. In *How to Lie with Maps* (1991) he argued that distortion is not an occasional abuse but a requirement: because a map must reduce, generalise, and choose, it cannot avoid shaping the truth. His book's most quoted line — that not only is it easy to lie with maps, it is essential — is a claim about the nature of maps, not a licence to deceive. Monmonier's point is that a reader who does not know the choices behind a map cannot judge it, and that the honest mapmaker states scale, projection, source, and date.

**Robinson and the compromise.** — Arthur H. Robinson spent his working life on the problem of how maps communicate. His book *The Look of Maps* (1952) argued that map design should be studied empirically — by asking how readers actually perceive symbols — rather than settled by taste. In 1963 the map company Rand McNally asked him to design a world projection that looked right to the eye and avoided the worst distortions of both Mercator and the equal-area maps. His answer, the Robinson projection, is neither equal-area nor conformal but a compromise, with the poles drawn as straight lines rather than points. Robinson later said he had chosen its parameters partly by eye, adjusting them until the world looked as it should. The projection that replaced Mercator at National Geographic in 1988 was therefore a design decision as much as a mathematical one.

**The question again.** — A map is a picture made for a purpose, and the purpose can be read from what it shows and what it hides. The scale tells the reader how much detail to expect; the projection tells them which distortion to allow for; the symbols tell them what the mapmaker thought important; the omissions tell them who and what did not count. The organizing question is not a trick. It is the method. Ask of any map: what does this let you see, and what does it hide?

## Questions to carry

- A map must reduce, generalise, and choose. Which of those three does the most to shape what a map shows, and why?
- Mercator's projection was built for sailors and then used for classrooms. What does that change of use tell us about how a map's purpose can be separated from its effect?
- Snow's cholera map is often credited with stopping an epidemic. What did the map actually do, and what did the argument around it do?
- Equal-area, conformal, and compromise projections each give up something. Is there a neutral way to decide which loss is acceptable, or is the decision always political?
- If every map is an argument, how should a reader tell the difference between a map that states its purpose and one that hides it?
