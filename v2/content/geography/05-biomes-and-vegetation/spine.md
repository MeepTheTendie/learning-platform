# Biomes and vegetation

**Region:** The whole earth

## Two worlds on one line of latitude

**The Tropic of Cancer, and two answers.** — The Tropic of Cancer circles the earth at 23.5 degrees north. It crosses the Sahara, where parts of the desert record less than one millimetre of rain in a year, and it crosses the Arakan coast of Myanmar, where the south-west monsoon delivers more than five thousand millimetres. The sunlight is nearly the same on both: latitude fixes the noon angle of the sun, and little else. What latitude does not fix is the movement of the air. At the equator the surface heats, the warm air rises, cools as it climbs, and releases its moisture as rain; this rising belt, the Intertropical Convergence Zone, is why the equatorial forests of the Amazon, the Congo, and Borneo are wet. The air that rose at the equator flows poleward, descends around thirty degrees north and south, and warms as it sinks, so it holds water rather than shedding it. That descending limb, the subtropical high, sits over the Sahara, Arabia, the Kalahari, and the deserts of Australia and the American south-west. Latitude sets the stage; the circulation of the atmosphere writes the play. The rest of this unit is the detail: how climate and soil together make the great plant communities called biomes.

## What a biome is

**A community defined by climate.** — A biome is a large region of the land surface defined by its climate and by the plants and animals adapted to it. The word came into wide use through the American botanist Frederic Clements, who employed it in *Plant Succession* (1916) for a community shaped by climate; the idea is older. Alexander von Humboldt, travelling in Spanish America from 1799 to 1804 with the botanist Aimé Bonpland, observed that vegetation recurred in belts that followed temperature and moisture rather than continents, and his *Essay on the Geography of Plants* (1807) set out to map those belts. The German botanist A. F. W. Schimper, in *Plant-Geography upon a Physiological Basis* (1898), and the Danish botanist Eugen Warming, in *Plantesamfund* (1895; in English as *Oecology of Plants*, 1909), turned the observation into a science of plant communities.

**Mapping the world's climates.** — Because biomes track climate, climatologists have tried to draw climate on a map. The Russian-German climatologist Wladimir Köppen published the first version of his classification in 1900 and refined it for decades; it sorts climates by temperature and rainfall and by the season in which the rain falls, and its letters — Af for the tropical rainforest, BWh for the hot desert, Csa for the Mediterranean climate — are still in use. The American ecologist Robert Whittaker, in *Communities and Ecosystems* (1970), drew the diagram that most students still meet: mean annual temperature on one axis, mean annual precipitation on the other, and the biomes falling into fields on the grid.

**Convergence, and the same biome twice.** — The same biome appears on continents separated for tens of millions of years, and its plants are often unrelated. The shrubs of the Mediterranean basin, California, central Chile, the Cape of Good Hope, and south-western Australia are not close relatives, yet all are evergreen, small-leaved, and fire-adapted; this is convergent evolution, the repeated invention of similar solutions to the same problem. The five regions share a climate found on the western edge of continents between about thirty and forty degrees latitude: wet, mild winters and hot, dry summers.

**A contested idea.** — Clements held that a biome was a "climax formation," a stable end-state toward which vegetation developed and which behaved almost like a single organism. The American botanist Henry Gleason disagreed. In papers of 1917 and 1926 he argued that a plant association is not an organism but a coincidence — each species responding to its own tolerances, arriving and departing on its own. Most ecologists now accept Gleason's individualistic view, but "biome" survives as a coarse and useful scale at which climate and life plainly correspond.

## Climate: why one latitude differs from another

**The circulation of the air.** — The sun heats the tropics most, and the atmosphere carries the surplus toward the poles. Warm, moist air rises near the equator, forms towering cumulus, and drops heavy rain; the same air, now dry, sinks at about thirty degrees, so the world's great deserts lie in two belts astride the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. Between the desert belts and the poles run the mid-latitude westerlies, which carry cyclonic rain and give the temperate zones their moisture. The belts shift with the seasons: in July the Intertropical Convergence Zone moves north, drenching the Sahel and the Indian subcontinent; in January it moves south, and the savannas of the southern hemisphere turn green. A place's rainfall is therefore not a constant but a rhythm, and the rhythm shapes the vegetation.

**Rain shadow, currents, and distance from the sea.** — Mountains force air upward, so the windward slope is wet and the leeward slope dry. The Himalaya wring the monsoon dry before it reaches the Gobi; the Andes cast a rain shadow over Patagonia; the Sierra Nevada leaves Death Valley among the driest places in North America. Cold ocean currents chill the air above them, so it holds its moisture and gives fog rather than rain: the Humboldt Current helps make the Atacama of Chile the driest desert outside the poles, and the Benguela Current does the same for the Namib, whose plants and animals live on fog. Distance from the sea matters too, because water moderates temperature and supplies vapour; the interior of Eurasia, far from any ocean, has the sharpest seasonal extremes on earth.

**Altitude as compressed latitude.** — Temperature falls with height at about 6.5 degrees Celsius for every kilometre, so climbing a tropical mountain passes through the same sequence of climates as travelling toward the poles. Humboldt and Bonpland mapped the belts on Chimborazo in Ecuador and saw palms and tree ferns at the base, oaks and conifers in the middle, grasses above the trees, and permanent snow at the top. The treeline — the altitude beyond which trees cannot grow — marks the boundary between forest and alpine tundra. The same compression governs the sky islands of East Africa: Kilimanjaro rises from savanna through rainforest, heath, and alpine desert to a summit glacier, five zones stacked on one mountain. Those glaciers have shrunk by more than eighty per cent since 1912.

## The biomes of the land

**Tropical rainforest.** — Found within about ten degrees of the equator where rain exceeds roughly two thousand millimetres a year and no month is dry, the rainforest is the most complex of the biomes. Its structure is layered, and the layers are its architecture. At the top, scattered emergents such as the kapok tree push above the general roof; below them the continuous canopy closes at twenty-five to forty-five metres and holds most of the leaves, flowers, and fruit; beneath that, the understory of young trees and palms receives only a few per cent of the light; lower still a sparse shrub layer; and on the forest floor, in near-darkness, a thin litter that decomposes within weeks. Lianas climb the trunks, epiphytes — plants that grow on other plants without parasitising them — crowd the branches, strangler figs wrap their hosts, and many trees grow buttress roots and drip-tip leaves that shed water. The soils are old, deeply weathered, and poor in nutrients, because rain leaches them away; most of the nutrient capital is held in the living plants and recycled through fungi that link to roots. The Amazon alone may hold a tenth of all known species, and a single hectare can contain more tree species than the whole of Europe.

**Savanna.** — Tropical grassland with scattered trees, the savanna covers great areas of Africa, South America, Australia, and India where a wet season alternates with a long dry one. Grasses dominate because they grow fast in the rains and survive the drought below ground; the trees — acacia, baobab, eucalyptus — are fire- and drought-tolerant. Fire is a principal architect of the biome: dry-season fires sweep the grass, kill young trees, and keep the canopy open, while mature trees survive with thick corky bark or by resprouting from protected buds. Many savanna grasses use a photosynthetic pathway called C4, which is efficient in heat and drought, and the grazing herds that depend on them are the largest on earth; some 1.3 million wildebeest move across the Serengeti each year in step with the rains. Humboldt crossed the Venezuelan llanos, a South American savanna, and described plains so level that a traveller could not tell a hill from the horizon.

**Desert.** — Where rainfall falls below about 250 millimetres a year, vegetation thins to desert. Deserts are hot (the Sahara, the Sonoran) or cold (the Gobi, Patagonia), and their plants are specialists. Cacti and their African look-alikes, the euphorbias, store water in fleshy stems and open their pores at night to lose less to the sun, a trick called CAM photosynthesis; deep-rooted shrubs such as mesquite reach water far underground; and a class of short-lived plants, the ephemerals, spends most of the year as seed and flowers within days of rain. Animals escape the heat by burrowing and by feeding at night. The Sahara, at 9.2 million square kilometres the largest hot desert, receives under twenty-five millimetres in most places and under one millimetre in some.

**Mediterranean chaparral.** — The five Mediterranean-climate regions — the Mediterranean basin itself, California, central Chile, the Cape, and south-western Australia — carry a shrubland of evergreen, hard-leaved plants known by different names: maquis and garrigue, chaparral, matorral, fynbos, and kwongan. The plants are adapted to a summer drought and to fire. Many have small, waxy leaves that lose little water; some store seed in cones or capsules that open only when heated, a trait called serotiny; and many resprout from swollen underground buds after a fire.

**Temperate grassland.** — The prairies of North America, the pampas of Argentina, the steppes of Eurasia, and the veld of South Africa are grasslands of the mid-latitudes, too dry for forest and too wet for desert. Grasses such as big bluestem send roots two metres or more into the ground, building a deep, dark, crumbly soil called chernozem, or black earth, among the most fertile in the world. Fire and grazing, and the low rainfall, keep trees out. The North American prairie once supported some thirty million bison; by 1889 fewer than a thousand remained, and the tallgrass prairie has been reduced by more than ninety-five per cent, ploughed for wheat and corn. This is the biome that the plough would turn, in the 1930s, into the Dust Bowl.

**Temperate deciduous forest.** — In eastern North America, Europe, and East Asia, where rainfall is ample and winters are cold but not extreme, broad-leaved trees shed their leaves each autumn and grow them again in spring. Oak, beech, maple, and hickory dominate, and the forest floor blooms with spring ephemerals that flower before the canopy closes and cuts off the light. The soil, a brown forest soil richer than podzol but less rich than chernozem, supports the leaf litter that feeds the trees. The American chestnut once made up as much as a quarter of the canopy in the eastern forests; a fungal blight introduced around 1904 killed an estimated four billion trees within fifty years, and the species survives mainly as stump sprouts.

**Boreal forest (taiga).** — A belt of conifers circles the northern hemisphere between about fifty and seventy degrees north, across Canada, Scandinavia, and Siberia, and it is the largest land biome on earth, covering some fifteen million square kilometres. Spruce, fir, pine, and larch tolerate cold and a short growing season; their needle leaves resist drought and cold, and their conical shape sheds snow. Decomposition is slow in the cold, so the litter turns acidic and the rain leaches the soil into a grey, ash-like layer: the podzol. Fire is a normal part of the cycle, returning every fifty to two hundred years and releasing the nutrients locked in the litter. In the far north the soil below the surface stays frozen all year — permafrost — and the trees grow shallow and stunted above it.

**Tundra and alpine.** — Above the treeline, whether in the Arctic or high on a mountain, lies tundra: a treeless cover of low shrubs, sedges, mosses, and lichens growing on a short summer thaw above permafrost. The growing season is only a few weeks, and the plants are small and slow. Caribou and reindeer, muskox, and migrating birds use the tundra, and the frozen ground stores enormous quantities of carbon in dead plant matter that has never fully rotted. Where the permafrost thaws, that carbon can be released, and the ground can slump and collapse. Alpine tundra on tropical mountains — the páramo of the Andes, the moorland of Kilimanjaro — is colder than the latitude alone would suggest.

## Soils: the ground beneath the biome

**What soil is.** — Soil is a mixture of weathered mineral grains, dead and living organic matter, water, and air, arranged in layers called horizons. The topmost organic layer lies over a mineral layer darkened by humus, which lies over a subsoil enriched with clay or iron, which lies over broken parent rock. The American soil scientist Hans Jenny argued in *Factors of Soil Formation* (1941) that a soil is the product of five things: climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time. Climate is usually the strongest, which is why the map of soils looks so much like the map of biomes.

**Laterite.** — In the humid tropics, heavy rain and heat weather the rock deeply and wash away silica and bases, leaving a residue rich in iron and aluminium oxides. The result is laterite, named from the Latin *later*, a brick, by the Scottish surveyor Francis Buchanan-Hamilton, who described it in India in 1807. Fresh laterite can be cut and shaped, but it hardens on exposure to air, and the soil is poor in the nutrients that most crops need; farmers in the tropics depend on the rapid recycling of litter through the living plants rather than on the soil itself.

**Podzol.** — Under the conifers of the boreal forest, acidic litter and abundant rain combine to leach iron, aluminium, and organic matter downward out of the surface layer, leaving a bleached, ash-grey horizon above a dark, cemented subsoil. The name is Russian, *pod zola*, "under ash." Podzols are acid and infertile, and northern forests grow slowly on them.

**Chernozem.** — Under the temperate grasslands, where rain is enough for grass but not enough to leach the soil, the deep roots of the grasses die back each year and build a thick, dark surface layer of humus charged with calcium carbonate. This chernozem, Russian for "black earth," is among the most fertile soils on earth and made the Ukrainian and North American plains the granaries of the world. It is also fragile: when the grass is removed and the soil is exposed to wind, the same fertility blows away.

**Permafrost.** — In the tundra and the northern taiga, ground below the surface remains frozen for two or more years, and often for thousands. Permafrost locks in water and carbon, and its thaw waterlogs the surface, producing the patterned polygons and pingos of the Arctic. When it thaws under a warming climate, the ground subsides, roads and buildings buckle, and stored carbon escapes.

**Salt and black earth.** — Two soils show what people can do to the ground. Irrigation in a dry climate raises the water table, and evaporation then draws salts to the surface, poisoning the soil; this salinization helped undermine the irrigated fields of ancient Mesopotamia and has damaged land from the Colorado valley to the Aral Sea. In the Amazon, by contrast, pre-Columbian farmers made a dark, fertile soil called terra preta, or Amazonian dark earth, by adding charcoal and organic waste; patches of it are still richer than the surrounding laterite five centuries after the people who made it disappeared.

## Adaptations

**Plants.** — Every biome sorts its plants by the problems they must solve. In dry country, succulents store water, deep roots find it, and ephemerals outrun the drought by living as seed. In fire country, thick bark, resprouting buds, and serotinous cones turn burning into a means of reproduction. In cold country, needles and conical crowns shed snow, and dormancy carries the plant through winter. In the rainforest, competition for light drives trees upward, lianas climb, epiphytes perch on branches, and drip tips shed the rain. Each is a different answer to the same set of physical limits.

**Animals.** — Animals adapt in parallel. Some migrate to follow the rains and the grass, as the wildebeest of the Serengeti and the birds of the Arctic do; some hibernate or become torpid through the cold or the dry season; some burrow to escape the heat. The kangaroo rat of the North American desert extracts water from seeds, and the snowshoe hare changes colour with the season to match snow or soil. Two broad patterns, described by the German biologist Carl Bergmann in 1847 and the American naturalist Joel Asaph Allen in 1877, hold that warm-blooded animals tend to be larger and have shorter limbs and ears in cold climates, because a compact body loses less heat.

## Fire as an ecological force

**A natural process.** — Lightning has set fire to vegetation for hundreds of millions of years, and people have set it deliberately for tens of thousands. In grasslands and savannas, fire burns the dead grass, returns minerals to the soil, and prevents trees from closing the canopy; in chaparral and eucalyptus forest it triggers the release of stored seed; in the boreal forest it clears old stands and lets new ones grow. Many ecosystems are not damaged by fire but maintained by it.

**Suppression and its cost.** — When settlers and governments treated all fire as an enemy, they interrupted those cycles. In the United States, the Forest Service's campaign to put out every fire, hardened after the great fires of 1910, allowed dead wood and brush to accumulate across the western forests. Fires then burned hotter and larger, as at Yellowstone in 1988, when a third of the park burned. The American fire historian Stephen Pyne, in *Fire in America* (1982), argued that the removal of fire from fire-adapted landscapes is itself a form of ecological damage. Indigenous peoples had long used controlled burning to manage grasslands and forests — the practice the Australian anthropologist Rhys Jones called "fire-stick farming" in 1969 — and its revival is now part of land management.

## Biodiversity gradients

**More species near the equator.** — The number of species falls from the equator toward the poles. Tropical rainforest covers only about six or seven per cent of the land but holds perhaps half of all species; the Amazon basin alone may contain a tenth of them. No single explanation commands agreement. One holds that the tropics are a "museum," an old and stable region where species accumulate and few go extinct; another holds that they are a "cradle," where species are generated faster; a third points to the greater and more constant supply of energy from the sun. The gradient is one of the oldest observations in ecology — Humboldt noticed it — and one of the least settled.

**Hotspots and rarity.** — In 1988 the British conservation biologist Norman Myers and colleagues identified a set of "hotspots": regions with exceptional numbers of species found nowhere else and exceptional loss of habitat. A later list of twenty-five hotspots covers about 1.4 per cent of the land surface but contains a large share of all plant and vertebrate species. The Cape Floristic Region, south-western Australia, Madagascar, and the Atlantic Forest of Brazil are among them.

## Human transformation of the biomes

**Deforestation.** — People have cleared forest for farmland for millennia; the forests of Europe and eastern North America were largely cut between 1100 and 1900, then partly grew back as farming moved west. In the tropics, clearance has accelerated. The Food and Agriculture Organization estimated net forest loss at roughly ten million hectares a year in the 1990s and about 4.7 million hectares a year between 2010 and 2020. Brazil's Atlantic Forest has been reduced to about twelve per cent of its original extent; Madagascar has lost most of its original forest; and the "arc of deforestation" along the eastern and southern edges of the Amazon advances with cattle ranching and soy. The loss includes species that live nowhere else.

**The Dust Bowl.** — The southern Great Plains of the United States were ploughed for wheat during the high prices of the First World War, and the native grass that had held the soil was broken. When drought struck in 1931 and lasted most of the decade, the exposed topsoil blew away in enormous black storms; on 14 April 1935, "Black Sunday," a wall of dust some two hundred miles wide darkened the plains and carried an estimated 300 million tons of soil eastward. Around a hundred million acres were damaged, and roughly two and a half million people left the region. The federal response was the Soil Conservation Service, created in 1935 under Hugh Hammond Bennett, and the Prairie States Forestry Project, which planted more than two hundred million trees as windbreaks between 1935 and 1942. The historian Donald Worster argued in *Dust Bowl* (1979) that the disaster was not simply a drought but the product of a culture that treated land as a commodity to be mined; other scholars stress the severity of the natural drought and the role of federal land policy. The debate turns on how much of the catastrophe was made by people and how much by the sky.

**Desertification.** — In the semi-arid lands on the edges of deserts, overgrazing, deforestation, and bad irrigation can strip the vegetation and leave bare ground that wind and water carry away. The Sahel, the belt south of the Sahara, suffered a catastrophic drought from the late 1960s, and the disaster focused attention on the process. A United Nations conference on desertification met in Nairobi in 1977, and a convention followed in 1994. How much of the change is permanent "desertification" and how much is the normal swing of a variable climate is disputed: some researchers argue that the term has been used too loosely and that the Sahel has partly recovered. What is not disputed is that dryland soils are easily damaged and slow to heal.

## Chronology

- **1799–1804** — Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland travel in Spanish America and describe vegetation belts.
- **1807** — Humboldt's *Essay on the Geography of Plants* lays out plant geography; Buchanan-Hamilton names laterite in India.
- **1831–1836** — Charles Darwin's voyage on HMS *Beagle*; he crosses the pampas in 1833.
- **1864** — George Perkins Marsh's *Man and Nature* warns that people can destroy the land.
- **1878–1879** — John Wesley Powell's *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region* maps the dry West.
- **1895–1898** — Warming's *Plantesamfund* and Schimper's *Plant-Geography* found the study of plant communities.
- **1900** — Köppen publishes his climate classification.
- **1916** — Clements's *Plant Succession* uses "biome"; he later defends the climax theory.
- **1917, 1926** — Gleason argues for the individualistic concept of the plant association.
- **1931–1939** — The Dust Bowl drought on the southern Great Plains.
- **14 April 1935** — "Black Sunday"; the Soil Conservation Service is created the same year.
- **1936** — The Great Plains Committee reports on *The Future of the Great Plains*.
- **1938** — The Soil Conservation Service publishes Russell Lord's *To Hold This Soil*.
- **1941** — Hans Jenny's *Factors of Soil Formation*.
- **1970** — Whittaker's biome diagram.
- **1977** — The United Nations Conference on Desertification meets in Nairobi.
- **1979** — Donald Worster's *Dust Bowl*.
- **1988** — Fires burn a third of Yellowstone; Myers names biodiversity hotspots.
- **1994** — The UN Convention to Combat Desertification is adopted.
