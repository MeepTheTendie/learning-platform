# The regions of the world

**Region:** The whole earth

## Where Europe ends

**A line through the Urals.** — The Ural Mountains run about 2,500 kilometres from the Arctic to the steppe, down the middle of the Eurasian landmass, and for nearly three centuries they have been treated as the boundary between Europe and Asia. The barrier is not physical: Mount Narodnaya, the highest summit, rises only 1,895 metres, and roads and railways cross the range. The line is a decision, fixed in the 1730s by Philip Johan von Strahlenberg, a Swedish officer captured at Poltava in 1709 and held for years in Siberia, who in 1730 published a book and map arguing that the Urals, the Ural River, and the Caspian should separate the two. Earlier geographers had put the boundary on the Don, which the Greeks called the Tanais, or on the Volga. The land does not change at the Urals; the label does. That is the first fact of regional geography: a region is a named part of the earth's surface, and the naming is an act of selection.

**One land, several continents.** — The planet's land surface is about 149 million square kilometres and its water about 361 million, so that oceans cover roughly 71 per cent of the globe. The Pacific alone is about 165 million square kilometres; the Atlantic about 106 million, the Indian about 70 million, the Southern about 22 million, and the Arctic about 14 million. The land is divided into continents, but the number depends on who is counting. The seven-continent convention common in the English-speaking world — Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, Australia — is not shared everywhere. Latin American schools teach six, with the Americas counted as one; Russian and Japanese geography also teach six, but count Eurasia as one and treat Europe and Asia as its parts. In *The Myth of Continents* (1997), Martin W. Lewis and Kären Wigen argue that the continent is not a natural object but a cultural one, and that the ordinary world map smuggles hierarchy into geography: the "West" and the "East" the continents seem to name are historical claims dressed as landforms.

**What a region is.** — Geographers distinguish three kinds of region. A formal region is defined by the uniform presence of some feature: a climate, a language, a crop. A functional region is defined by a centre and the flows that bind its hinterland to it — a city and its commuting field, a port and its trade. A perceptual region is held together by what people believe about it, such as the "Midwest" or the "Balkans," whose edges no two people draw alike. The French geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache (1845–1918) treated a region as a *pays*, a small territory whose character came from the long meeting of a people and its land. His American contemporary Carl O. Sauer argued in *The Morphology of Landscape* (1925) that a region is the visible record of human labour on a natural site — a cultural landscape, made by clearing, planting, building, and burning. The same ground can be a formal region on one map, a functional region on another, and a perceptual region in the minds of those who live there.

**The organizing question.** — This unit surveys ten major regions as geographers commonly divide the world: North America; Latin America; Europe; Russia and Central Asia; North Africa and Southwest Asia; Sub-Saharan Africa; South Asia; East Asia; Southeast Asia; and Oceania. It treats each by its physical setting, its climate, its population, its economy, and one defining issue. Through all of them it keeps two questions in view. What holds a region together — a mountain chain, a sea, a language, a state, a trade route, an idea? And where do its boundaries really lie — in the rock and the rain, or only on the map? The ten regions are a working convention, and some of the cuts below are disputed by the people who live on the line.

## North America

**Physical setting.** — North America is the third-largest continent, about 24.7 million square kilometres including Greenland and Central America. The Rocky Mountains run down the west and the older, lower Appalachians down the east, with the interior plain drained by the Mississippi and Missouri rivers between them. To the north-east lie the Great Lakes, about 244,000 square kilometres, the largest group of freshwater lakes on earth.

**Climate and people.** — The continent reaches from Arctic tundra to the subtropical Gulf coast, and its interior is continental: far from the moderating ocean, the Great Plains swing from scorching summers to severe winters and breed more tornadoes than anywhere else, and hurricanes strike the Gulf and Atlantic coasts. About 600 million people live here — some 335 million in the United States, 128 million in Mexico, and 40 million in Canada — and more than four in five are urban. The United States has the world's largest economy, near 27 trillion dollars; Canada supplies oil, timber, and hydroelectric power, and Mexico manufactured goods under the continental trade agreement first signed in 1994.

**One defining issue: the Colorado.** — The Colorado River drains about 640,000 square kilometres and supplies some 40 million people and vast areas of irrigated farmland in seven states. Its water was divided in 1922 by the Colorado River Compact, when the river ran higher than its long-term average, so the compact promised more water than the river carries. The Colorado now rarely reaches the Gulf of California, and the reservoirs of Lake Mead and Lake Powell have fallen to record lows.

## Latin America

**Physical setting.** — Latin America stretches from the Rio Grande to Tierra del Fuego. The Andes run about 7,000 kilometres down the Pacific side, the longest continental mountain range on earth. East of them lies the Amazon basin, some seven million square kilometres. To the south are the grassy Pampas of Argentina; along the Pacific coast lies the Atacama, the driest desert outside the polar regions.

**Climate and people.** — The region spans equatorial rainforest, savanna, high mountain, grassland, and desert. El Niño and La Niña, a periodic shift in ocean temperature and winds, bring floods to Peru and drought to parts of Brazil. About 660 million people live here, roughly 215 million in Brazil and 128 million in Mexico; four in five are urban. The languages are mainly Spanish and Portuguese, but millions speak Indigenous languages such as Quechua and Guaraní. The economies rest on commodities: copper from Chile, which produces about a quarter of the world's supply; oil from Venezuela, holder of the largest proven reserves; soy and beef from Brazil and Argentina; and lithium from the salt flats of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia.

**One defining issue: the Amazon.** — The Amazon rainforest covers some five and a half million square kilometres and holds perhaps a tenth of all known species. Since the 1970s, roads, ranches, soy farms, and mines have cleared roughly a fifth of the Brazilian forest. Scientists debate whether it is approaching a tipping point — some place near 20 to 25 per cent loss, though the figure is contested — at which enough dries out that the remainder can no longer make its own rain. The forest is the largest store of living carbon on land.

## Europe

**Physical setting.** — Europe is the western peninsula of the Eurasian landmass, about 10.2 million square kilometres. Its coasts are broken and its seas shallow — the Mediterranean, the Baltic, the North Sea, the Black Sea — and mountain chains such as the Pyrenees, the Alps, and the Carpathians divide it into peninsulas and basins. The Danube, about 2,850 kilometres long, passes through ten countries before reaching the Black Sea.

**Climate and people.** — The western coast is unusually mild for its latitude because the North Atlantic Drift, a warm ocean current, carries heat northward; London and Calgary lie at nearly the same latitude, but London's winters are far gentler. The south has a Mediterranean climate of wet winters and dry summers; the east becomes steadily more continental. About 745 million people live in Europe, some 448 million of them in the European Union. The population is old by world standards and birth rates are low, so immigration is the main source of growth. The European Union, built from the Treaty of Rome of 1957, is the world's largest single market, and most member states use the euro.

**One defining issue: the union itself.** — Europe is the clearest case in the world of a region deliberately constructed. The Schengen Agreement of 1985, in force from 1995, abolished passport checks at the internal borders of most member states, so that a region was created not by mountains or language but by treaty. The same project produced its own counter-movement: in 2016 Britain voted to leave, and it departed in 2020. What holds a region together — shared institutions and law, or a nation and its sovereignty? — is not settled.

## Russia and Central Asia

**Physical setting.** — Russia is the largest country on earth, about 17.1 million square kilometres, an eighth of all the land. West of the Urals lies the European plain; east lies Siberia, a vast expanse of taiga — the largest forest on earth — underlain for much of its depth by permafrost, ground that stays frozen year-round. The Caspian Sea, about 371,000 square kilometres, is the largest inland body of water in the world. To the south lie the five states of Central Asia — Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan — a region of steppe, desert, and high mountain crossed for centuries by the Silk Road.

**Climate and people.** — The climate is sharply continental: far from any ocean, the interior has the widest temperature swings on earth, and the Siberian town of Oymyakon has recorded minus 67.7 degrees Celsius. Russia holds about 144 million people and Central Asia about 75 million, both multi-ethnic. The economies rest on oil and gas, minerals, and, in Central Asia, cotton grown on irrigated land.

**One defining issue: the Aral Sea.** — In the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the two rivers that fed the Aral Sea, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, to irrigate cotton. The sea shrank, and by the 2000s it had lost about 90 per cent of its volume and split into fragments; fishing towns were left stranded many kilometres from water, and salt and dust blow off the exposed seabed. It also shows how borders are made: in the 1920s and 1930s Soviet authorities drew the internal boundaries of Central Asia so that no single ethnic group dominated a republic, and those lines became international borders in 1991.

## North Africa and Southwest Asia

**Physical setting.** — This region joins the Sahara, the world's largest hot desert at about 9.2 million square kilometres, to the Arabian Peninsula and the highlands of Turkey and Iran. The Nile runs about 6,650 kilometres north from the lakes of East Africa to the Mediterranean; the Tigris and Euphrates carry water from the mountains of Turkey into the Persian Gulf. At the Strait of Hormuz, about 39 kilometres wide at its narrowest, the Persian Gulf meets the Indian Ocean.

**Climate and people.** — Most of the region is arid, and its people have always lived where water is: along rivers, at oases, and on the coasts. About 500 million people live here. Egypt holds some 105 million, almost all crowded into the narrow Nile valley and delta; Iran has about 89 million, Turkey about 85 million, Iraq about 45 million, and Saudi Arabia about 36 million. It is the birthplace of three world religions and today mostly Muslim, but not one people: Arabs, Persians, Turks, Kurds, and Berbers speak different languages and claim different histories. The Persian Gulf holds roughly half the world's proven oil reserves.

**One defining issue: water.** — The region has less fresh water per person than almost anywhere on earth, and its rivers cross borders. Egypt takes nearly all its water from the Nile and has long claimed a historic right to it; upstream, Ethiopia has built the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, and the two countries, with Sudan between them, have argued for years over how fast it may be filled and how much water must be released downstream. The Tigris and Euphrates are shared by Turkey, Syria, and Iraq. In a dry region the boundary that matters most is drawn on the water.

## Sub-Saharan Africa

**Physical setting.** — Sub-Saharan Africa runs from the Sahel, the semi-arid belt that edges the Sahara, south to the Cape of Good Hope. Its great physical feature is the East African Rift, where the continent is splitting along a line of faults, volcanoes, and lakes; Kilimanjaro, at 5,895 metres the highest point in Africa, is a rift volcano. The Congo basin holds the second-largest rainforest on earth, and the Niger, Congo, and Zambezi rivers drain the west, centre, and south.

**Climate and people.** — The equatorial belt is hot and wet, the savanna has one wet season and one dry, and the far south and north-west have Mediterranean climates. The region is governed by the seasonal movement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone, the band of rising air that brings rain; when it fails, the result is drought, as in the Sahel between 1968 and 1973. Sub-Saharan Africa has about 1.2 billion people, the fastest-growing population of any region. Nigeria holds about 223 million, Ethiopia about 126 million, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo about 102 million. Economies depend on farming, on minerals — the Congo supplies about 70 per cent of the world's cobalt — and on oil, as in Nigeria.

**One defining issue: the borders.** — In 1884 and 1885, at the Berlin Conference, European powers agreed on rules for dividing Africa among themselves. The borders they drew, and those fixed in later treaties, cut through kingdoms, language areas, and grazing lands; perhaps a third of Africa's land boundaries are straight lines ruled across the map, ignoring the ground. When the colonies became independent, the new states kept those borders, because the alternative was endless war over every frontier. The result is a political map that is a European artefact, its peoples divided and joined by lines none of them drew.

## South Asia

**Physical setting.** — South Asia is a subcontinent walled off by the Himalaya, the highest mountains on earth, whose summit, Everest, stands 8,849 metres above sea level. South of the mountains, the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers meet and build the largest delta in the world, some 100,000 square kilometres of shifting silt shared by India and Bangladesh. The interior is the Deccan plateau, a high table of ancient rock; the north-west is the Thar Desert. The region is a peninsula thrust into the Indian Ocean, a crossroads of trade for millennia.

**Climate and people.** — The defining climate is the monsoon: in summer the land heats, the winds reverse, and moist air sweeps in from the ocean, dropping most of the year's rain between June and September; in winter the winds blow dry from the interior. Places on the windward slopes, such as Mawsynram in north-eastern India, are among the wettest on earth. South Asia is the most populous region in the world, about 1.9 billion people. India, with some 1.43 billion, passed China in 2023 as the most populous country; Pakistan has about 240 million and Bangladesh about 173 million. India's economy, near 3.7 trillion dollars, rests on services and industry as well as farming, and Bangladesh exports ready-made garments.

**One defining issue: the monsoon and the water under it.** — South Asia's farmers depend not only on the monsoon rain but on the groundwater they pump from beneath their fields, and in the north-west of India the aquifers are drawn down faster than the rain refills them. The region's rivers cross borders, too: India and Bangladesh share the Ganges, India and Pakistan the Indus, and the treaties that divide the water are under strain as populations grow. The monsoon is a physical system, but who gets its water is a political question.

## East Asia

**Physical setting.** — East Asia centres on China, with Japan, the Korean peninsula, Mongolia, and Taiwan around it. The Tibetan Plateau, some 2.5 million square kilometres at an average elevation above 4,500 metres, is the source of the region's great rivers: the Yangtze, about 6,300 kilometres long, and the Yellow River, about 5,464 kilometres. The Gobi Desert lies to the north.

**Climate and people.** — The eastern seaboard has a monsoon climate, wet in summer and dry in winter; the interior is dry and extreme, and typhoons strike the coasts and Japan. About 1.6 billion people live in East Asia, and the region contains two of the world's largest economies: China, near 18 trillion dollars, and Japan, near four trillion. China holds about 1.41 billion people, Japan about 124 million. The region is the workshop of the world, exporting electronics, machinery, ships, and cars.

**One defining issue: an ageing population.** — East Asia is growing old faster than any region in history. Japan has the oldest population in the world, with a median age near 49; China's one-child policy, in force from 1979 to 2015, left it with a median age near 40 and a shrinking workforce, and its population fell in 2022 for the first time in six decades. Fewer workers must support more retirees, and the region's economic model — built on a vast supply of young labour — is being forced to change.

## Southeast Asia

**Physical setting.** — Southeast Asia has two halves. The mainland — Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and Vietnam — is drained by the Mekong, a river about 4,350 kilometres long that rises in Tibet and passes through six countries. The archipelago — Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and East Timor — includes Indonesia's more than 17,000 islands and sits on the Ring of Fire, with more than a hundred active volcanoes. Between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra runs the Strait of Malacca.

**Climate and people.** — The region is tropical, hot and wet, with a monsoon rhythm and a typhoon season in the north. About 680 million people live here. Indonesia holds some 277 million, the fourth-largest population of any country; the Philippines about 117 million, Vietnam about 99 million, and Thailand about 72 million. The population is young by the standards of Europe or East Asia, and cities are growing quickly. The economies range from Singapore's finance and shipping to Vietnam's factories, Indonesia's palm oil and coal, and the rice paddies that feed the region.

**One defining issue: the rising sea.** — Southeast Asia's coasts and deltas are among the most exposed on earth. Parts of Jakarta, the Indonesian capital, are sinking by as much as ten centimetres a year as groundwater is pumped out and the weight of the city presses down, even as the sea rises; Indonesia has begun building a new capital, Nusantara, on Borneo. The Mekong delta, which grows much of Vietnam's rice, is losing land to the sea and to upstream dams that starve it of silt. The Strait of Malacca carries a quarter of the world's traded goods, so a change in this coastline is felt in every port on earth.

## Oceania

**Physical setting.** — Oceania is the smallest region by land, spread over the largest area of ocean. Australia, about 7.7 million square kilometres, is the flattest and driest inhabited continent, with an arid interior and a fertile rim; New Zealand lies 2,000 kilometres to the south-east; New Guinea, the second-largest island in the world, lies to the north; and thousands of small islands, from Fiji to Samoa to the atolls of Micronesia and Polynesia, are scattered across the Pacific. Off Australia's north-east coast runs the Great Barrier Reef, some 2,300 kilometres long.

**Climate and people.** — The interior of Australia is desert; the north is tropical, with a wet and a dry season; the south-east is temperate. The Pacific is governed by El Niño and La Niña, the see-saw of ocean temperature that brings drought to Australia and rain to the eastern Pacific, and back again. About 44 million people live in Oceania, some 26 million in Australia, 10 million in Papua New Guinea, and 5 million in New Zealand. Indigenous peoples — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians, Māori, and Pacific islanders — have histories reaching back tens of thousands of years. Australia is a major exporter of iron ore, coal, and lithium.

**One defining issue: the atolls.** — The low coral islands of the Pacific are among the places most exposed to rising seas. Tuvalu, with about 26 square kilometres of land and a highest point under five metres, and Kiribati, spread over 3.5 million square kilometres of ocean, have bought land abroad and argued in international forums about what happens to a nation whose territory is drowned. For these islands the boundary between land and sea is not a line on a map but a condition of survival.

## The connections between regions

**Trade.** — The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a merchant's guide written in the first century, lists the goods that moved between Egypt, Arabia, India, and the East: ivory and tortoise-shell out of Africa, frankincense from Arabia, pepper and cotton from India, silk from the land it calls This. About 90 per cent of world trade by volume travels by sea, funnelled through a handful of narrow passages: the Strait of Malacca, the Strait of Hormuz, the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal.

**Migration.** — People move along the same routes, and the money they send home ties regions together. India receives more remittances — money sent by workers abroad — than any other country, over 100 billion dollars a year; Mexico, the Philippines, and Egypt are close behind. The oil states of the Persian Gulf employ millions of migrant workers from South and Southeast Asia.

**Climate.** — The atmosphere and the oceans are single connected systems. El Niño links the Pacific to drought in Australia and Indonesia and floods in Peru; the dust of the Sahara blows across the Atlantic to fertilise the Amazon; a warmer Arctic changes the jet stream and the storms that cross Europe and North America. The carbon one region burns is measured in the air of all of them.

**The view from the map.** — Halford Mackinder, in "The Geographical Pivot of History" (1904), argued that the interior of Eurasia — the "Heartland" — was the pivot on which world power turned, because it was closed to sea power and open to the horsemen and, later, the railways that could move armies across it. Geography, not culture or wealth, decided which regions mattered. A different claim runs through this unit: that regions are made as much by naming, trade, migration, and treaty as by rock and rain. The land sets the conditions; the people draw the lines; and the lines, once drawn, become real.

## Chronology

- **c. 450 BCE** — Herodotus puzzles over why the Greeks divide one continuous earth into three continents.
- **c. 150 CE** — Ptolemy's *Geography* fixes the known world by coordinates and divides it into regions.
- **1494** — The Treaty of Tordesillas draws a line from pole to pole through the Atlantic, dividing the non-European world between Spain and Portugal.
- **1730** — Strahlenberg proposes the Ural Mountains and the Caspian as the boundary between Europe and Asia.
- **1884–85** — The Berlin Conference sets rules for the European partition of Africa.
- **1904** — Halford Mackinder publishes "The Geographical Pivot of History."
- **1922** — The Colorado River Compact divides the river's water among seven states.
- **1957** — The Treaty of Rome founds the European Economic Community.
- **1985** — The Schengen Agreement abolishes internal passport checks in much of Europe.
- **1994** — The North American Free Trade Agreement takes effect.
- **1997** — Lewis and Wigen publish *The Myth of Continents*.
- **2007** — The mobile-money service M-Pesa launches in Kenya.
- **2015** — The Paris Agreement is adopted by the parties to the climate convention.
- **2020** — The United Kingdom leaves the European Union.
- **2023** — India passes China as the world's most populous country.

## Questions to carry

- If a continent is a convention, what is the difference between a region and a label?
- Which of the ten regions is held together mainly by physical geography, and which mainly by institutions or history?
- The Ural line was drawn by a prisoner of war. What does that suggest about how other regional borders were made?
- Trade moves goods between regions. Does it make their boundaries weaker, or does it give them something to defend?
- If the sea rises a metre, which regions change shape, and who decides what is left of them?
