# Cities and urbanization

**Region:** The whole earth · **Theme:** Site, growth, and the shape of the city

## Site and situation

**Two ways to ask where a city is.** — Every city occupies a particular piece of ground and holds a position within a wider region, and geographers keep the two ideas apart. The **site** is the ground itself: its slope and drainage, its soil and building stone, its supply of fresh water, how easily it can be defended, how much room it offers to build. The **situation** is where that ground sits in relation to everything around it — the rivers, passes, harbours, roads, resources, and rival towns that make a place useful or useless to other people. Rome is the standard illustration. Its site is the cluster of hills above the Tiber, beside a small island and a ford where the river could be crossed, and set back from the malarial marshes of the coastal plain. Its situation is the middle of the Italian peninsula, where the north–south land route met the roads that crossed the Apennines to the Adriatic; the Appian Way and the Flaminian Way later radiated from the city in different directions. The hills explain why a settlement could sit there; the position explains why it came to rule the peninsula.

**Site and situation can be separated, and they change at different speeds.** — Constantinople was sited on a peninsula on the European shore of the Bosphorus, with the sheltered inlet of the Golden Horn as a harbour and a single short land wall to defend. Its situation was the narrow crossing between Europe and Asia and the only sea route between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean; for a thousand years that position made it one of the richest cities on earth. When the ocean routes shifted after 1492 and 1498, the situation weakened while the site remained. New York is the reverse case: a mediocre site of rocky islands and tidal flats, but a situation of genius — a deep, sheltered harbour at the mouth of the Hudson, close to the one broad lowland gap through the Appalachian chain. The Erie Canal, opened in 1825, turned that gap into a water road to the Great Lakes, and the city at the harbour mouth became the great entrepôt of the eastern seaboard. A site is chosen for reasons that can later vanish; a situation can be remade by a canal, a railway, or an airline route.

**Why cities grow where they do.** — Four forces recur. **Water** is the first: drinking water, water for irrigation and livestock, water as a road, water to carry away waste. The earliest large cities stand on rivers — Memphis and Thebes on the Nile, Ur on the Euphrates, Mohenjo-daro on the Indus, Anyang on the Yellow River. Cities cluster at confluences, where two rivers meet and trade can be gathered: Khartoum at the Blue and White Nile, Pittsburgh where the Allegheny and Monongahela form the Ohio, Belgrade at the Sava and Danube. They also cluster at the **fall line**, the boundary where a river drops from hard rock onto the softer coastal plain; boats could go no farther upstream and waterwheels could begin, so Richmond, Fredericksburg, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Washington all grew along that line as **break-of-bulk** points, where cargo changes from one form of transport to another. **Defence** is the second: hilltops (the Acropolis at Athens, the castle rock at Edinburgh, the ridge at Jerusalem), river bends and islands (Paris on the Île de la Cité, Montreal on the St Lawrence), peninsulas, and marsh moats. Walls set the limit of the old city and left their shape on the modern one: Vienna's Ringstrasse follows the line of the demolished walls, and the boulevards of many European cities trace old fortifications. **Crossroads** are the third: bridging points, mountain passes, caravan routes, and portages. Palmyra and Petra grew rich on desert caravans; Timbuktu on the gold and salt routes across the Sahara; Samarkand and Aleppo at the meeting of trade roads; Chicago where the Chicago River almost touched the Des Plaines, a short portage between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi basin. When the route moved, the city could wither — Timbuktu declined as Atlantic shipping drew trade to the coast. **Resources** are the fourth: coal and iron ore made Newcastle, the Ruhr, and Pittsburgh; gold made Johannesburg after 1886; silver made Potosí after 1545; oil made Tulsa and Houston. Resource towns are the most fragile of all, because a single deposit can be exhausted or undercut, as Detroit learned when its industry contracted.

**Not every city grows for an economic reason.** — Some were planted by decision. Peter the Great founded St Petersburg in 1703 on a marsh at the mouth of the Neva to face Europe; the United States built Washington on the Potomac after 1791 and Brasília on the empty central plateau in the 1950s; Australia built Canberra between Sydney and Melbourne. Others grew around a sacred place: Mecca, Jerusalem, Varanasi, Amritsar. And many places that could have become cities never did, because a rival captured the bridge, the railway, or the ruler's favour. Which site grows is a matter of contingency, not of geography alone.

## The pre-industrial and the industrial city

**The walking city.** — Before mechanical transport, almost every city was small, dense, and walled. A person could walk across it in an hour, so its working radius was about five kilometres. Home and workshop shared a building; the market, the guild hall, the temple or cathedral, and the ruler's palace stood at the centre, and the social order was visible in the plan. The wealthy lived near the centre, where the best sites and the monuments were; the poor crowded the edges, the low ground, the districts downwind of the tanneries and the slaughterhouses, and often the land just outside the walls. In an agrarian economy only a small share of the people lived in cities — commonly under a tenth, though the share was higher in prosperous regions such as Song China or Renaissance Italy. Walls controlled entry, collected tolls, and closed at night. The American sociologist Gideon Sjoberg argued in *The Preindustrial City* (1960) that these cities shared a common structure everywhere: a literate elite, a low-technology economy, and a rigid social order with the elite at the centre and "outcast" groups at the periphery. His model has been criticised for flattening the differences among Chinese, Indian, Islamic, and European cities, which were considerable.

**The industrial city.** — From the late eighteenth century in Britain, factories, coal, canals, and then railways broke the walking city open. Cities grew faster than ever before. Manchester held perhaps 25,000 people in the 1770s and more than 300,000 by the 1851 census; London passed a million by 1801, reached about 2.4 million by 1851, and was then the largest city in the world. The growth came from rural migrants and from a young population, and it outran housing, water, and sewers. Land uses pulled apart: factories gathered along the water and the railways; workers' housing packed in beside them; commerce concentrated in a central business district of offices, banks, and warehouses; and those who could afford it moved outward. Friedrich Engels, whose father owned a mill in Manchester, described the result in 1845: a commercial core ringed by an almost unbroken girdle of working-class districts, with the middle class able to travel from the Exchange to their businesses along shop-lined streets without ever seeing the misery behind them. Railways then let the middle class live farther out and travel in daily, and the **commute** was born.

**Sanitation and reform.** — The industrial city killed people. Cholera swept Britain in 1831–32, 1848–49, and 1853–54. Edwin Chadwick's *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population* (1842) tied disease to filth, overcrowding, and bad drainage, and pushed for piped water and sewers. In 1854 John Snow mapped cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump in Soho and showed that the disease travelled in water, not in bad air. Joseph Bazalgette's main drainage for London, built between 1859 and 1875, carried sewage out of the city and ended the worst of the river pollution. Public health acts, beginning with the Act of 1848 and strengthened in 1875, gave towns powers over water, drainage, and building, and tenement laws in New York (notably 1901) required light and air in every room. Case by case, the industrial city was made survivable.

## Reading the city as a map

**The Chicago school.** — In the 1920s sociologists at the University of Chicago studied their own city as if it were a laboratory. In 1925 **Ernest W. Burgess** published his **concentric-zone model**: growth takes place by expansion outward from the centre and by "invasion" of each ring by the next. At the centre is the Loop, the central business district; around it a zone in transition of industry and decaying housing; then a zone of workingmen's homes; then a residential zone; and outermost a commuter zone. **Homer Hoyt** revised the picture in 1939 after studying 142 American cities: residential areas, he argued, grow not in rings but in **sectors**, wedges that follow transport routes, high ground, and the outward movement of high-rent districts. In 1945 **Chauncy Harris** and **Edward Ullman** offered the **multiple-nuclei** model: many cities have several distinct centres — a port, a university, an industrial district, an airport — because different land uses need different sites and attract or repel one another.

**What the models leave out.** — All three were built from North American cities of the motor age. They assume a market in land that is more or less free, outward growth from a single core, and no walls, and they fit Chicago far better than they fit Fez or Kolkata. Later geographers proposed regional alternatives: Ernst Griffin and Larry Ford's Latin American city model (1980) and Terry McGee's Southeast Asian model (1967). The models are not laws. They are attempts to see an order in one kind of city, and they are most useful when they fail, because the failure shows what local history, terrain, and politics have done.

## Central Place Theory

**Christaller's question.** — In 1933 the German geographer **Walter Christaller** published *Die zentralen Orte in Süddeutschland* (*Central Places in Southern Germany*), a study of where towns sit and how big they are. He began from a deliberately simplified world: a flat, uniform plain, an evenly spread farming population, transport equally easy in every direction, and consumers who go to the nearest place that offers the good they want. Two ideas do the work. The **threshold** is the least population a good needs in order to be worth providing. The **range** is the farthest a customer will travel for it. Cheap everyday goods — bread, a newspaper, a haircut — have low thresholds and short ranges, so many small centres supply them. Rare, expensive goods — a hospital, a department store, a university — have high thresholds and long ranges, so only a few large centres can support them. The result is a **hierarchy** of central places, from hamlet to village to town to city, and a pattern of market areas that, if the assumptions hold, is best shaped as **hexagons**, the figure that covers a plain with equal, non-overlapping service areas. Christaller offered three variants: a market arrangement (k=3), a transport arrangement (k=4), and an administrative arrangement (k=7). The economist August Lösch extended the geometry in 1940.

**Where the theory breaks.** — The uniform plain does not exist. Mountains, coasts, rivers, and borders interrupt the pattern; population is not evenly spread; transport is not equally easy in all directions; and people do not always shop at the nearest centre, because they prefer a particular shop, a lower price, or a route they already travel. Central place theory explains service towns, not coalfield or port cities, and it says little about the great industrial and political centres that owe their size to something other than serving a farming hinterland. Yet the regular spacing of market towns and the rank-size ordering of settlements are real enough that geographers such as Brian Berry tested and refined the theory rather than discarded it. Its limits are as instructive as its predictions.

## The Latin American and the Islamic city

**The planned colonial grid.** — When Spain and Portugal colonised the Americas, they laid out towns to a written rule. Philip II's **Ordinances of 1573** ordered the founders to choose a healthy, elevated, well-watered site with farmland and building materials; to lay out a rectangular grid of streets around a main plaza; to place the church, the royal council house, and the customs house on the plaza; and to send the slaughterhouses, tanneries, and fisheries to places where their filth could be carried away. The result is the plan shared by Mexico City (built on the ruins of Aztec Tenochtitlan after 1521), Lima (1535), Buenos Aires (1580), and most of their neighbours: a strong central plaza, straight streets, and a social gradient in which wealth, services, and prestige cluster near the centre and decline with distance. From the mid-twentieth century, mass migration from the countryside produced vast peripheral settlements — the *colonias populares* of Mexico City, the *barriadas* of Lima, the *favelas* of Rio de Janeiro, the *villas miseria* of Buenos Aires — often on steep or flood-prone land that the formal market rejected. Griffin and Ford's model of the Latin American city therefore inverts Burgess: the poor sit at the edge, not in an inner transition zone, while an elite "spine" runs out along a main avenue to gated suburbs and shopping malls.

**The medina.** — In the historic cores of Fez, Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Tunis, and Isfahan the plan is different again. The centre is the Friday mosque and the market, or suq, often near a citadel. Residential quarters are separated by origin, trade, or religion; the streets are narrow and frequently end in dead ends; houses turn inward to a courtyard and present blank walls to the street; and there is no monumental civic square of the European or colonial kind. European scholars once treated this as "the Islamic city," a single type produced by Islamic law and society. The historian **Janet Abu-Lughod** argued in "The Islamic City — Historic Myth, Islamic Essence, and Contemporary Relevance" (1987) that the type was largely a construct of French colonial administrators and Orientalist scholarship, and that what these cities share comes more from climate, building materials, and pre-Islamic inheritance than from religion; Fez and Cairo, she noted, differ as much as they resemble each other. The debate is unsettled. Some urban historians still find a family resemblance; others deny that there is a single type. Either way, the comparison shows that a shared religion did not produce a shared plan.

## Suburbs, sprawl, and commuting

**How the suburb became mass.** — Wealthy households kept country villas long before industrialisation, but the suburb as a mass form waited on cheap transport. The omnibus and then the commuter railway, from the 1830s and 1840s, pushed the residential edge out along the tracks; the electric streetcar, from the 1880s and 1890s, opened "streetcar suburbs" a few miles from the core. The automobile and the expressway removed the last limit. Levittown, New York, built between 1947 and 1951, put up more than 17,000 nearly identical houses on former potato fields and sold them to returning soldiers; it became the emblem of the postwar suburb. Suburbs were shaped by policy as much as by preference. In the United States the Home Owners' Loan Corporation's "residential security" maps, drawn from 1935, graded neighbourhoods and marked Black and immigrant districts in red — **redlining** — so that mortgages and insurance were refused there, while federal highway and mortgage programmes subsidised suburban ownership for white families. The resulting geography of race and wealth outlasted the maps that made it. **Kenneth Jackson**, in *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985), argued that the American suburb came from cheap land, cheap transport, and government subsidy rather than from a natural taste for grass. **Joel Garreau**, in *Edge City* (1991), described the office and retail clusters that grew at highway interchanges until many suburbs held more jobs than the old downtown.

**Megacities and world cities.** — The scale of cities changed again in the twentieth century. In 1800 perhaps only Beijing approached a million people; by 1900 London was over six million; by 1950 the New York region was the first to approach ten million. The United Nations calls an urban agglomeration of more than ten million a **megacity**. In 2020 the largest included Tokyo (about 37 million), Delhi (about 30 million), Shanghai (about 27 million), São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Dhaka, Mumbai, Beijing, and Osaka. Most of the largest now lie in Asia and the poorer half of the world, and many grow faster than their housing, water, and sewers can follow. A different idea concerns not size but command. **Peter Hall**, in *The World Cities* (1966), described the great cities as centres of political and economic decision. **Saskia Sassen**, in *The Global City* (1991), argued that a small number of cities — New York, London, Tokyo — became the command points of a dispersed world economy, concentrating finance and the specialized services that support it, and producing a new split between highly paid professionals and low-paid service workers. Critics reply that the idea imposes a single ranking on very different cities and neglects industrial and regional centres. **Jean Gottmann** had already described, in *Megalopolis* (1961), the continuous urbanised belt from Boston to Washington — a new scale of settlement that is not one city at all.

## Housing, sanitation, and segregation

**Who gets what, and where.** — Density and inequality have always decided who lives where and how safely. The pre-industrial city had its crowded tenements, its fires, and its plagues. The industrial city was worse: back-to-back houses in Leeds and Birmingham, tenements in Glasgow and New York, the cellar dwellings Engels found in Stockport and Manchester, with shared privies and water from a common pump. Reform followed the epidemics — Chadwick's report, Snow's pump, Bazalgette's sewers, the public health and tenement acts — and each reform redrew the city, pushing new housing to higher standards and old housing down the market. Segregation took many forms: the ethnic and religious quarters of the pre-modern city; racial zoning, restrictive covenants, and redlining in the United States; the Group Areas Act and the townships of apartheid South Africa from 1950, which moved Black residents to places such as Soweto; and the gated communities and informal settlements of the present. Housing is where a city's politics becomes visible, because it decides who gets land, services, and safety, and who is moved to make room for others.

## Urban planning

**Planning is as old as the grid.** — The Greek architect Hippodamus of Miletus, in the fifth century BCE, is credited with the orthogonal plan; Roman colonies were laid out from a military camp with two main streets, the *cardo* and *decumanus*; the medieval *bastides* of southern France and England were planned grids; Renaissance theorists drew ideal cities, and Palmanova, founded in 1593, was built as a nine-pointed star. The nineteenth century produced the great reconstructions. Baron Haussmann cut broad boulevards through the crowded quarters of Paris between 1853 and 1870 for traffic, light, and control; Vienna replaced its walls with the Ringstrasse from 1857. **Ebenezer Howard**, in *Garden Cities of To-morrow* (1898), proposed self-contained towns of about 32,000 people ringed by farmland, to combine the advantages of town and country; Letchworth (1903) and Welwyn Garden City (1920) were built on his ideas. **Zoning** — separating land uses by law — began in New York in 1916 and was upheld by the United States Supreme Court in *Euclid v. Ambler* (1926). Le Corbusier proposed towers set in parks, realised in part at Chandigarh in India from the 1950s and in the apartment blocks of many cities. Entire capitals were designed: Brasília, planned by Lúcio Costa with buildings by Oscar Niemeyer and inaugurated in 1960, was laid out on a cross; Canberra and the new towns of Britain and Scandinavia followed their own plans.

**The record is mixed.** — Planning has delivered water, sewers, parks, and light, and it has also bulldozed neighbourhoods, sorted people by race and class, and produced bleak estates and car-dependent sprawl. Whether the answer is higher density or dispersal, a strong plan or a weak one, preservation or renewal, is argued in every city, and the argument is not settled. The plan on paper is never the whole story: people use, ignore, and remake it, and the city that results is a bargain between the design and the lives lived in it.

## Questions to carry

- Why did this city grow at this crossing of the river and not at the next one?
- Burgess, Hoyt, and Harris and Ullman each saw an order in the American city. What does each model leave out, and which cities refuse it?
- Christaller's hexagons assume a blank plain. What happens to the pattern when a mountain, a coast, or a border crosses it?
- Fez, Mexico City, and Manchester are all cities. What does each plan assume about who belongs where?
- When a city is planned, who decides the plan, and who is moved by it?
