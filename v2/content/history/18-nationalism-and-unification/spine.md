# Nationalism and the making of nations

**Period:** c. 1815–1871 · **Region:** Europe

## The settlement of 1815

**A map drawn by diplomats.** — When the Congress of Vienna closed in June 1815, the victors believed they had settled Europe for a generation. Prince Klemens von Metternich for Austria, Viscount Castlereagh for Britain, Tsar Alexander I for Russia, Karl August von Hardenberg for Prussia, and Talleyrand for defeated France worked from three principles: legitimacy, the restoration of lawful dynasties; compensation, the reward of the powers; and the balance of power, so that no state could dominate. They moved borders and populations without consulting the people. Belgium was joined to the Netherlands, Norway handed to the King of Sweden, the Rhineland given to Prussia as a barrier on France's frontier, and Austria given Lombardy and Venetia and the presidency of a new German Confederation. France kept its frontiers of 1792 and was not dismembered, because a permanently weak France would leave Austria and Russia too strong.

**The German Confederation.** — Germany, until 1806 the thousand-odd pieces of the Holy Roman Empire, became a Confederation of thirty-nine states with a Federal Diet at Frankfurt, chaired by Austria. It was a league of princes, not a nation-state. Italy was in a comparable condition; Metternich is said to have called it a "geographical expression." It was divided among the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont, Austria's Lombardy and Venetia, the Papal States, Tuscany, Modena, Parma, and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. Poland had been partitioned out of existence in 1772, 1793, and 1795; the Congress gave the Russian share a kingdom and a constitution under Alexander I, who ignored it. The Ottoman Empire still ruled Greece, Serbia, and the Romanian principalities. The organizing question of this unit is how, within half a century, the idea of the nation broke open that settlement: how did the idea of the nation remake the map of Europe?

**The Concert of Europe.** — The powers also set up a way of managing the continent. The Quadruple Alliance of 1815, and the Holy Alliance of Russia, Austria, and Prussia, promised to uphold the settlement and to consult at congresses. This "Concert of Europe" met at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818, Troppau in 1820, Laibach in 1821, and Verona in 1822, mostly to decide how to suppress revolutions. Metternich wanted to freeze the map and the social order together: no redrawing of borders, no constitutions, no national claims. The system could hold only while the powers agreed and the peoples stayed quiet. National feeling proved harder to police than an army: poets, teachers, and printers spread it.

## The idea of the nation

**What the word meant.** — *Nation* comes from the Latin *natio*, a people of common birth, and for centuries it did not mean what it means now. At medieval universities a "nation" was a group of students from one region; in ordinary political speech it often meant the small elite that took part in public life. The French Revolution fused the word with sovereignty. In January 1789 the abbé Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès asked what the Third Estate was and answered that it was "the nation," the body that did the work of society and therefore had the right to make its law. The Declaration of the Rights of Man of August 1789 declared that "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." After 1815 the claim that a legitimate state must rest on a nation was a standing challenge to every restored dynasty. A nation was no longer merely a population under one ruler; it was a people that ought to govern itself.

**Two ways of belonging.** — The new idea had two faces. The French, or civic, version held that the nation is a body of citizens who accept the state and its laws, whatever their ancestry or language; the state makes the nation. The German, or cultural, version, born where no single German state existed, held that the nation is a people defined by language, descent, and shared tradition, and that such a people ought to have a state. The civic version can absorb newcomers and demands assimilation; the cultural version can exclude people who live inside the borders but do not belong to the people. Both draw a line, and that line is the central problem of the subject. The French revolutionary state, though it proclaimed universal rights, set out to destroy the regional languages — Breton, Occitan, Basque, Alsatian — and in 1794 the abbé Henri Grégoire reported to the Convention on the *patois* that had to be abolished so that all citizens could share one speech.

**Romantic nationalism.** — The cultural version drew on Romanticism, which prized feeling and folk memory above eighteenth-century reason. Johann Gottfried von Herder (1744–1803) argued that each people, each *Volk*, had its own language and poetry expressing a collective spirit; he was not a political nationalist, but his idea that language carries a people's soul became a foundation of later nationalism. In occupied Berlin, Johann Gottlieb Fichte delivered his *Addresses to the German Nation* in 1807–08, turning cultural identity into a summons to renewal. The brothers Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm collected German folktales (*Kinder- und Hausmärchen*, 1812) and began in 1838 the *Deutsches Wörterbuch*, claiming in language and story a common past that no prince had given them. Nationalism looked backward for evidence and forward for its programme. Its bearers were mostly middle-class: teachers, journalists, lawyers, students. The student associations called *Burschenschaften* marched and sang, and at the Wartburg festival of 1817 burned reactionary books.

## The conservative order and its cracks

**Metternich's police.** — The conservative order answered the national idea with censorship and police. After a student, Karl Ludwig Sand, murdered the playwright August von Kotzebue in 1819, Metternich pushed the Carlsbad Decrees through the Confederation: universities supervised, student associations banned, newspapers censored, liberal professors dismissed. Repression could not answer the deeper question of why a people should obey a dynasty it had not chosen.

**Greece.** — In 1821 the Greeks rose against Ottoman rule. European opinion was moved by Christian Greeks fighting a Muslim empire — philhellenism — and Byron died of fever at Missolonghi in 1824. The powers intervened at Navarino in 1827, destroying the Ottoman and Egyptian fleets, and Greek independence was recognised in 1830 and confirmed in 1832. Greece was the first new nation-state carved out of an empire, proof that the Vienna settlement could be revised.

**1830.** — In July 1830 a revolution in Paris replaced Charles X with Louis Philippe, who took the title "King of the French" rather than "King of France." In August the Belgians rose against Dutch rule; the powers recognised Belgium in 1830–31 and gave it Leopold of Saxe-Coburg as king. In November 1830 the Poles rose against Russia; the rising was crushed in 1831 and the constitution abolished, and thousands fled west, among them Chopin and the poet Adam Mickiewicz. In Italy the Carbonari stirred risings that Austrian troops put down. The map had moved, but only where a great power permitted it.

## 1848

**The chain of revolutions.** — In February 1848 Paris rose again. Louis Philippe abdicated, the Second Republic was proclaimed, and Lamartine led a provisional government promising work and the vote. In March Metternich fled Vienna in disguise for London; the Emperor Ferdinand promised a constitution. In Berlin crowds built barricades and Friedrich Wilhelm IV honoured the dead and promised a constitution and a united Germany. Milan drove out the Austrian garrison in five days; Venice proclaimed a republic; Charles Albert of Sardinia-Piedmont took the field against Austria. In Budapest Lajos Kossuth led a Hungarian government claiming autonomy; in Prague Czech leaders called a Slavic Congress. This was the "springtime of peoples."

**The Frankfurt Parliament.** — The German revolution produced the most ambitious assembly of the century. In May 1848 elected deputies gathered in the Paulskirche at Frankfurt — lawyers, professors, journalists, officials, few workers — to write a constitution. They debated fundamental rights, but the hard question was who belonged. The *Grossdeutsch* (Greater German) solution would include the German-speaking provinces of Austria and leave the Habsburgs at the head of Germany; the *Kleindeutsch* (Lesser German) solution would exclude Austria and put Prussia in charge. The assembly had no army, no tax revenue, and no agreement with the princes. In March 1849 it completed a constitution and offered the crown to Friedrich Wilhelm IV, who refused: an assembly had no right to bestow a crown. Prussian and Austrian troops dissolved the rump, and by 1850 the old order was back.

**Why the revolutions failed.** — They failed for several reasons. The revolutionaries were divided: liberals wanted constitutions and property, radicals wanted democracy, socialists wanted to reorganise work; in Paris the split became the June Days of 1848, when the army crushed the workers of the national workshops and thousands died. Nationalists were divided from each other: in Prague German liberals and Czech nationalists quarrelled over whether Bohemia was German or Czech; in Hungary the new government's Magyar nationalism alarmed the Croats, Slovaks, and Romanians, who revolted against it; in Italy, Piedmont's war frightened republicans as much as Austrians. The old armies stayed loyal, and the monarchs recovered their nerve. Many peasants, freed from seigneurial dues, got what they wanted. The powers helped each other: Russia sent an army into Hungary in 1849 to crush the independence declared that April, and France destroyed the Roman Republic defended by Mazzini and Garibaldi in July 1849. Within eighteen months the rulers had won everywhere — but the national question was now at the centre of European politics.

## Who is included and who is excluded

**The boundary problem.** — Every nationalism must draw a boundary, and every boundary leaves some people on the wrong side. The nation-state claims to be the state of a people, but real populations are mixed: cities have immigrants, frontiers have bilingual villages, empires contain dozens of peoples. New states used schools, armies, censuses, and law to turn subjects into citizens and to make one language and one history official. Prussia had acquired a large Polish-speaking population in the partitions and spent the century trying to Germanise it, banning Polish in schools in some provinces. The new Kingdom of Romania wrote a constitution in 1866 whose Article 7 granted citizenship only to Christians, excluding its Jewish population. In Schleswig, a duchy with mixed Danish and German populations, the question of which nation it belonged to helped start a war.

**Emancipation and its backlash.** — Jews were the clearest test of the civic idea. The French Revolution granted them citizenship in 1791 and Prussia followed in 1812; over the next decades most western and central European states removed the old legal disabilities, and many Jews entered the professions and public life. If the nation was a body of citizens, Jews could be citizens. But the cultural version told a different story, and in the last third of the century a new political antisemitism appeared. The journalist Wilhelm Marr popularised the word *antisemitism* in 1879; the historian Heinrich von Treitschke argued the same year that Jews were a foreign element in the German nation; the court preacher Adolf Stöcker built a Christian Social party in Berlin that attacked them. The nationalism that began as a claim for liberty could be turned against a minority inside the gate, and the exclusion followed from defining the nation as a people of common descent.

**Empire and nation.** — The multinational empires faced the same problem from the other side. Austria contained Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Ukrainians, Slovaks, Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Italians, and Slovenes; the Ottoman Empire held Greeks, Serbs, Bulgarians, Arabs, and Armenians. In 1867, after defeat by Prussia, Austria reorganised as Austria-Hungary: the *Ausgleich*, or Compromise, gave the Hungarians their own parliament and a share of power in a dual monarchy. It satisfied the Magyars and left the Slavs and Romanians outside, guaranteeing that the national question would continue. Hungary's nationality law of 1868 offered limited language rights, but the state's schools and administration pressed minorities toward Magyar.

## Italy: Mazzini, Cavour, Garibaldi

**Mazzini and the idea.** — Giuseppe Mazzini, a Genoese lawyer turned revolutionary, founded Young Italy at Marseille in 1831 and spent his life in exile, organising, writing, and plotting. His nationalism was democratic, republican, and European: a free nation was a moral community, each people had a contribution to make to humanity, and Italy's freedom required the freedom of other peoples. His followers swore to make Italy "one, independent, free, republican." The failures were many — a rising in Savoy in 1834, the Bandiera brothers shot in Calabria in 1844, the Roman Republic crushed in 1849 — but his writing gave the movement its language. His essay "On Nationality" (1852) declared that "the question of nationality can only be resolved by destroying the treaties of 1815, and changing the map of Europe and its public Law."

**Cavour and diplomacy.** — The man who made unification happen was a minister, not a conspirator. Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, became prime minister of Sardinia-Piedmont in 1852. He built railways, freed trade, reformed the finances, and modernised the state so that Piedmont could be a model for a new Italy. His method was diplomacy backed by war. In 1855 he brought Piedmont into the Crimean War on the side of Britain and France, not for any Black Sea interest but to buy a seat at the Congress of Paris in 1856, where he raised the Italian question before the powers. In July 1858 he met Napoleon III in secret at Plombières and agreed a plan: France would help drive Austria out of Lombardy and Venetia, and Piedmont would cede Nice and Savoy to France. War came in 1859. The French and Piedmontese won at Magenta and Solferino, but Napoleon, alarmed by the cost, made a separate armistice at Villafranca and left Venetia in Austrian hands. In 1860 Piedmont paid its price and handed Nice and Savoy to France.

**Garibaldi and the Thousand.** — The republicans acted on their own. In May 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi sailed from Quarto near Genoa with about a thousand volunteers, landed at Marsala in Sicily, and defeated a much larger Bourbon force. He took Palermo, crossed to the mainland, and entered Naples on 7 September 1860 while the Bourbon king fled. Garibaldi was a master of irregular war and a hero to democrats across Europe and the Americas; his advance threatened to become a social revolution, as peasants in the south rose against landlords. Cavour, fearing the republicans and French intervention, sent the Piedmontese army south. At Teano in October, Garibaldi handed his conquests to King Victor Emmanuel II and returned to his island home. On 17 March 1861 the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed. Massimo d'Azeglio is said to have remarked that Italy had been made and now Italians had to be made — often wrongly attributed to Cavour, it names the problem exactly. Venice was added in 1866 after Prussia defeated Austria; Rome was taken on 20 September 1870 after the French garrison withdrew, and became the capital in 1871. 
## Germany: Bismarck and "blood and iron"

**Prussia's advantages.** — Prussia's strength rested on unglamorous foundations. The *Zollverein*, a customs union begun in 1834, knitted the German states into a single market under Prussian leadership and deliberately left Austria out. The army was reformed in 1860–62 by War Minister Albrecht von Roon and Chief of Staff Helmuth von Moltke, who gave it breech-loading rifles, railways, and the telegraph. The reforms provoked a constitutional crisis: the liberal parliament refused the money, and King Wilhelm I considered abdicating. In September 1862 he appointed Otto von Bismarck as minister-president to push the budget through without parliament's consent. On 30 September Bismarck told the deputies that "the great questions of the time will not be decided by speeches and majority resolutions — that was the great error of 1848 and 1849 — but by iron and blood." The phrase, shortened to "blood and iron," became the name for his method.

**The three wars.** — Bismarck unified Germany through three short wars, each arranged so that Prussia fought one enemy while the others stayed neutral. In 1864 Prussia and Austria attacked Denmark over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein and won them; the two powers then quarrelled over the spoils. In 1866 Prussia turned on Austria. Moltke used the railways to concentrate his armies and destroyed the Austrian force at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866. The peace was deliberately moderate: Bismarck refused to take Austrian territory or march into Vienna, because he wanted Austria as a future partner and did not want to absorb millions of Catholics. Austria was expelled from Germany, and the North German Confederation of 1867 put Prussia at the head of all states north of the Main. The southern states stayed independent but signed secret military agreements with Prussia. In 1870 the pretext came from Spain, where a Hohenzollern prince was offered the throne. France protested, and Bismarck edited the report of the king's meeting with the French ambassador at Ems into a version that insulted both sides; France declared war on 19 July. The German states joined Prussia; the French army was beaten at Sedan on 2 September, Napoleon III was captured, and Paris was besieged. On 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the German Empire was proclaimed with Wilhelm I as emperor. The Treaty of Frankfurt in May annexed Alsace and much of Lorraine and imposed a large indemnity.

**What made it work, and what did not have to.** — Bismarck's success rested on real advantages — the Prussian army, the Zollverein, the disunity of his opponents — but also on skill and luck. He kept Britain and Russia neutral by timing and by choosing his quarrels. The outcome was not inevitable: his own position was precarious in 1862, the Ems Dispatch might not have provoked France, the southern states might not have joined. Historians argue over how much should be credited to Bismarck's design and how much to circumstances he did not control. What is certain is that German unification excluded the German-speaking Austrians and absorbed the Poles of Posen, the Danes of North Schleswig, and the French of Alsace-Lorraine. After 1871 Bismarck turned to domestic enemies: the *Kulturkampf* against the Catholic Church, the anti-Socialist laws of 1878, and overseas empire in the 1880s. Nationalism had become conservative, a claim to power rather than against it.

## Other states and the unfinished map

**Smaller unifications.** — Germany and Italy were the largest cases, but not the only ones. Belgium became independent in 1830–31 with a liberal constitution. Greece won independence in 1832. Wallachia and Moldavia elected the same prince, Alexandru Ioan Cuza, in 1859 and were recognised as Romania in 1861; Carol I took the throne in 1866. Serbia won autonomy and then independence from the Ottoman Empire in stages, and Bulgaria followed in 1878. Norway and Sweden were joined in one kingdom until 1905. But many national claims remained unanswered: the Poles had no state, divided among Russia, Prussia, and Austria; the Irish pressed for home rule; the Czechs, Slovaks, Croats, and Slovenes had none; the Jews had neither a state nor, in much of eastern Europe, secure citizenship. Nationalism was not a finished programme in 1871.

**The map after 1871.** — By the end of the period the map of central Europe had been transformed. Two new great powers, Germany and Italy, stood where a patchwork of states had been; Austria had been pushed out of Germany and reconstituted itself as Austria-Hungary; France had lost territory and sought allies. The balance of power Metternich had tried to preserve was gone, replaced by an arrangement in which national grievance and ambition drove foreign policy. The nationalism that began as a liberal creed, the creed of Mazzini and the students of 1848, had been captured by the conservative order it once opposed.

## How historians argue about it

**The modernists.** — A generation of historians after the Second World War asked whether the nation caused nationalism or was its product, and most concluded that the nation was the product. Ernest Gellner, in *Nations and Nationalism* (1983), argued that nationalism is not the awakening of an ancient nation but the invention of nations where they did not exist, and that it arose from the needs of industrial society, which required a mobile, literate, standardized population and therefore a single language and schooled culture. The argument explains why national identity became useful, but not why particular identities were chosen; Anthony Smith's ethno-symbolism replies that older ethnic ties were the raw material of modern nations.

**Hobsbawm and invented tradition.** — Eric Hobsbawm, in *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* (1990), argued that the nation is a modern invention, that the popular nation came late — states and middle classes usually got there first — and that nationalism is best understood from above, as a programme of states and elites. With Terence Ranger he edited *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), showing how much of what looks ancient — tartans, ceremonies, flags — was manufactured in the nineteenth century. His critics reply that he underrates popular attachment to nation and the persistence of pre-modern ethnic identities.

**Anderson and imagined communities.** — Benedict Anderson, in *Imagined Communities* (1983), defined the nation as "an imagined political community — and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign." It is imagined because members will never meet most of their fellows, yet carry an image of their communion; limited because no nation imagines itself as coterminous with humanity; sovereign because it replaced the legitimacy of a divinely ordained dynasty. Anderson traced the possibility of this imagining to "print capitalism": the newspaper and the novel let scattered readers imagine themselves as a community moving through shared time. Critics note that "imagined" is often misread as "fake," and that his account, drawn from print societies, underplays the state's violence and the ethnic hatreds nationalism produced.

## Chronology

- **1814–15** — The Congress of Vienna redraws Europe; the Quadruple Alliance and the Holy Alliance are formed.
- **1815** — The German Confederation of thirty-nine states is founded, chaired by Austria.
- **1817** — The Wartburg festival; student associations burn reactionary books.
- **1819** — The Carlsbad Decrees impose censorship and supervise the universities.
- **1821–32** — The Greek war of independence; independence is recognised in 1830 and confirmed in 1832.
- **1830** — The July Revolution in Paris; the Belgian revolution begins.
- **1830–31** — The Polish rising against Russia is crushed; the constitution is abolished.
- **1831** — Mazzini founds Young Italy; Leopold I becomes King of the Belgians.
- **1834** — The Zollverein customs union begins under Prussian leadership.
- **1848** — Revolutions in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Venice, Budapest, and Prague; the Second French Republic; the Frankfurt Parliament meets in May.
- **1849** — Friedrich Wilhelm IV refuses the German crown; the Roman Republic falls; Hungary's declaration of independence is crushed with Russian help.
- **1852** — Cavour becomes prime minister of Sardinia-Piedmont; Mazzini publishes "On Nationality."
- **1853–56** — The Crimean War; at the Congress of Paris in 1856 Cavour raises the Italian question.
- **1858** — The secret agreement of Plombières between Cavour and Napoleon III.
- **1859** — War against Austria; French and Piedmontese victories at Magenta and Solferino; the armistice of Villafranca.
- **1860** — Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand; he enters Naples on 7 September and hands his conquests to Victor Emmanuel at Teano.
- **1861** — The Kingdom of Italy is proclaimed on 17 March; Wallachia and Moldavia are recognised as Romania.
- **1862** — Bismarck becomes Prussian minister-president; his "blood and iron" speech on 30 September.
- **1864** — Prussia and Austria defeat Denmark and take Schleswig and Holstein.
- **1866** — The Austro-Prussian War; the battle of Königgrätz on 3 July; Austria leaves Germany; Italy gains Venice.
- **1867** — The North German Confederation is founded; the Austro-Hungarian Compromise creates the dual monarchy.
- **1870** — The Franco-Prussian War begins in July; Sedan falls in September; Italian troops enter Rome on 20 September.
- **1871** — The German Empire is proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January; the Treaty of Frankfurt annexes Alsace-Lorraine; Rome becomes the capital of Italy.

## Questions to carry

- The Congress of Vienna redrew Europe without consulting its peoples. What made the settlement hold for a generation, and what made it break?
- Was the nation discovered or invented? How would Herder, Mazzini, and Bismarck each answer, and what follows from the difference?
- The revolutions of 1848 began with such hope and were defeated within two years. Which failure mattered most: division among revolutionaries, the loyalty of armies, or the recovery of the great powers?
- Italy was unified by a conspirator's idea, a minister's diplomacy, and a volunteer's army. Did the nation come from below, from above, or from the coincidence of the two?
- The new nation-states of 1871 contained Poles, Danes, Alsatians, Jews, and Slavs. Who was left outside the nation, and what does that tell us about what the nation was for?
