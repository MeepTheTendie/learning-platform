# The interwar crisis and totalitarianism

**Period:** c. 1919–1939 CE · **Region:** Europe

## The peace of 1919

**An armistice, not a surrender.** — At eleven in the morning on 11 November 1918 the guns on the Western Front stopped. The German army had been beaten in the field but not driven back across its own frontier, and no Allied soldier stood on German soil. The armistice was a renewable ceasefire; the peace had still to be written. The men who wrote it met in Paris from January 1919: Woodrow Wilson of the United States, David Lloyd George of Britain, Georges Clemenceau of France, and Vittorio Orlando of Italy. Bolshevik Russia was not invited; Germany was told to accept what was decided. The Treaty of Versailles was signed in the Hall of Mirrors on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo.

**The terms.** — Germany lost about a tenth of its territory and population. Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Eupen and Malmedy went to Belgium; most of West Prussia and Posen to the new Polish state, cutting the "Polish Corridor" to the sea and leaving East Prussia an island. Danzig became a free city under League supervision, Memel went to Lithuania, the Saar was placed under League administration for fifteen years, and Upper Silesia was divided after a plebiscite. Germany's colonies became "mandates" of the victors under Article 22 of the League Covenant, and union with Austria was forbidden. The army was cut to 100,000 men, the navy to 15,000 and six old battleships, with no submarines, military aircraft, tanks, poison gas, or general staff; the Rhineland was demilitarised. Article 231, the "war guilt" clause, declared Germany and her allies responsible for "all the loss and damage" caused by a war "imposed upon" the Allies by German aggression. Article 232 required compensation, and in 1921 the Reparation Commission fixed the bill at 132 billion gold marks.

**A settlement its critics rejected.** — The Germans called it a *Diktat*, a dictated peace, and never accepted Article 231. In Britain the economist John Maynard Keynes resigned from the delegation and published *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), arguing that reparations would cripple Germany and ruin Europe; the book shaped a generation's belief that Versailles was vindictive. Self-determination was applied unevenly: it broke up the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires and created states for Poles, Czechs, Slovaks, Serbs, and Romanians, but roughly a quarter of the people in the new eastern states belonged to some other nation, and the principle was not extended to the colonies. When Germany defaulted on coal deliveries, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January 1923; the government answered with passive resistance and printed money, and by November a dollar was worth some 4.2 trillion marks. Savings were wiped out and the middle classes ruined. The Dawes Plan of 1924 and the Young Plan of 1929 rescheduled the reparations, and Lausanne effectively ended them in 1932, but the republic that had signed the armistice and the treaty carried the blame.

## The League of Nations and collective security

**A parliament of states without power.** — The League of Nations was written into the front of the treaty. Its Covenant bound members to submit disputes to arbitration and not to go to war until three months after a decision (Article 12); to respect one another's territorial integrity against external aggression (Article 10); and, if a member broke these rules, to apply economic sanctions and recommend force (Article 16). It had an Assembly where every member had one vote, a Council of the great powers, and a Secretariat at Geneva.

**Why it did not work.** — The machinery had three weaknesses. The United States Senate rejected the Covenant in March 1920, so the power that had made the League a reality stayed outside it; Germany was admitted only in 1926 and left in 1933, Japan withdrew in 1933, and the Soviet Union joined in 1934 and was expelled in 1939. Under Article 5 most decisions required the agreement of all members represented, so the friend of an aggressor could block action. And Article 16's sanctions were not automatic: the great powers would not risk war to uphold the Covenant, and there was no standing force. The League did useful work in mandates, refugees, labour standards, and disease, but as a guarantee of security it depended on the nations it was meant to restrain.

**The tests it failed.** — In September 1931 the Japanese army in Manchuria blew up a section of its own railway near Mukden and used the incident to seize the province, setting up the puppet state of Manchukuo. A League commission under Lord Lytton reported in 1932 that Japan's action was not self-defence; Japan simply left the League in March 1933. Four years later Italy invaded Abyssinia. The League imposed limited sanctions but excluded oil, and Britain and France pursued a secret plan — the Hoare–Laval Pact of December 1935 — to give Mussolini most of what he wanted; it collapsed when it leaked. Collective security had been tested by two great powers and had not held.

## New states, fragile democracies

**A continent of new frontiers.** — Interwar Europe was a patchwork of states created or enlarged by the peace. Poland, restored after more than a century of partition, fought and won a war with Soviet Russia in 1920. Czechoslovakia, the most industrialised successor state, was a democracy with a large German minority in the Sudetenland. The Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes — renamed Yugoslavia in 1929 — united peoples of different religions under a Serbian dynasty. Austria and Hungary were small, poor rump states; Hungary lost two-thirds of its territory and was governed after 1920 by an authoritarian regent, Admiral Horthy. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania emerged from the Russian collapse, as did Finland.

**The weaknesses of the new democracies.** — Parliamentary government had shallow roots: large peasant populations, weak middle classes, and no tradition of representative rule. Constitutions gave presidents emergency powers that could be turned against the system. The Depression hit farm exports hard and made coalitions look helpless. Between 1926 and 1938 parliamentary government was replaced by dictatorship in Poland, Lithuania, Yugoslavia, Romania, Bulgaria, Estonia, and Latvia; by 1939 only Czechoslovakia, Finland, Switzerland, and Sweden kept free institutions throughout. The new rulers were not all alike — some conservatives, some soldiers, some fascists — but they shared the claim that parliaments were too weak and divided to govern.

## The Great Depression and its political consequences

**The crash and the slump.** — On 24 October 1929, "Black Thursday", the New York stock market fell sharply and kept falling. American banks called in the short-term loans that had sustained European recovery, and the fragile system built on them came apart. World industrial production fell by roughly a third between 1929 and 1932 and world trade by more than half. Governments clung to the gold standard and cut spending to balance budgets, deepening the slump. Unemployment reached about a quarter of the workforce in the United States and nearly a third in Germany, where some six million people were out of work by early 1932; in Britain it passed three million. France, protected by a weaker franc and a rural economy, felt the crisis later, from 1931.

**Why it destroyed Weimar.** — Germany's coalition governments fell apart in March 1930 over unemployment insurance, and Chancellor Heinrich Brüning ruled through President Hindenburg's emergency powers under Article 48, with the Reichstag bypassed. His deflationary policy — cutting wages, benefits, and spending — was meant to prove Germany could not pay reparations, but it made despair general. In September 1930 the Nazi Party rose from 12 seats to 107; in July 1932 it won 230 seats and 37.3 per cent of the vote, the largest party in the Reichstag. By November it had slipped to 196, and the party was short of money, but the constitutional order was exhausted. Hindenburg, an old field marshal, had governed through chancellors of his own choosing; the last two, Franz von Papen and Kurt von Schleicher, intrigued against each other while the largest party waited outside the door.

**The wider political damage.** — The slump discredited liberal economics and parliamentary government together, and electorates turned to parties that promised action without debate: communists on the left, fascists and authoritarian nationalists on the right. The democracies' own remedies — tariffs, quotas, devaluation, empire preference — made the world economy smaller and rivalry sharper. The Depression did not cause dictatorship by itself, but it removed the room for manoeuvre in which fragile parliamentary systems had been surviving.

## Fascism in Italy

**The crisis of the "mutilated victory".** — Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 for promises of territory and received less than its nationalists expected. In September 1919 the poet Gabriele D'Annunzio seized the port of Fiume with a band of veterans, dramatising the grievance of the *vittoria mutilata*, the "mutilated victory". The postwar years brought inflation, unemployment, and a wave of strikes and land occupations — the *biennio rosso*, the "two red years" of 1919–20 — that frightened landowners and the middle classes. The liberal state, led by weak coalitions, seemed unable to keep order.

**Mussolini and the March on Rome.** — In March 1919 in Milan Benito Mussolini, a former socialist journalist, founded the *Fasci di Combattimento*, a movement of veterans, nationalists, and syndicalists. Its instrument was the *squadristi*, armed squads in black shirts who attacked socialist halls, unions, and town councils with the tolerance of the police and courts. In 1921 Mussolini turned the movement into the National Fascist Party and won 35 seats. On 28 October 1922 the squads began a "March on Rome"; the prime minister asked the king to sign a decree of martial law, and King Victor Emmanuel III refused, inviting Mussolini to form a government on 30 October instead. The army could probably have stopped the marchers. The seizure of power was not a coup that succeeded but a bluff the established order declined to call.

**From prime minister to dictator.** — In 1923 the Acerbo law gave the largest party two-thirds of the seats if it won a quarter of the vote, and the 1924 election, fought with intimidation, gave the fascists a huge majority. When the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti denounced the fraud, he was kidnapped and murdered in June 1924; for months the regime wobbled as opposition deputies withdrew to the Aventine Hill. On 3 January 1925 Mussolini claimed full responsibility in the Chamber, and from then the *leggi fascistissime* abolished free assembly and association, muzzled the press, banned opposition parties, and created a secret police, the OVRA, and a Special Tribunal. By 1926 the dictatorship was complete.

**The corporate state and its limits.** — Mussolini's regime rested on more than force. The Lateran Treaty of 1929 ended the quarrel with the papacy, recognising the Vatican and making the Church a pillar of the regime. The "corporate state" organised owners and workers into state-supervised corporations meant to replace class conflict with national cooperation, though it did more for owners than workers. The regime ran the Battle for Grain, the Battle for Births, land reclamation, and the *dopolavoro* recreation organisation, and built a cult of the *Duce* around the slogan "Mussolini is always right". But it never destroyed the monarchy, the Church, the army, or the old bureaucracy; the king could still dismiss Mussolini, as he did in 1943. Italy was a one-party dictatorship, but a less total one than Germany would become.

## Nazism in Germany

**The early movement.** — Adolf Hitler, born in Austria in 1889, served as a corporal in the German army in the First World War and stayed in Munich after it. In 1919 he joined a tiny nationalist group that became the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP), and by 1921 he was its unchallenged leader. The party had its own armed wing, the SA or Storm Troopers. In November 1923, with the general Erich Ludendorff, it attempted a coup in Munich — the Beer Hall Putsch. The police broke it up; sixteen marchers and four policemen were killed. Hitler was tried for treason and used the trial to make himself a national figure. Sentenced to five years, he served about nine months in Landsberg prison, where he dictated the first volume of *Mein Kampf*. The book set out his aims: the union of German-speaking peoples, the conquest of *Lebensraum* — "living space" — in the east, and the removal of the Jews from German life. He also drew a lesson: the party would take power through the ballot box, not another putsch.

**Elections and the backstairs.** — After the putsch the party rebuilt itself and waited. It won only 12 seats in the prosperous year of 1928, and then the Depression transformed its fortunes: 107 seats in 1930, 230 in July 1932, making it the largest party in the Reichstag. Its support was broad rather than class-bound — farmers ruined by debt, shopkeepers afraid of the department store, young men with no work, and voters who had never belonged to a party before. By late 1932 its vote had dipped and its funds were low. But the old conservative elite around Hindenburg, having failed to govern through Papen and Schleicher, decided to use Hitler. On 30 January 1933 Hindenburg appointed him chancellor in a cabinet in which only two other members were Nazis. The conservatives believed they had hired him.

**The seizure of power.** — On 27 February 1933 the Reichstag building burned; a Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested, and the Nazis used the fire as proof of a communist plot. The next day Hindenburg signed a decree under Article 48 suspending the civil liberties of the constitution and allowing arrest and detention without warrant. Thousands of communists and socialists were taken into "protective custody". In the election of 5 March the NSDAP won 43.9 per cent; with its nationalist allies it had a majority but not the two-thirds needed to change the constitution. On 23 March the Reichstag passed the Enabling Act, giving the government power to make laws without parliament for four years, by 444 votes to 94, with communist deputies already arrested or in hiding and the Social Democrats voting against. Within months all other parties were banned or dissolved, the trade unions were smashed, and the states were brought under central control in a process the Nazis called *Gleichschaltung*, "coordination".

**The Führer state.** — On 30 June 1934 Hitler turned on his own movement in the Night of the Long Knives, killing the SA leader Ernst Röhm and much of the SA leadership, along with old enemies such as Schleicher. The army, which had feared the SA, was satisfied. When Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934 Hitler combined the offices of chancellor and president and took the title *Führer*; the army swore an oath to him personally. The regime rested on terror — the SS, the Gestapo, the first concentration camps — and on consent cultivated by propaganda, mass rallies, and the promise of work and national revival. Rearmament and public works cut unemployment, and many Germans accepted the loss of liberty as the price of order. Persecution of the Jews moved step by step: a boycott of Jewish businesses in April 1933, a law removing Jews from the civil service, then the Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935, which stripped Jews of Reich citizenship and forbade marriage or sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews. In November 1938, after a German diplomat was shot in Paris, the regime organised the pogrom known as Kristallnacht, burning synagogues and arresting thousands of Jews.

## The Spanish Civil War

**A republic against its enemies.** — Spain had been a constitutional monarchy under a military dictator, General Miguel Primo de Rivera, from 1923 to 1930. In April 1931 the king left and a republic was proclaimed. Its constitution separated church and state and promised reform, but the country was divided between a Catholic, landowning right and a socialist and anarchist left. The right won in 1933, reversed much of the legislation, and crushed a miners' rising in Asturias in 1934 with colonial troops. In February 1936 a Popular Front narrowly won, and land occupations and street violence increased.

**The war and foreign intervention.** — In July 1936 a group of generals, including Francisco Franco, rose against the republic from Spanish Morocco. The rising succeeded in the conservative north and west and failed in Madrid and much of the south, and the country fell into a three-year civil war. Franco's Nationalists were supplied by Hitler, who sent the Condor Legion, and by Mussolini, who sent some 70,000 troops; the Republic was helped by the Soviet Union with advisers, aircraft, and tanks, and by the International Brigades, some 35,000 foreign volunteers. Britain and France, fearing a wider war, adopted Non-Intervention, which in practice stopped aid to the lawful republic while leaving Germany and Italy free to aid the rebels. The bombing of Guernica by the Condor Legion in April 1937 became the emblem of the war's savagery. The Republic's own divisions weakened it as much as the enemy. Barcelona fell in January 1939 and Madrid in March; Franco ruled until his death in 1975. The war cost perhaps half a million lives and served the dictators as a rehearsal — for bombing, for the German–Italian alliance, and for the passivity of the democracies.

## Appeasement and the Munich Agreement

**The revisionist power.** — Hitler's foreign policy was the revision of Versailles, the union of German speakers, and the conquest of eastern living space. In October 1933 Germany left the League and the disarmament conference; in 1935 it announced conscription and signed a naval agreement with Britain. In March 1936 German troops marched into the demilitarised Rhineland and Britain and France did nothing. In March 1938 German troops entered Austria and the *Anschluss* united it with Germany. Hitler then turned to Czechoslovakia, where more than three million Germans lived in the Sudetenland.

**Munich.** — In September 1938, after weeks of threats, Hitler met Neville Chamberlain, Édouard Daladier, and Mussolini at Munich. Czechoslovakia was not invited. On 30 September the four powers agreed that Germany should receive the Sudetenland in stages, and Chamberlain returned to Britain promising "peace for our time". The Czech government, abandoned by its allies, submitted. In March 1939 German troops occupied the rest of Bohemia and Moravia and Slovakia became a German client; the annexation of non-German territory showed that the claim to protect German speakers had been a pretext. Britain guaranteed Poland at the end of March. In August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression pact dividing eastern Europe between them in secret. On 1 September Germany invaded Poland, and on 3 September Britain and France declared war.

**The argument about appeasement.** — The memory of 1914–18, the fear of bombing civilians, the strain of rearmament, and the belief that Germany had real grievances at Versailles made British and French leaders reluctant to fight. Chamberlain's defenders argue he was buying time to rearm and that opinion would not have supported war in 1938; his critics, including Winston Churchill, argue that a firm stand would have exposed Hitler's bluff while Germany was still weak. The German army's own generals had plotted against Hitler in 1938 and were encouraged by the prospect of war; Munich, and the sense that the West would always give way, discouraged them. What is clear is that the settlement made the next crisis larger: the arms Germany seized in Czechoslovakia equipped a substantial part of its army in 1939.

## The nature of totalitarianism

**A new word for a new kind of rule.** — The word "totalitarian" entered political language in the 1920s. Mussolini used it of his own regime, and by 1925 he could say that for fascism "everything is within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state". Observers of Italy and the Soviet Union noticed that these regimes claimed not merely obedience but the whole of a person's life: work, family, thought, and art. In *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951) Hannah Arendt argued that this was a form of government unlike any earlier tyranny. It rested, she said, not on ordinary ambition but on a mass society of isolated individuals, on a totalising ideology that explained all history, and on terror that made the ideology real; the concentration camp was its central institution. Her examples were Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union.

**What the regimes had in common.** — A later study by Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski, *Totalitarian Dictatorship and Autocracy* (1956), listed features that recur: an official ideology, a single mass party led by a charismatic leader, a secret police using terror, a monopoly of communications, a monopoly of weapons, and a centrally directed economy. Fascist Italy, Nazi Germany, and the Soviet Union shared most of these, though they differed in ideology and in how completely they controlled society. All three used propaganda and spectacle — the Nuremberg rallies, the *Duce*'s balcony speeches — to bind the population to the leader.

**The argument against the concept.** — Other historians argue that "totalitarianism" flattens real differences and exaggerates the control these regimes had. Ian Kershaw's *The "Hitler Myth"* (1987) shows that the Nazi state was not a single machine but a competition of rival offices and party bosses, all trying to guess the Führer's will and "working towards the Führer"; it was a polycratic regime, driven by popular expectations and bureaucratic rivalry, not a smooth total machine. Scholars also distinguish the regimes: Franco's Spain, Salazar's Portugal, and much of interwar eastern Europe are usually called authoritarian rather than totalitarian, because they kept the old institutions, tolerated a limited pluralism, and demanded obedience rather than total transformation. Whether the concept clarifies or obscures remains one of the central disputes about interwar Europe — and it matters, because it shapes how far the collapse of democracy is blamed on the regimes alone, and how far on the societies and elites that made room for them.

## Questions to carry

- Was the Versailles settlement a reasonable peace that Germany refused to accept, or a settlement that made a second war more likely?
- The League of Nations had a Covenant that promised collective security. Why did great powers not enforce it in Manchuria or Abyssinia?
- Mussolini came to power through a march the army could have stopped, and Hitler through a backstairs deal. How much of the collapse of democracy lay in the systems, and how much in the choices of a few individuals?
- What did the Great Depression change about European politics, and what was already broken before 1929?
- Hannah Arendt and Ian Kershaw disagree about whether Nazi Germany was a totalitarian state. What evidence would settle the question, and can it be settled?
