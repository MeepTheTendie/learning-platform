# The first cities

**Period:** c. 3500–1750 BCE · **Region:** Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia)

## The land between the rivers

**The basin.** — The southern plain between the Tigris and the Euphrates — the Greeks called the whole region Mesopotamia, "the land between the rivers" — was built of silt the two rivers dropped into the Persian Gulf, and lies as level as the sea. Both rivers rise in spring from snowmelt in the mountains of Anatolia; rain on the plain itself is scarce and irregular.

**Irrigation and the surplus.** — Farming therefore depended on canals, ditches, and levees that spread the floodwater and drained it away again. Clearing a canal and keeping it clear were tasks no single household could manage, and the water had to be divided by agreement or by force. The surplus the fields produced — barley, wheat, dates, flax — fed people who did not farm: potters, weavers, metalworkers, scribes, priests, and soldiers. The grain grown on these flats astonished Greek travellers, and Herodotus, fearing to be disbelieved, told less than the whole truth.

**The first cities.** — By about 3500 BCE the largest settlements of the south — Uruk above all — had become cities. Uruk covered several hundred hectares and held tens of thousands of people; a brick wall more than nine kilometres long ran around it. At the centre stood a temple raised on a platform of mud brick, the ancestor of the stepped towers called ziggurats. The temple owned land and workshops and stored grain. Around it, houses of mud brick were packed close along narrow lanes, with courtyards and drains; the dead were often buried beneath the floors.

## Writing and number

**Writing for accounts.** — The earliest writing was invented for bookkeeping, not literature. From about 3200 BCE, scribes at Uruk pressed reeds into wet clay to record quantities of grain, beer, and livestock — a picture of a jar or an ox, then a sign for a number. In time the pictures became a fixed set of wedge-shaped marks, cuneiform, and the signs came to stand for sounds as well as things. A script that could write speech could write anything: receipts and contracts, letters, hymns, king lists, and stories.

**The scribes.** — Scribes trained in schools called tablet houses, copying lists of signs and proverbs until they could write quickly and correctly. Writing remained the skill of a small class, and it is through their tablets — accounts, contracts, and school exercises — that most of what survives about daily life has come down.

## Kings, temples, and the first empire

**City-states.** — For most of this period the region was a patchwork of city-states — Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Nippur, and others — each with its own ruler and its own patron god. The ruler was both king and priest, and wars between neighbours over land and water were common.

**Empire.** — About 2334 BCE, Sargon of Akkad conquered the Sumerian cities and ruled them as one state, the first empire in the region. His dynasty fell within two centuries; the Third Dynasty of Ur reunited the land, and that too collapsed. From a small kingdom at Babylon on the Euphrates, Hammurabi (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE) later defeated his rivals and had a code of laws carved on a stone stele and set up in a temple.

## The law of Hammurabi

The stele — a block of diorite about 2.25 m tall — was set up where it could be read. It carries 282 case rulings, not a philosophy of law. Some turn on rank: a man who steals from temple or palace pays thirtyfold, but a poor man pays tenfold, and a thief who cannot pay is put to death. Some appeal to ordeal: a man accused of sorcery goes to the holy river, and the river decides. Some fix the fees and penalties of a doctor according to the rank of the patient. The code shows what the state claimed the right to decide, and who counted.

## Chronology

- **c. 3500 BCE** — Uruk grows into a walled city in southern Mesopotamia.
- **c. 3200 BCE** — The earliest cuneiform tablets appear at Uruk, recording quantities of goods.
- **c. 2900–2350 BCE** — Sumerian city-states compete for land and water.
- **c. 2334–2154 BCE** — Sargon of Akkad rules the region as one state.
- **c. 2112–2004 BCE** — The Third Dynasty of Ur reunites it; its kings build the ziggurat at Ur.
- **c. 1894 BCE** — Babylon, on the Euphrates, begins to expand.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE** — Hammurabi rules Babylon and issues his code of laws.
- **c. 1595 BCE** — Hittite forces sack Babylon; the Old Babylonian kingdom ends.
