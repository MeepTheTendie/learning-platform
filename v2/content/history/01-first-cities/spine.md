# The first cities

**Period:** c. 3500–1750 BCE · **Region:** Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia)

## The land between the rivers

**Two rivers, one plain.** — The Tigris and the Euphrates both rise in the mountains of eastern Anatolia and run south-east some 1,500 km to the Persian Gulf. The Euphrates is slow and navigable; the Tigris is faster, and floods earlier and less predictably. Both rise in spring, fed by snowmelt far to the north. The southern plain between them — later called Babylonia, and by the Greeks Mesopotamia, "the land between the rivers" — was built of the silt the two rivers laid down as the gulf receded, and is almost perfectly flat.

**A land without stone.** — The plain has no stone, no timber, and little rain: only mud, reeds, and water. Its people built in mud brick and wrote on clay. Timber, copper, and stone had to be brought down from the mountains and up from the gulf, which is one reason trade and record-keeping grew so early here.

**Irrigation and the surplus.** — Rain falls mainly in winter and is not enough for a dependable harvest. Farmers led the spring flood onto the fields through canals and ditches and drained it off again, and grew barley, wheat, dates, flax, and vegetables. The work had to be coordinated across whole villages, because a canal serves many fields, and whoever holds its head holds the water. The surplus the fields produced fed people who did not farm — potters, weavers, metalworkers, scribes, priests, and soldiers — and it could be stored, taxed, and traded. In the long run irrigation also drew salt to the surface, and yields fell; salinization became a slow, permanent problem.

## The first cities

**Uruk.** — By about 3500 BCE the largest settlement of the south, Uruk, had grown into a city. It covered some 250 hectares and may have held tens of thousands of people; a brick wall more than nine kilometres long ran around it. At its centre stood the Eanna precinct, sacred to the goddess Inanna, with the White Temple raised on a platform, and nearby the Anu ziggurat. From Uruk come the Warka Mask, a woman's face carved in marble; the Uruk Vase, an alabaster vessel showing offerings brought to the goddess; and the earliest known cylinder seals and cuneiform tablets. Uruk traded far afield: its colonies and trading posts appear as far away as Syria and Iran.

**The temple and the ruler.** — At the centre of the city stood the temple, which owned land and workshops, stored grain, and employed the people who served it. Beside it stood the ruler, shown in art as the "priest-king" — a bearded man in a netted skirt who leads processions and hunts lions. He was both king and priest, and his authority rested on the favour of the city's god.

**Daily life.** — Around the temple, houses of mud brick were packed close along narrow lanes, with courtyards, ovens, and drains; the dead were often buried beneath the floors, with pots and ornaments. People ate barley bread and beer, fish, dates, and onions. Beer was a staple and was rationed to workers. Most people farmed or worked in the temple workshops; a minority were slaves, mostly prisoners of war or debtors.

## Writing and number

**Writing for accounts.** — The earliest writing was invented for bookkeeping, not literature. From about 3200 BCE, scribes at Uruk pressed the end of a reed into wet clay to record quantities of grain, beer, and livestock — a picture of a jar or an ox, then a sign for a number. The first tablets are lists: so many measures of barley, so many animals. In time the pictures became a fixed set of wedge-shaped marks — cuneiform, from the Latin for "wedge" — and the signs came to stand for sounds as well as things, by the "rebus" principle: a picture of an arrow could stand for the sound of the word, and so write a syllable.

**The scribes and their schools.** — Scribes trained in schools called tablet houses, copying lists of signs, proverbs, and model contracts until they could write quickly and correctly. Writing stayed the skill of a small class. From their tablets — accounts, contracts, letters, king lists, hymns, and school exercises — most of what survives about daily life has come down. The first author known by name is a woman: Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad and high priestess of the moon god at Ur, who wrote hymns to Inanna around 2300 BCE.

## Kings, temples, and the first empire

**City-states.** — For most of this period the region was a patchwork of city-states — Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Nippur, Eridu, and others — each with its own ruler and its own patron god. The ruler governed with a council of elders and an assembly of armed men, and war between neighbours over land and water was common. The best-known quarrel is between Lagash and Umma over a strip of border land, the Gu'edena; the Stele of the Vultures (c. 2450 BCE) commemorates Lagash's victory. The Standard of Ur, a decorated box from a royal grave at Ur (c. 2600–2400 BCE), shows an army and prisoners on one side and a banquet on the other — war and peace in a single object.

**Empire.** — About 2334 BCE, Sargon of Akkad conquered the Sumerian cities and ruled them as one state, the first empire in the region. Later legend claimed he had been set adrift in a basket as a baby; in fact he was a court official who seized power. He installed his own governors, standardized weights and measures, and took the title "King of the Four Quarters"; his grandson Naram-Sin was worshipped as a god. The dynasty fell within two centuries, undone in part by the Gutians from the mountains, and a later Sumerian poem, "The Curse of Agade," remembered the fall as divine punishment. The Third Dynasty of Ur then reunited the land; its kings built the great ziggurat at Ur and issued the earliest surviving law code, that of Ur-Nammu (c. 2100 BCE). It too collapsed, when the Elamites sacked Ur in 2004 BCE.

**Babylon and Hammurabi.** — After a century of smaller kingdoms, a city on the Euphrates, Babylon, began to expand under Hammurabi (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE). By defeating his rivals one by one he made Babylon the capital of most of Mesopotamia. He is remembered for a code of laws carved on a stone stele and set up in a temple.

## The law of Hammurabi

**The stele and the god.** — The stele, a block of diorite about 2.25 m tall and now in the Louvre, shows at its top the king standing before Shamash, the sun god and god of justice, who holds out the symbols of rule. Below, 282 case rulings are cut in cuneiform. A prologue and epilogue frame the whole as an act of justice by a king chosen by the gods.

**What the laws assume.** — The rulings are cases, not principles. They divide people into three ranks — the awilum (free man), the mushkenum (a dependent, perhaps a client of the palace), and the wardum (slave) — and the penalty depends on the rank of both parties. A man who causes the loss of a gentleman's eye loses his own eye; if he causes the loss of a commoner's eye, he pays a fine. A surgeon who saves a gentleman is paid ten shekels of silver; if the patient dies, his hands are cut off. Some rulings turn on ordeal: a man accused of sorcery goes to the "holy river," and the river decides. The code shows what the state claimed the right to decide, and who counted.

**Was it applied?** — How far the code was enforced is not known. Some historians read it as a royal statement of justice — displayed, copied in schools, and meant to be admired — rather than a working statute book; others point to court records that follow its rules. Either way, it is the fullest surviving picture of how an ancient state imagined order.

## Chronology

- **c. 3500 BCE** — Uruk grows into a walled city in southern Mesopotamia.
- **c. 3200 BCE** — The earliest cuneiform tablets appear at Uruk, recording quantities of goods.
- **c. 2900–2350 BCE** — Sumerian city-states compete for land and water.
- **c. 2450 BCE** — Lagash and Umma fight over the Gu'edena; the Stele of the Vultures commemorates it.
- **c. 2334–2154 BCE** — Sargon of Akkad and his dynasty rule the region as one state.
- **c. 2300 BCE** — Enheduanna, the first named author, writes hymns at Ur.
- **c. 2112–2004 BCE** — The Third Dynasty of Ur reunites the region and issues the code of Ur-Nammu.
- **2004 BCE** — The Elamites sack Ur.
- **c. 1894 BCE** — Babylon, on the Euphrates, begins to expand.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE** — Hammurabi rules Babylon and issues his code of laws.
- **c. 1595 BCE** — Hittite forces sack Babylon; the Old Babylonian kingdom ends.
