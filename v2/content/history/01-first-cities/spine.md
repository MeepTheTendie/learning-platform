# The first cities

**Period:** c. 3500–1750 BCE · **Region:** Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia)

## The land between the rivers

**Two rivers, one plain.** — The Tigris and the Euphrates both rise in the mountains of eastern Anatolia, in what is now Turkey, and run south-east for some 1,500 kilometres to the Persian Gulf. They are not alike. The Euphrates is broad and slow, and can be floated downstream and, with effort, towed upstream; the Tigris is faster and steeper, and it floods earlier and less predictably, which makes it harder to tame but easier to defend along. Both rivers rise in spring, when snow melts in the mountains, and both carry heavy loads of silt — fine grey-brown soil in suspension. Where they reach the flat land near the gulf they slow and drop it. Over thousands of years that silt built the southern plain, which is why the land between the lower rivers is almost perfectly level and why the gulf once reached much farther inland than it does now. The cities of the south — Ur, Eridu, Larsa — now sit far from open water; in antiquity they sat on the marsh and the sea.

**A land without stone.** — The plain has no stone, no timber, and little rain. There is mud in endless quantity, reeds along the marshes, and water. So the people of the south built in mud brick — mud mixed with straw, pressed into moulds, and dried or baked in the sun — and they wrote on clay, which was the one durable material they had in abundance. Everything else had to be brought in: timber from the forests of the mountains, copper and tin and lapis lazuli from Iran and Afghanistan, cedar from Lebanon, stone from the desert edges. That single fact — a rich land with almost no raw materials — goes a long way toward explaining why trade, accounting, and record-keeping appear here so early. A people who must import nearly everything must keep track of nearly everything.

**The flood and the canal.** — Rain on the southern plain falls mainly in winter and is not enough for a dependable harvest of the crops that grow there. What the land has instead is the spring flood, and the flood is dangerous as well as generous: left alone, it drowns the fields and moves the river. So from an early date the people of the south learned to lead the water where they wanted it, through canals, ditches, and levees, letting the flood onto the fields and then draining it off. This was the basis of everything that followed. Barley, wheat, dates, flax, and vegetables grew in quantity; two harvests a year were possible in places. But the work could not be done by one household alone. A canal serves many fields, and the head of a canal is a choke point: whoever holds it holds the water for everyone below. Building the canal, keeping it clear of silt, and settling who drew water first were tasks that forced people to cooperate, and gave whoever organised them power. In the very long run, irrigation also drew salt up from the soil to the surface, and yields declined; salinization would become a slow, permanent problem that later dynasties tried, and failed, to reverse.

**The surplus and the people who did not farm.** — The point of irrigation was not only to feed the farmers. It was to produce more food than the farmers needed, and that surplus is what made a city possible. Food that could be stored could also be taxed, and food that could be taxed could support people who did no farming at all: potters and weavers, metalworkers and carpenters, scribes and priests, soldiers and officials. A society that can feed specialists has divided labour, and a society with divided labour has a state. The Greek travellers who came to the plain centuries later were astonished by its fertility; Herodotus, who never quite believed his own informants, said he would not report the full size of the barley harvest for fear of being disbelieved. The soil is still fertile. What is gone is the water, because the canals that once spread it now lie choked with sand.

## The first cities

**Eridu and the beginnings.** — The oldest of the southern settlements is Eridu, near the gulf, which the Sumerians themselves regarded as the first city. It grew around a temple to Enki, god of the fresh water beneath the earth. In the fourth millennium BCE, settlements like Eridu, Uruk, and Nippur grew from villages into towns, and towns into cities. What makes a city is not only size but density and difference: many people living close together who do not all do the same work, governed from a centre, surrounded by the fields that feed them.

**Uruk.** — By about 3500 BCE the largest of them, Uruk, had become a city on a scale the world had not seen. It covered some 250 hectares — hundreds of football fields — and may have held tens of thousands of people, though the estimates are disputed. A wall of mud brick more than nine kilometres long ran around it. At its centre lay the Eanna precinct, sacred to the goddess Inanna, goddess of love and of war, and beside it the cult area of Anu, god of the sky, crowned by the White Temple raised on a high platform — the ancestor of the stepped towers the Sumerians later called ziggurats. From Uruk come objects that still carry the city's strangeness and power: the Warka Mask, a woman's face carved in smooth white marble, perhaps the goddess herself; the Uruk Vase, a tall alabaster vessel whose carved bands show worshippers bringing grain, fruit, and animals to the temple; and the earliest cylinder seals, small stone cylinders cut with scenes and rolled across wet clay to sign a document or seal a jar. Uruk did not stay within its walls. Its style of pottery, architecture, and administration spread across the region, and Uruk-style settlements and trading posts appear as far away as northern Syria and western Iran — an expansion so striking that some historians call it a world system.

**The temple and the priest-king.** — At the centre of the city stood the temple, and the temple was not only a place of worship. It owned land and herds and workshops, stored the grain that fed the city, lent and collected, and employed the people who served it — brewers, bakers, weavers, shepherds, scribes. In the earliest period, the temple's wealth and the city's wealth were hard to tell apart. Beside the temple stood the ruler, shown again and again in the art of the period as the "priest-king": a bearded man in a netted skirt and a rounded cap, leading a procession of worshippers, or hunting lions with a spear. He was both king and priest, and his right to rule rested on the favour of the city's god. To disobey him was, in the language of the time, to disobey the god.

**Daily life.** — Around the temple and the palace, ordinary houses of mud brick were packed close together along narrow winding lanes, with a courtyard at the centre, an oven, and a drain. They were small by modern standards and built to last a few generations before being rebuilt on the same spot, raising the ground level bit by bit; the mound of an ancient Mesopotamian city is made of the mud brick of its own houses. The dead were often buried beneath the floors of the houses, with pots, jewellery, and ornaments for the journey. People ate barley bread and drank beer — beer was a staple, safer than water and rationed out to workers — along with fish from the rivers and canals, dates, onions, lentils, and, for those who could afford it, mutton. Most people were farmers or labourers in the temple and palace workshops. A minority were slaves, mostly prisoners of war or people who had fallen into debt; slavery existed, but it was not the foundation of the whole economy.

**The Standard of Ur.** — In the 1920s the archaeologist Leonard Woolley opened a royal cemetery at Ur and found, among the graves, a small wooden box inlaid with shell, red limestone, and lapis lazuli — the Standard of Ur. Its two long sides are usually read as war and peace. On the war side, an army of donkeys and chariots tramples enemies and brings naked prisoners before a seated king. On the peace side, figures carry animals and fish to a banquet where the king and his court drink and listen to music. Whether the object was a standard carried in battle, a soundbox, or something else is not certain. What it shows is the city's two faces at once: organised violence outside, ordered abundance within, and the king at the centre of both.

## Writing and number

**Writing for accounts.** — The earliest writing was invented for bookkeeping, not for literature. From about 3200 BCE, scribes at Uruk pressed the end of a cut reed into a lump of wet clay, making a mark for a thing and a mark for a number. The first tablets are not stories or laws; they are lists. So many measures of barley; so many jars of beer; so many sheep in the flock. The signs began as small pictures — a jar, an ox's head, a fish — drawn with a pointed stylus. As scribes wrote faster, the curved lines gave way to straight wedges pressed with the corner of the reed, and the writing became cuneiform, from the Latin cuneus, a wedge. In time the signs came to stand not only for the things they pictured but for sounds, by a trick we call the rebus: a picture of an arrow could stand for the sound of the word for arrow, and so could be used to write that syllable inside some other word. Once a script could write sounds, it could write anything a voice could say.

**The scribes and their schools.** — Writing was the skill of a small class, and the scribes guarded it. They trained in schools called tablet houses, where pupils copied out lists of signs, proverbs, and model contracts, again and again, until the signs came without thinking; some of the surviving tablets are clearly a schoolboy's clumsy practice beside a master's clean line. From the scribes' work — accounts, contracts, receipts, letters, king lists, hymns, and school exercises — most of what we know about daily life has come down, because clay is almost indestructible. Fire that would burn a library of papyrus bakes clay tablets harder.

**The first named author.** — Literature grew out of the schools. The Sumerians wrote hymns, proverbs, laments, myths, and epics, of which the most famous, the Epic of Gilgamesh, tells of a king of Uruk, his friend Enkidu, and the search for a way to escape death. The first author known to us by name is a woman: Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad, appointed high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur around 2300 BCE, who wrote hymns to Inanna in which she speaks in the first person about her own exile and suffering. She is the earliest person in the world whose words survive with her name attached.

## Kings, temples, and the first empire

**City-states.** — For most of this period the region was not one country but a patchwork of city-states — Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Nippur, Eridu, Kish, Umma, and others — each with its own ruler, its own temple, and its own patron god. The ruler, called in Sumerian the ensi or, when he claimed greater authority, the lugal, "big man," governed with a council of elders and, at least in early times, an assembly of the city's armed men, who could be asked to approve a war. The cities shared a language and a culture — scribes, gods, and customs were much the same from one to the next — but they did not share a government, and they fought constantly over the two things that mattered: land and water.

**War on the plain.** — The best-documented quarrel of the period is between Lagash and its neighbour Umma over a stretch of border land called the Gu'edena. The dispute ran for generations, and one episode is commemorated in a carved stone slab known as the Stele of the Vultures (c. 2450 BCE), which shows the king of Lagash, Eannatum, at the head of his army and the defeated men of Umma beneath his feet, with vultures carrying off the dead. The inscription tells the story from Lagash's side, as such monuments always do: Umma broke the agreement, the gods judged against her, Lagash won. Wars like this were fought with spears, axes, and massed infantry behind rectangular shields, and their purpose was usually not to destroy a rival city but to take its fields and its water.

**The temple, the palace, and the gods.** — Each city belonged to a god, and the gods of the Sumerian pantheon were not remote. Enlil, god of the storm and of the assembly of the gods, was worshipped above all at Nippur, and to control Nippur was to claim the favour of Enlil and with it the right to rule. Enki of Eridu was god of the water and of craft and wisdom; Utu was the sun and justice; Inanna of Uruk was love and war. The gods were believed to own the city and its land; the human king ruled as their steward, and the temple was their house. This is why the temple was so rich, and why the boundary between religion, government, and the economy was not really a boundary at all.

**Empire.** — About 2334 BCE a new power ended the age of independent city-states. Sargon, a man of the northern city of Akkad, seized the throne, conquered the Sumerian cities one by one, and ruled them as a single state — the first empire in the region, and perhaps in the world. Sargon's own origins were modest, and later legend, the Legend of Sargon, told how his mother had set him adrift as a baby in a basket of reeds on the Euphrates, a story that closely resembles the story of Moses told many centuries later. He was in fact a court official, a cup-bearer, who took power. Sargon installed his own governors in the conquered cities, standardised weights and measures across the realm, and took the grand title "King of the Four Quarters" — king of the whole world, as he understood it. He placed his daughter Enheduanna as high priestess at Ur, and his grandson Naram-Sin went further, having himself worshipped as a god during his lifetime. The Akkadian empire held the region for about a century and a half and then broke apart, undone in part by the Gutians, a people from the mountains to the east. A later Sumerian poem, "The Curse of Agade," remembered the fall as the gods' punishment for a king's arrogance — the first surviving attempt to explain why an empire collapses.

**The Third Dynasty of Ur.** — After the Gutians were driven out, the city of Ur reunited the region under its Third Dynasty (c. 2112–2004 BCE). Its kings ruled a centralised state with a vast bureaucracy of scribes who recorded everything: fields, herds, rations, and labour. Its founder, Ur-Nammu, or his son Shulgi, issued the earliest law code that survives, the Code of Ur-Nammu, older by centuries than Hammurabi's and gentler in its penalties: fines rather than death for many offences. Its kings built the great ziggurat at Ur, the best-preserved of the stepped temples, a mountain of mud brick faced with baked brick and topped by a shrine. The dynasty came to a violent end in 2004 BCE when the Elamites from the east sacked the city, and a bitter poem, "The Lament for Ur," mourned the destruction of the temples and the death of the people.

**Babylon and Hammurabi.** — For a century after Ur's fall, the region was divided among smaller kingdoms, and out of that confusion rose a city on the Euphrates named Babylon. Under Hammurabi (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE), Babylon defeated its rivals one by one — Elam, Larsa, Mari, and finally the northern kingdom of Assyria — and made itself the capital of most of Mesopotamia. Hammurabi was a patient and calculating ruler who made and broke alliances as suited him, and he governed as an administrator as well as a conqueror, digging canals, protecting the weak, and issuing the code of laws for which he is remembered.

## The law of Hammurabi

**The stele and the god.** — The code was cut into a stele, a block of black diorite about 2.25 metres tall, which stood in a temple in Babylon and now stands in the Louvre. At the top, in relief, Hammurabi stands before Shamash, the sun god and god of justice, who holds out to him the rod and ring of rule; below, in neat columns of cuneiform, are the laws. The stele is framed by a prologue and an epilogue in which the king declares that the gods chose him "to cause justice to prevail in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil, that the strong might not oppress the weak." The laws themselves sit between the two, as the working out of that claim.

**What the laws assume.** — The code is not a philosophy of law or a set of general principles. It is 282 case rulings, each beginning "If a man…" and ending with what shall be done. They divide people into three ranks: the awilum, a free man; the mushkenum, a dependent of lower status, perhaps a client of the palace; and the wardum, a slave. The penalty often depends on the rank of both parties. A man who causes the loss of a gentleman's eye loses his own eye; if he causes the loss of a commoner's eye, he pays a fine instead. A surgeon who saves a gentleman's life is paid ten shekels of silver; if the gentleman dies under his knife, his hands are cut off. A builder whose house falls and kills the owner is put to death; if it kills the owner's son, the builder's own son is put to death. Some rulings turn on ordeal rather than evidence: a man accused of sorcery goes to the "holy river," and the river decides — if he drowns, he was guilty; if he survives, his accuser is put to death. The code shows what the state claimed the right to decide, and who counted in its eyes.

**Was it applied?** — How far the code was ever enforced is not known and is much debated. Some historians read it as a royal statement of justice — a monument to be displayed, admired, and copied in the scribal schools — rather than a working statute book; the fact that it was copied by scribes for centuries after Hammurabi's kingdom fell suggests it was valued as a text. Others point to court records and contracts that follow its rules, and argue that it reflected real practice. It may have been both: a statement of how a just king imagined order, and a rough guide to how his courts decided. Either way, it is the fullest surviving picture of how an ancient state thought about justice, rank, and the gods.

## Society, economy, and belief

**Ranks and slaves.** — Mesopotamian society was unequal at every level. At the top stood the king and the great temple and palace officials; below them the mass of free farmers and craftsmen; below them the dependents; and at the bottom the slaves, who were mostly prisoners of war, or people sold for debt, or their children. Slaves could be bought and sold, and were marked by a particular hairstyle or a clay tag, but they could also own property, marry, and in some cases buy their freedom. Slavery was real and cruel, and it was not the whole economy: most work was done by free people.

**The temple as an economic machine.** — The temple held more land and put more people to work than anyone else in most cities. It collected grain and wool as rent and offerings, stored them in great granaries, and paid them out as rations to the people who worked its land and its workshops. Scribes recorded every jar of beer and every measure of barley that came in or went out, which is why we can reconstruct ancient wages and rations in detail. The palace ran a similar machine for the king. Trade beyond the city was in the hands of merchants who carried copper, tin, timber, and luxury goods along the rivers and overland, and who kept their own accounts — the very first writing.

**Weights, measures, and the number sixty.** — The Sumerians counted in sixties as well as in tens — a system that survives in the sixty seconds of a minute and the sixty minutes of an hour. They fixed standard weights and measures, so that a mina or a shekel of silver meant the same in one city as another, and kings like Sargon enforced those standards across their realms. Standard measures are a quiet but essential part of how a state works: without them, no tax, no contract, and no army can be trusted.

**The gods and the afterlife.** — The Mesopotamians worshipped a large family of gods, each with a city, a temple, and a sphere. They believed the gods had made humans to do the work the gods did not wish to do, and that the gods could be angry, capricious, and dangerous; much of religion was an attempt to find out what the gods intended and to keep them content. Priests read the future in the entrails of sacrificed animals, in the smoke of incense, in the flights of birds, and in the stars, and the king above all was expected to know the will of the gods. What happened after death was a dimmer matter: the dead went to a dusty underworld, and the living fed them with offerings. The great temples, the ziggurats, and the rituals were built not around hope of heaven but around the ordering of life on earth, which the gods alone could guarantee.

## Chronology

- **c. 3500 BCE** — Uruk grows into a walled city in southern Mesopotamia.
- **c. 3200 BCE** — The earliest cuneiform tablets appear at Uruk, recording quantities of goods.
- **c. 2900–2350 BCE** — Sumerian city-states compete for land and water.
- **c. 2450 BCE** — Lagash and Umma fight over the Gu'edena; the Stele of the Vultures commemorates it.
- **c. 2334–2154 BCE** — Sargon of Akkad and his dynasty rule the region as one state.
- **c. 2300 BCE** — Enheduanna, the first named author, writes hymns at Ur.
- **c. 2112–2004 BCE** — The Third Dynasty of Ur reunites the region and issues the code of Ur-Nammu.
- **2004 BCE** — The Elamites sack Ur; "The Lament for Ur" mourns it.
- **c. 1894 BCE** — Babylon, on the Euphrates, begins to expand.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE** — Hammurabi rules Babylon and issues his code of laws.
- **c. 1595 BCE** — Hittite forces sack Babylon; the Old Babylonian kingdom ends.
