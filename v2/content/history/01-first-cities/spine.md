# The first cities

**Period:** c. 3500–1750 BCE · **Region:** Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia)

## The land

Mesopotamia — the name is Greek for "the land between the rivers" — is the plain between the Tigris and the Euphrates, running from the mountains of Anatolia south to the Persian Gulf. Rain there is scarce and irregular, but both rivers rise in spring from snowmelt far to the north. Farmers dug canals, ditches, and levees to spread the floodwater over the fields and drain it away again, and grew barley, wheat, dates, and flax on land that would otherwise have been dry. Clearing a canal, keeping it clear, and settling who drew water first were tasks no single household could do alone. The surplus the fields produced fed people who did not farm: potters, weavers, metalworkers, scribes, priests, and soldiers.

## The cities

By about 3500 BCE the largest settlements of southern Mesopotamia — Uruk above all — had grown into cities. Uruk covered several hundred hectares and held tens of thousands of people; a brick wall, more than nine kilometres long, ran around it. At the centre stood a temple complex raised on a platform of mud brick, the ancestor of the stepped towers the Sumerians called ziggurats. The temple owned land and workshops, stored grain, and employed the people who served it. Around the temple, ordinary houses were built of mud brick, packed close along narrow lanes, with courtyards and drains; the dead were often buried beneath the floors.

## Writing and number

The earliest writing was invented for accounts, not for literature. From about 3200 BCE, scribes at Uruk pressed the ends of reeds into wet clay to record quantities of grain, beer, and livestock — a picture of a jar or an ox, then a sign standing for a number. Over centuries the pictures became a fixed set of wedge-shaped marks, cuneiform, and the signs came to stand for sounds as well as things. Once a script could write speech, it could write anything: receipts and contracts, letters, hymns, king lists, and stories. Scribes trained in schools called tablet houses, copying lists of signs and proverbs until they could write quickly and correctly.

## Kings, temples, and law

For most of this period the region was a patchwork of city-states — Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Nippur, and others — each with its own ruler and its own patron god. The ruler was both a king and a priest, and war between neighbours over land and water was common. Around 2334 BCE Sargon of Akkad conquered the Sumerian cities and ruled them as one state, the first empire in the region; his dynasty fell within two centuries, and the Third Dynasty of Ur reunited the land before it too collapsed. Later, from a small kingdom at Babylon on the Euphrates, Hammurabi (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE) defeated his rivals and had a code of laws carved on a stone stele and set up in a temple, where any citizen could read it.

## Chronology

- **c. 3500 BCE** — Uruk grows into a walled city in southern Mesopotamia.
- **c. 3200 BCE** — The earliest cuneiform tablets appear at Uruk, recording quantities of goods.
- **c. 2900–2350 BCE** — Sumerian city-states compete for land and water.
- **c. 2334–2154 BCE** — Sargon of Akkad rules the region as one state.
- **c. 2112–2004 BCE** — The Third Dynasty of Ur reunites it; its kings build the ziggurat at Ur.
- **c. 1894 BCE** — Babylon, on the Euphrates, begins to expand.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE** — Hammurabi rules Babylon and issues his code of laws.
- **c. 1595 BCE** — Hittite forces sack Babylon; the Old Babylonian kingdom ends.

## Three vantage points

The documents in this unit come from three different places and times: a Babylonian king's laws, a Greek writer describing Babylon centuries later, and a Hebrew tradition about a city in the same land. They do not tell the same story, and the differences are part of the evidence.
