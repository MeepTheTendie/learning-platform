# Life and thought in the Greek world

**Period:** c. 500–146 BCE · **Region:** Athens · Alexandria · the Mediterranean

## The household and the citizen

**The oikos.** — The basic unit of Greek life was not the individual but the *oikos*, the household: a house, its land, its stores, and the people who lived and worked in it. The head, the *kyrios*, was an adult male citizen, and in law he stood for everyone under his roof — wife, children, unmarried female relatives, slaves, hired hands. He owned the property, arranged marriages, represented the household in the city, and answered for its taxes and religious duties. When an Athenian said "my house," he meant a small economy with a workforce and a larder. Most Greeks lived this way; the household, not the assembly, was where most work was done.

**Marriage and women.** — An Athenian girl was usually married in her mid-teens to a man around thirty, in an arrangement between her father and the groom. She brought a dowry, which remained a claim she had on the household's property. Her life was centred on the inner house, the *gynaikonitis*, and her work was the supervision of weaving, food, children, and slaves; respectable citizen women appeared in public rarely, mostly at festivals and funerals. The ideal was not the whole reality: women went to market, worked the fields at harvest, ran shops when poor or widowed, and managed estates while men were at war. But the public institutions were closed to them: a woman could not attend the assembly, serve on a jury, hold office, or vote. Her citizenship mattered for her children's status and her protection in law, but carried no political voice. Not every city worked this way: Spartan women could own land, and a fifth-century law code at Gortyn on Crete gave women property rights an Athenian woman lacked. Athens was one option among several, not the Greek rule.

**Who counted as a citizen.** — Citizenship was narrow and guarded. From Pericles' law of 451/450 BCE, a man was a citizen only if both parents had been citizens. At eighteen, a youth of citizen birth was registered in his father's *deme*, the district that kept the rolls, then served two years of military training as an *ephebe*. In the 430s BCE there may have been 40,000 to 60,000 adult male citizens out of a total population of perhaps 250,000 to 300,000, though every figure is disputed. Alongside them lived *metics*, resident foreigners — perhaps 20,000 to 30,000 — who paid taxes and served in the army and navy but could not vote or own land; and slaves, whose number is the most disputed of all, perhaps 80,000 to 150,000. The assembly on the Pnyx was a meeting of citizens only. The hands that fed and equipped it belonged to people who were not in the room.

**Whose labour.** — This is the organising fact of Greek political life, easy to lose behind the speeches. A citizen could spend a morning in the assembly, a day in court, or a month rowing because someone else was ploughing, baking, weaving, and carrying water — a wife or daughter, a slave, a metic. The freedom of the citizen was not a general human condition; it was a status defined against those who did not have it. To understand Greek thought and art, keep in view the household and workshop that paid for its leisure.

## Slavery and the mines

**Chattel slaves.** — Greek slavery was chattel slavery: a slave was property, bought and sold, inherited, counted in an estate like an ox. Most slaves in Athens were not Greek but "barbarian" — Thracians, Scythians, Phrygians, Syrians — taken in war or bought in the markets of the eastern Mediterranean, especially Delos and Byzantium. They worked as domestic servants, cooks, farm labourers, shepherds, craftsmen, clerks, rowers, and as the teachers who drilled the sons of the rich. A skilled slave was expensive, and an owner could lease one out. The law gave slaves almost no protection: a slave's testimony was normally taken under torture, on the theory that only pain would make a slave tell the truth, and a master could punish or sell at will. Athens had no slave revolt on the Roman scale, but slaves ran away, and the city offered rewards for their return.

**The mines at Laurium.** — The hardest work was in the silver mines at Laurium, in the hills of southern Attica. The state owned the mineral land and leased it to contractors, who used slave gangs to dig the shafts by hand, haul the ore, and crush and wash it. The work was dark, cramped, and poisonous, done by men and boys fed and housed as cheaply as possible; the death rate was high. Nicias is said to have leased a thousand slaves to a mining contractor, and in the 350s Xenophon proposed that the city buy public slaves and rent them to the mines as steady revenue. The silver paid for the Athenian navy: in 483 BCE, when a rich seam was struck, Themistocles persuaded the assembly to spend the windfall on two hundred triremes rather than distribute it, and those ships helped save Greece at Salamis. The fleet was built on the labour of the mines.

**Was slavery necessary?** — The Greeks argued about it. Aristotle thought some people were slaves by nature, while admitting slavery by conquest might be unjust; the sophists and later the Stoics came closer to saying slavery was convention, not nature. Modern historians disagree about how much of the economy depended on it. M. I. Finley argued that slavery was not a marginal misfortune but a system, and that citizen freedom and chattel slavery were two sides of one coin. Others note that most farms were small and family-worked, that many craftsmen were free or metic, and that the numbers are guesswork; on that reading slaves were pervasive but not the whole foundation. The debate turns on what "necessary" means, and on evidence never collected for our benefit.

## The democracy in practice

**The assembly.** — The *ekklesia*, the assembly of all citizens, was sovereign. It met about forty times a year on the Pnyx, a hillside west of the Acropolis. Any citizen could attend, speak, and vote; a herald asked, "Who wishes to speak?" The assembly declared war, made peace, voted laws and decrees, elected generals, audited officials, and decided finances. Some decisions required a quorum of 6,000. Voting was by show of hands, and the meeting was run by the presiding committee of the moment, not a permanent government. There was no prime minister and no cabinet; the assembly was the government.

**The council and the lot.** — Day-to-day business was prepared by the *boule*, a council of 500, fifty from each of the ten tribes, chosen by lot for a year. It set the assembly's agenda, received ambassadors, and supervised the administration; members were paid and could serve no more than twice. Each tribe's fifty took turns as the *prytany*, presiding for a tenth of the year and living at public expense in the Tholos. The lot — *sortition* — was a principle, not a shortcut: it was thought more democratic than election, which favoured the rich, the famous, and the well-spoken. Almost all offices were filled by lot for one year, with a scrutiny before and an audit after; the main exceptions were the ten generals, elected and re-electable because war required experience. *Demokratia*, the power (*kratos*) of the people (*demos*), meant rule by the many, and especially the poor majority — rule of the poor over the rich, as its friends and enemies alike understood.

**The jury courts.** — The popular court, the *dikasterion*, was the most important institution after the assembly. Each year 6,000 citizens were chosen by lot as jurors and assigned to courts on a trial day by a machine that made tampering difficult. A jury might number 201, 501, or 1,001; there was no judge to rule on the law, and the jurors decided facts and law together, voting by secret ballot without deliberating among themselves. There was no public prosecutor; a private citizen brought the case and argued it himself or hired a speechwriter. Pericles introduced pay for jurors — eventually three obols a day — so the poor could afford to serve, turning the courts from a rich man's pastime into a wage for the ordinary.

**Liturgies and the rich.** — The democracy did not confiscate wealth; it put it to work. The *leitourgia*, or liturgy, was a compulsory public service laid on the rich. The *choregos* paid for a festival chorus; the *trierarch* paid to fit out and command a warship for a year. A liturgy could consume much of a rich man's income, and the rich competed to spend well, because public generosity bought honour and influence. The arrangement was a bargain: the many got pay, power, and festivals; the few got prestige and a voice; both kept it because each needed the other.

**Oligarchic intervals.** — The democracy was not inevitable. Twice, in 411 and 404 BCE, the assembly handed power to small oligarchic councils; the second, the Thirty Tyrants, killed some 1,500 people in a year of terror. Both times the democracy was restored, and the restored version was more careful about the rule of law. The lesson drawn — that the many could be trusted with power and that those who took it away were worse — was an argument, not a fact of nature.

## The theatre as a civic institution

**The City Dionysia.** — Every spring, at the City Dionysia, Athens held a competition of plays in the theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis. The festival was religious and civic at once: a priest of Dionysus presided, officials and generals sat in front, and the citizen body — perhaps women and metics too — filled the stone seats. Through liturgies the state selected three tragic poets, each of whom put on three tragedies and a satyr play, and several comic poets with one comedy each; a wealthy *choregos* paid for the chorus and rehearsals, and the winners were crowned. The audience was large, noisy, and opinionated, and it voted. Theatre was not private entertainment; it was the city thinking in public.

**Tragedy and comedy.** — Tragedy took old myths — the house of Atreus, Oedipus, the Trojan War — and set them in a world of hard choices and human error. Aeschylus wrote of law and justice; Sophocles made the conflict between individual and community almost unbearable; Euripides put slaves, women, and outsiders on stage and made the audience doubt its own values. The survivors are a fraction of what was written. Comedy worked differently: Aristophanes named living politicians, mocked the assembly and the courts, made fun of Socrates, and let ordinary people talk back to the powerful. In the middle of a war, in a democracy, the city paid a chorus to sing jokes about itself — a clear sign of what free speech meant in practice.

**What the theatre did.** — The plays did not settle arguments; they staged them, asking whether the gods were just, whether the city was right to demand obedience, what happened when a woman or a slave told the truth. They gave citizens a shared set of stories and a vocabulary for arguing about power. A play could flatter the demos or accuse it, and the audience decided which. Funded by the rich and judged by the many, the theatre repeated the bargain that ran the courts and the fleet.

## The sophists and Socrates

**The sophists.** — In the mid-fifth century a new kind of teacher appeared: the *sophistai*, "wise men," a word that soon became a slur. They travelled city to city teaching, for a fee, the art of speaking and arguing. Protagoras of Abdera taught that "man is the measure of all things"; Gorgias of Leontini made the weaker argument seem the stronger. They were not a school and did not agree with each other, but they shared the claim that success in public life came from skill, not birth, and that skill could be taught. That was unsettling: if a city's laws and customs were conventions rather than divine commands, what made them binding? The sophists made the question unavoidable; their critics, above all Plato, blamed them for it.

**Socrates.** — Socrates of Athens (c. 470–399 BCE) wrote nothing. We know him from Plato's dialogues, Xenophon's memoirs, and the caricature in Aristophanes' *Clouds*. He was a citizen of modest means who spent his days in the marketplace and gymnasium asking questions. His method — later called the *elenchus* — took a confident opinion, asked what it meant, and drew out contradictions until the person improved the answer or admitted ignorance. He claimed to know nothing, and said the oracle at Delphi had told his friend Chaerephon that no one was wiser than Socrates; he read this to mean he was wise only in knowing his own ignorance. He took no pay and ran no school, calling himself a *gadfly*, a stinging insect sent by the god to keep the sluggish city awake.

**The trial of 399.** — In 399 BCE, five years after Athens lost the Peloponnesian War and four years after the amnesty that ended the Thirty Tyrants, Socrates was prosecuted by three citizens — Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon — for not believing in the city's gods, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young. The political background was heavy: Critias and Alcibiades, two of the most hated men of the recent past, had been his associates. Socrates refused to flatter the jury or abandon his questioning, and was convicted by a narrow margin. Asked to propose a penalty, he suggested a small fine; after the jury voted for death, he told them the unexamined life was not worth living and that they would not easily find another like him. He drank hemlock. The trial became the founding case of the conflict between the philosopher and the city. In its own terms it was a democratic jury, in a city recently torn apart by civil war, deciding what speech it would tolerate.

**The schools.** — Socrates' death shaped his influence rather than ending it. Plato founded the Academy at Athens around 387 BCE, an informal community of scholars that lasted for centuries. Aristotle studied there for twenty years, then founded his own school, the Lyceum, around 335 BCE, teaching while walking — hence "Peripatetic." Isocrates ran a rival school of rhetoric that trained generations of political men. Athens had no university in the modern sense, but by the fourth century a young man from anywhere in the Greek world came there to study, paying fees to teachers who lived on fees and patrons.

## Plato and Aristotle

**Plato's world of forms.** — Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) wrote dialogues in which Socrates is usually the main speaker. His central claim is that the things we see and touch are imperfect copies of eternal, unchanging *forms* — behind every particular triangle lies the perfect Triangle, behind every just act the Form of Justice. Knowledge, on this view, is the mind's recollection of the forms, not the report of the senses. In the *Republic* he asked what justice is and answered with an ideal city ruled by philosopher-kings, men and women trained to know the forms and govern for the whole; he distrusted democracy as rule by the uninformed. Whether the *Republic* was a blueprint or a provocation is disputed; what is certain is that it put the question of who should rule, and on what knowledge, at the centre of political thought.

**Aristotle's systematic mind.** — Aristotle (384–322 BCE) came from Stagira, studied with Plato, and for a few years tutored the young Alexander of Macedon. Where Plato looked up to the forms, Aristotle looked at the world: he dissected animals, classified plants, collected constitutions, and reasoned from observation. He invented formal logic and wrote on physics, biology, ethics, rhetoric, and poetry, and in the *Politics* analysed the household, the citizen, and the forms of government. His ethics is about character and *arete*, excellence, and the middle way between extremes. For centuries his books were the frame within which educated people thought about nature and politics.

**The argument about slavery.** — In the *Politics* Aristotle gives the classic defence of slavery as natural: a slave is a "living tool," property with a body, and some people are by nature fit only to be ruled, as the soul rules the body. He also reports the opposite view — that all slavery is contrary to nature, and that slavery by conquest is force dressed as law — and does not silence it. Aristotle's own household rested on slaves, as did the schools and the leisure of the men who studied in them. The philosophy of the good life was written by people whose lives were supported by the labour of the unfree.

## Hellenistic learning

**Alexander and the successor kingdoms.** — In 334 BCE Alexander of Macedon crossed into Asia and in a decade conquered the Persian Empire as far as the Indus. When he died at Babylon in 323 BCE his generals fought over the pieces, producing great kingdoms — the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Syria and the east, the Antigonids in Macedon — ruled by Greek-speaking kings over mostly non-Greek populations. The old city-state was no longer the centre of the Greek world. Power lay with kings and courts, and culture followed the money. Greek became the language of government and of a cosmopolitan elite from the Nile to the Oxus, and cities founded by Alexander and his successors — Alexandria above all — became the new centres of learning.

**The Museum and the Library of Alexandria.** — The Ptolemies made Alexandria the capital of a Greek kingdom in Egypt and of Greek learning too. They founded the Museum — the *Mouseion*, a "shrine of the Muses," a research institution with salaried scholars and a common dining hall — and the Library, which grew by purchase and copying into the largest collection of books in the ancient world. The librarian's post became famous; Zenodotus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Eratosthenes, and Aristarchus of Samothrace held it in turn. They edited Homer, invented punctuation and critical signs, and catalogued the collection — the figure of 500,000 or 700,000 scrolls is a boast, not a count. Paid for by the wealth of Egypt, the Library made the idea of a universal collection real for the first time.

**Euclid and the axiomatic method.** — Euclid taught at Alexandria around 300 BCE and wrote the *Elements*, a geometry textbook that begins with definitions, postulates, and common notions and deduces hundreds of propositions in order. Its method — start from a few assumptions, derive the rest by strict reasoning — became the model for mathematics and, later, for philosophy and science. The *Elements* was copied, translated, and taught for more than two thousand years. Its author is a shadow; what survived was not the man but the method.

**Archimedes of Syracuse.** — Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE) was a Greek mathematician and inventor in Syracuse, the most original scientist of the age. He worked out the principle of the lever and the law of buoyancy — the story that he ran through the streets shouting "Eureka" is a later anecdote, but the mathematics is his — and used geometry to find the areas and volumes of curves and solids in ways that anticipated the calculus. He also designed war machines that held off the Roman siege of Syracuse, and when the city fell in 212 BCE he was killed by a soldier, supposedly while drawing a diagram in the sand.

**Eratosthenes measures the earth.** — Eratosthenes (c. 276–194 BCE), librarian at Alexandria, estimated the circumference of the earth. He knew that at Syene, in the south, the sun stood overhead at noon on the summer solstice, while at Alexandria it cast a shadow. From the shadow's angle and the distance between the two places he calculated the circumference. His figure, in *stadia*, comes out — depending on which stade he used — to within a few per cent of the true value, though the conversion is uncertain. He also mapped the known world on a grid of latitude and longitude. The measurement is an emblem of Hellenistic science: observation, geometry, and a royal library, answering a question of no practical use to a king.

## Greek culture under Rome

**Conquest.** — The Greek cities lost their independence to Macedon at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, and then to Rome. In 146 BCE the Romans destroyed Corinth, sold its people into slavery, and made Greece a province; the same year they ended the last Macedonian kingdom. In 31 BCE Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium, and in 30 BCE Egypt became a Roman province. The political history of the Greek world was over; the cultural history was not.

**Greek learning in Roman hands.** — The Romans had borrowed from the Greeks for centuries: gods, alphabet, epic and drama, rhetoric, philosophy. Wealthy Romans hired Greek tutors, sent their young men to Athens and Rhodes to study, and filled villas with Greek statues and libraries with Greek books. Greek was the second language of the Roman elite. In the other direction, Greek scholars found patrons in Rome, wrote Roman history in Greek, and taught rhetoric to provincial governors. The relationship was unequal — Rome held the power — but not one-way, and it produced a bilingual culture that shaped the Mediterranean for centuries.

**What survived, and why.** — The texts of Greek philosophy, science, and literature survived because people with money and power wanted them: kings paid for the Library, Roman patrons paid for copies, monasteries copied manuscripts, and Arab scholars translated them and passed them back to Europe. Works that found no patron were lost — most of Greek tragedy, lyric, and history. The intellectual life of Greece was not preserved by the Greek people as a whole but by institutions and individuals, often far away, who found it worth their while. That is the lesson of the household and the mines too: art and thought rested on arrangements of labour, patronage, and power, and lasted only as long as they did.

## Chronology

- **c. 508 BCE** — Cleisthenes reorganises the Athenian citizen body by tribes and demes.
- **483 BCE** — Laurium's silver pays for Themistocles' fleet.
- **480–479 BCE** — Greece defeats the Persian invasion; Athens' power grows.
- **c. 470 BCE** — Socrates is born in Athens.
- **462/461 BCE** — Ephialtes and Pericles reduce the power of the Areopagus.
- **451/450 BCE** — Pericles' law restricts citizenship to those of two citizen parents.
- **431 BCE** — The Peloponnesian War begins; Pericles delivers the Funeral Oration.
- **c. 430s BCE** — Herodotus and Sophocles are at work; the Parthenon is completed.
- **429 BCE** — Pericles dies of plague.
- **c. 425 BCE** — Jury pay is raised to three obols.
- **411 BCE** — An oligarchic coup briefly ends the democracy.
- **404 BCE** — Athens surrenders; the Thirty Tyrants rule for a year.
- **399 BCE** — Socrates is tried and executed.
- **c. 387 BCE** — Plato founds the Academy.
- **384–322 BCE** — Life of Aristotle; he teaches at the Lyceum from c. 335.
- **338 BCE** — Philip of Macedon defeats the Greek cities at Chaeronea.
- **334–323 BCE** — Alexander conquers the Persian Empire; dies at Babylon.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Euclid writes the *Elements* at Alexandria.
- **c. 276–194 BCE** — Life of Eratosthenes; he measures the earth.
- **212 BCE** — Archimedes is killed at the fall of Syracuse.
- **146 BCE** — Rome destroys Corinth and makes Greece a province.
- **31 BCE** — Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra at Actium.

## Questions to carry

- What did the Athenians mean by *demokratia*, and how did that meaning differ from later uses of the word?
- Whose labour made a citizen's day in the assembly possible? How does that change how the speeches are read?
- Why did the Athenians choose officials by lot rather than by election?
- What does the design of the popular courts assume about ordinary citizens as judges?
- Aristotle defends natural slavery and reports the argument against it. What does the shape of his argument reveal?
- Why did the trial of Socrates happen in 399 BCE, and why has it been remembered the way it has?
- What made Alexandria a centre of learning, and who paid for it?
- The Library of Alexandria is often described as a single great loss. What actually happened to ancient books?
- Greek culture spread under Macedonian and Roman rule. What does that tell us about how ideas travel?
- Which parts of Greek intellectual life depended on institutions that could be destroyed, and which survived them?
