# Kingdoms become empires

**Period:** c. 900–330 BCE · **Region:** Assyria, Babylonia, Israel, Phoenicia, Persia

**The question.** — How can one ruler govern peoples separated by distance, language, and custom? No king here could see his whole realm at once. A letter from the Assyrian capital to a governor in the Levant took weeks; a frontier rebellion could be over before the court heard of it. The siege train, the deportation lists, the provincial map, the royal road, and the imperial post are all answers to that problem. Two answers compete. The first is conquest: rule by the army, by terror, and by moving whole populations where the king wants them. The second is administration: rule through local elites, local gods, and local law, bound to the centre by tribute, roads, and officials. Which one an empire leaned on is the difference between Assyria and Persia, and it is the argument of this unit.

## The world the empires inherited

**A corridor between three continents.** — The Fertile Crescent is an arc of cultivable land from the Persian Gulf up the Tigris and Euphrates, across northern Syria, and down the Levantine coast to Egypt. Any army moving between Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and Egypt had to cross the narrow land bridge of Syria and Canaan, which is why the small kingdoms of Israel, Judah, and Phoenicia were never left alone. Assyria lay in the north of Mesopotamia, with its royal cities at Nimrud and Nineveh; Babylonia lay in the south, with Babylon as its great city; Persia lay far east on the Iranian plateau.

## Assyria: the war machine

**The kingdom on the Tigris.** — Assyria had been a power before, but Aramean tribes pressed in from the west and it shrank through the tenth century. It recovered under Ashurnasirpal II (r. 883–859 BCE), who rebuilt the royal city of Kalhu, the biblical Nimrud, and under Shalmaneser III (r. 859–824), whose Black Obelisk shows the Israelite king Jehu bringing tribute. At Qarqar in 853 BCE an Assyrian army met a coalition that included Ahab of Israel; Assyria claimed victory, but the coalition held.

**Tiglath-Pileser III and the standing army.** — The break came in 745 BCE, when a general named Tiglath-Pileser seized the throne and ruled as Tiglath-Pileser III (r. 745–727). He made the Neo-Assyrian empire. He created a professional standing army: troops paid and kept in service, with cavalry, archers, and engineers who could build a ramp to a city wall or dig under it. He annexed Damascus in 732, took tribute from Menahem of Israel, and reduced the Levantine kingdoms one by one. His administrative invention mattered as much. Where earlier kings left defeated rulers in place as vassals, Tiglath-Pileser carved conquered land into provinces, each with a governor, a garrison, and a fixed tribute. The vassal, who could change his mind, was replaced by the province, which could not.

**Sargon II and the fall of Samaria.** — Tiglath-Pileser's son Shalmaneser V besieged the Israelite capital Samaria, and in 722 or 721 BCE the city fell. Shalmaneser died at about that moment, and the next king, Sargon II (r. 722–705), claimed the conquest and recorded that he carried away 27,290 of its people. This is the end of the northern kingdom of Israel. Sargon built a new capital at Dur-Sharrukin, the biblical Khorsabad, and died on campaign in 705.

**Sennacherib and the siege of Jerusalem, 701.** — In 701 BCE Sennacherib (r. 705–681) marched west against a revolt led by Hezekiah of Judah. He took the towns of Judah and besieged Lachish, then demanded Jerusalem's surrender. The Assyrian annals, on a clay prism now in the British Museum, claim Sennacherib shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage" and forced on him a heavier tribute. They do not claim the city was taken. The Hebrew book of Kings says the angel of the Lord struck down 185,000 men in the Assyrian camp, and that Sennacherib was murdered by his sons. Two accounts of one campaign survive, and they cannot both be true in the same sense: one is a victory monument, the other a religious history by the conquered.

**Ashurbanipal and Nineveh.** — Sennacherib's son Esarhaddon (r. 681–669) conquered Egypt in 671, and his son Ashurbanipal (r. 668–c. 631) completed the conquest, sacking Thebes in 663. Ashurbanipal also fought a civil war against his own brother, who ruled Babylon as a vassal; when the city revolted, he sacked it in 648. He is best known for the library he assembled at Nineveh: perhaps 30,000 clay tablets, including the Epic of Gilgamesh. The king boasted of his learning — "I am Ashurbanipal, king of the world, king of Assyria," runs one colophon. When Nineveh burned, the fire baked the tablets harder, which is why so much Mesopotamian literature survives.

**What the annals are for.** — The royal inscriptions are not objective records. They were composed as displays of the king's piety, courage, and justice, carved on prisms, cylinders, and palace walls. They list campaigns, tribute, and building works; credit victories to the god Ashur; inflate numbers, omit defeats, and describe the torture of rebels in detail. The historian Mario Liverani argues that this rhetoric expresses a coherent imperial ideology — a claim to universal dominion in which the king is the agent of the gods and resistance is sin. That is an argument, not a settled fact. Others stress that policy was driven as much by revenue and secure trade routes as by any mission.

**The fall of Nineveh, 612.** — Assyria looked permanent and fell fast. After Ashurbanipal's death the empire was torn by succession struggles. In 626 a Chaldean chief named Nabopolassar took the throne of Babylon and made war on Assyria. He allied with the Medes, an Iranian people from the mountains east of the Tigris, and in 612 BCE their combined armies destroyed Nineveh; the last Assyrian forces held out at Harran until 609. Within a generation the empire that had ruled the Near East for three centuries had no capital and no army. Older histories blamed "decadence"; most now explain the collapse by overextension and the defection of the elites Assyria relied on.

## Deportation and the machinery of empire

**Moving people as policy.** — The Assyrians did not only conquer; they moved. Deportation — the forced transfer of whole communities from one part of the realm to another — was a systematic instrument. Tiglath-Pileser III and his successors resettled conquered populations in Assyria itself, in newly conquered lands, and along frontiers. The purpose was to break the local ties that made rebellion possible and to mix peoples so that no conquered nation could act as one. The annals count deportees in the tens of thousands at a time; the historian Bustenay Oded estimated that over the life of the empire the total ran into the millions. The policy was not mass murder, but it was a vast act of demographic engineering. Its most famous consequence is the fate of the northern kingdom of Israel: after 722 its people were resettled and replaced, and later tradition called them the ten lost tribes.

**Provinces, vassals, and tribute.** — Annexed land became provinces, governed by Assyrian officials with garrisons and tax quotas. Land left to a native king became a vassal state, bound by treaty and an oath of loyalty, required to pay tribute, supply troops, surrender fugitives, and deal only with Assyria abroad. Tribute came in silver, gold, horses, timber, and labour, which the palace collected and redistributed to soldiers, officials, and temples. Roads, way-stations, and messengers tied the provinces to the capital: the same infrastructure that let the army march let the tribute flow.

## Israel and Judah

**Two kingdoms.** — According to the Hebrew Bible, Israel and Judah were once united under Saul, David, and Solomon, and split around 930 BCE into a northern kingdom called Israel and a southern kingdom called Judah. Israel was larger and less stable; its capital was eventually Samaria. Judah was smaller, and its Davidic dynasty held Jerusalem for centuries. The Bible is the main narrative source for both, written by scribes in Judah and often hostile to the north and to kings it disliked. Other evidence checks it: the Mesha Stele, a ninth-century Moabite inscription, boasts of victories over Israel, and the Tel Dan Stele, found in 1993, refers to the "House of David." These confirm that real kingdoms and a real Davidic line existed; they do not confirm the size and glory the Bible attributes to the united monarchy.

**Prophecy as political criticism.** — From the eighth century BCE the Hebrew Bible preserves the words of prophets: Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, and later Jeremiah and Ezekiel. A prophet here is not mainly a fortune-teller. He claims to speak for the god of Israel and delivers that message to kings and people, often against them. Amos, a herdsman preaching in the northern kingdom around 750 BCE, denounces the wealthy for crushing the poor and announces that the kingdom will fall — and it did, a generation later. Jeremiah, in the last years of Judah, says resistance to Babylon is futile and the city will fall, which is why he was beaten and imprisoned. Whether the prophetic books record the prophets' own words or later editors' reshaping of them is debated; what is clear is that they are argument, not reportage.

**The Hebrew Bible as a source.** — The Hebrew Bible was not written all at once. Its books rest on older oral traditions, royal annals, temple records, and prophetic collections, and they were edited over centuries, much of it during and after the Babylonian exile. The sequence from Joshua to 2 Kings, which scholars call the Deuteronomistic History, explains the fall of both kingdoms as the punishment of a god who had warned his people again and again. That is a theological argument, and it shapes what the text includes and omits. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001), argue from archaeology that the united monarchy of David and Solomon was at most a small chiefdom and that the exodus and conquest narratives reflect much later concerns. Others, including William Dever, reply that archaeology supports a real, if modest, early kingdom and that the writers preserved genuine memories even when they shaped them. The text is excellent evidence for what its editors believed, and only sometimes evidence for what happened.

**The fall of Samaria, 722, and the fall of Jerusalem, 586.** — The two catastrophes must be kept distinct. In 722 or 721 BCE the Assyrians took Samaria and ended the northern kingdom; Judah survived as a vassal. In 701 BCE Hezekiah rebelled and was punished, but Jerusalem was not taken. A century later King Josiah was killed at Megiddo in 609 by the Egyptian pharaoh Necho. When King Jehoiakim rebelled against Babylon, Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem; in 597 the city surrendered, the young king Jehoiachin and thousands of the elite were deported, and Zedekiah was installed as a vassal. When Zedekiah too rebelled, the Babylonians returned. In 586 BCE they breached the walls, destroyed the temple of Solomon, blinded Zedekiah and killed his sons, and deported a second and larger group.

## Babylon and the exile

**The Neo-Babylonian empire.** — The dynasty that destroyed Assyria was Chaldean, a people of the southern marshes, and its first king was Nabopolassar (r. 626–605). His son Nebuchadnezzar II (r. 605–562) won the battle of Carchemish in 605 and made Babylon the centre of an empire reaching from the Levant to the Persian Gulf. Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt his capital on a grand scale: the Ishtar Gate, faced with blue glazed bricks, lions, and dragons; the Processional Way along which the god Marduk was carried at the new year festival; and the great ziggurat called Etemenanki. Later Greek writers described the Hanging Gardens as a wonder of the world; no Babylonian text mentions them. The king's legitimacy rested on Marduk and on the new year festival, in which the king took the god's hand and was confirmed in office.

**The exile.** — The deportations of 597 and 586 carried the Judean elite — royal family, priests, scribes, craftsmen, soldiers — to settlements in and around Babylon. They did not vanish into a melting pot. The exiles kept their genealogies, laws, and stories; the prophet Ezekiel, himself among them, preached by the canal Chebar; they gathered for prayer and study in a land where the temple could not be reached. A letter attributed to Jeremiah advises them to settle down: build houses, plant gardens, marry, and seek the welfare of the city where they live, for in its welfare they will find their own. Psalm 137 gives the other mood, of grief and rage, as the exiles hang their harps on the willows and refuse to sing the songs of Zion in a strange land. The exile lasted roughly fifty years for the first group and less for the second, but its effect was permanent: much of the Hebrew Bible was collected and edited in this period, the Sabbath and circumcision took on new weight as marks of identity, and the idea of a people defined by law and memory rather than by land and king became possible.

## Phoenicia and the sea

**Cities, not an empire.** — The Phoenicians never formed a single state but were the people of a string of port cities on the coast of what is now Lebanon and northern Israel: Arwad, Byblos, Sidon, and above all Tyre, each with its own king and its own god. They traded textiles dyed purple from the murex sea-snail — the dye that gave the Phoenicians, and later the Romans, their royal colour — along with glass, metalwork, wine, and oil. The Bible remembers Hiram of Tyre supplying Solomon with cedar and craftsmen for the temple in Jerusalem.

**Colonies and Carthage.** — Phoenician trade produced colonies: Kition on Cyprus, Utica and Gades in the west, and, according to later tradition, Carthage in what is now Tunisia, said to have been founded by Tyre around 814 BCE. Carthage grew into the greatest naval and commercial power of the western Mediterranean and fought Rome for control of it. Our sources for Carthage are mostly Greek and Roman and hostile, which is one reason the debate over Phoenician and Punic religion — including the evidence of child sacrifice at the tophets, the open-air sanctuaries where urns of burnt bones have been found — is so heated. Tyre was besieged by Nebuchadnezzar for thirteen years and taken by Alexander in 332 BCE after he built a causeway out to its island fortress.

**The alphabet.** — The Phoenicians' most lasting invention was a script. Building on earlier Canaanite writing, they developed by about 1050 BCE an alphabet of twenty-two signs, each standing for a consonant, written right to left. It had no vowels, but it needed only a few dozen signs and could be learned quickly. Because they wrote on papyrus and parchment, which rot in the damp Levant, almost nothing of their own literature survives. Greek traders adapted the alphabet around 800 BCE, adding signs for vowels, and from the Greek alphabet came the Latin one used here; the Aramaic branch became the administrative script of the Persian empire. A writing system cheap enough for merchants and clerks outlasted every empire in this unit.

## The Achaemenid empire

**Cyrus and the conquest of Babylon.** — The Achaemenid Persians came from Anshan and Parsa on the Iranian plateau and were for a time subjects of the Median empire to their north. Cyrus II (r. c. 559–530 BCE) changed that. He defeated the Medes around 550, conquered Lydia and its king Croesus around 546, and in 539 entered Babylon. The fall of Babylon seems to have been almost bloodless: the Babylonian Chronicle describes the army of Cyrus entering the city, and the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay barrel inscribed in Babylonian cuneiform and buried in Babylon's foundations, presents Cyrus as chosen by the city's god Marduk. In it Cyrus claims to have restored neglected sanctuaries, returned the images of the gods to their temples, and gathered the displaced inhabitants of the region and returned them to their homes. The Hebrew Bible welcomed him: the prophet we call Second Isaiah calls Cyrus the Lord's anointed, and the book of Ezra records a decree permitting the Judeans to return and rebuild their temple.

**How to read the Cyrus Cylinder.** — The cylinder was found at Babylon in 1879 and is now in the British Museum. In the twentieth century it became famous as an early statement of religious toleration, and the United Nations displayed a copy in 1971. That reading has been challenged. The historian Amélie Kuhrt argued in a 1983 article that the cylinder is a traditional Babylonian foundation inscription, written in the idiom Babylonian priests expected, and that it shows Cyrus adopting the local language of legitimate kingship rather than announcing a general policy of human rights. It tells us what the priests of Marduk wanted and what Cyrus wanted them to believe. The disagreement between the "toleration" reading and Kuhrt's reading is a disagreement about how much we can infer about policy from a single monument.

**Darius and the crisis of 522.** — Cyrus's son Cambyses conquered Egypt in 525 and died three years later, and the empire was thrown into confusion. Darius I (r. 522–486), a distant relative, killed a man he called the false Smerdis and seized the throne, then spent a year defeating revolts. He commemorated the crisis in the Behistun inscription, carved high on a cliff beside the road from Babylon to Ecbatana and written in three languages and three scripts: Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian cuneiform. The text lists nineteen battles in a single year, names the rebel leaders, and presents Darius as king by the will of Ahura Mazda, who helped him defeat the lie. Behistun is a victory monument, and its account is Darius's own; but it is also the key that unlocked the decipherment of cuneiform, when the nineteenth-century scholar Henry Rawlinson copied and studied it.

**Satrapies, the Royal Road, and the king's eyes.** — Darius is the great administrator of the Persian empire. He organized the realm into provinces called satrapies — the word comes from the Old Persian for a protector of the kingdom — and Herodotus, writing a century later, counts about twenty and describes the tribute each paid. A satrap was usually a Persian noble or a member of the royal family, and he governed his province, collected tribute, raised troops, and judged cases, in theory on the king's behalf. In practice a satrap could become very powerful and could revolt, which is why the kings set checks around him: royal secretaries and treasurers, garrisons under separate commanders, and inspectors whom the Greeks called the king's eyes and ears. The Royal Road ran from Sardis to Susa, roughly 2,700 kilometres, with relay stations where couriers changed horses. The empire used Aramaic as its administrative language, issued the daric, a gold coin, and standardized weights and measures. This is the administrative answer to the question with which this unit began, and it is the opposite of Assyrian deportation: keep the local elites, the local gods, and the local law, and bind them to the centre with roads, money, and officials.

**Persepolis and the imperial style.** — Around 518 BCE Darius began a new ceremonial capital at Persepolis, on a terrace at the foot of a mountain. Its centrepiece is the Apadana, an audience hall whose stairways are carved with processions of delegates from the peoples of the empire, each in distinctive dress, each bringing a gift. The reliefs show no battle and no king trampling a defeated enemy; they show ordered movement toward the king, who sits in majesty while his subjects come in peace. This is an imperial ideology of a different kind from Assyria's: not terror but order, not conquest but willing tribute.

**Zoroastrianism and the gods of the king.** — The religion associated with the Achaemenids is Zoroastrianism, named for Zarathustra, whose hymns, the Gathas, are preserved in the Avesta. Its central figure is Ahura Mazda, the wise lord, opposed by Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit, in a cosmic struggle between truth and the lie. Later Zoroastrian tradition developed a strong eschatology — judgment after death, heaven and hell — a priesthood, the Magi, and the ritual use of fire. The Achaemenid kings invoked Ahura Mazda and presented themselves as ruling by his favour, but they did not impose one religion on the empire. Cyrus claimed Marduk as his patron in Babylon; Cambyses and Darius took the titles and rituals of Egyptian kingship; local temples were supported. Whether the Achaemenids were strict Zoroastrians, or whether their religion was a looser Iranian cult, is debated, and so is how much Zoroastrian ideas influenced Judaism and, through it, Christianity and Islam.

**The limits of Persian rule.** — Persian administration was effective, but not all-powerful, and it did not try to make everyone Persian. The empire had repeated revolts: the Ionian Greeks rose in 499 BCE and were put down, Egypt rebelled more than once, and Babylon revolted and was punished. The invasions of Greece under Darius, defeated at Marathon in 490, and under Xerxes, whose campaign of 480–479 ended at Salamis and Plataea, showed the limits of what a huge but slow-moving empire could project across the Aegean. The deeper limit was structural. The Persians ruled through local elites who could change sides; they depended on the loyalty of satraps who might aim at the throne; and distance still mattered. The empire was a patchwork held together by the king's person, the king's army, and the king's roads, and it lasted two hundred years, longer than any empire before it.

## The end of the age

**Alexander.** — The Achaemenid empire did not collapse from within. It was conquered from outside, and quickly. Philip II of Macedon reformed the Macedonian army, and his son Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 BCE. He defeated Darius III at Issus in 333 and again at Gaugamela in 331, took the Persian capitals including Persepolis, and burned the palace there in 330; the same year, Darius III was murdered by his own satrap Bessus. In four years Alexander had destroyed the largest empire the world had yet seen. This is a lesson in contingency: the Persian empire was rich, populous, and well administered, and its fall was not written in its structure. It fell because Darius lost two decisive battles and because Alexander commanded an army drilled to a finer edge. Alexander's successors divided the empire into kingdoms that kept the Persian machinery — the satrapies, the tribute, the roads, the Aramaic chancery — and Greek kings ruled Egyptians, Babylonians, and Persians by much the same methods the Achaemenids had used.

## Chronology

- **c. 1200 BCE** — The Late Bronze Age empires collapse; smaller states and new armies emerge.
- **c. 1050 BCE** — The Phoenician alphabet takes shape on the Levantine coast.
- **745–727 BCE** — Tiglath-Pileser III creates the professional army and the provincial system.
- **722/721 BCE** — Samaria falls; the northern kingdom of Israel ends.
- **701 BCE** — Sennacherib campaigns in the west and besieges Jerusalem; the city is not taken.
- **668–c. 631 BCE** — Ashurbanipal rules; the library of Nineveh is assembled.
- **612 BCE** — Nineveh falls to the Medes and Babylonians.
- **609 BCE** — The last Assyrian forces are defeated at Harran; Josiah of Judah dies at Megiddo.
- **597 BCE** — Jerusalem surrenders; the first deportation of Judeans to Babylon.
- **586 BCE** — Jerusalem and its temple are destroyed; the exile.
- **559–530 BCE** — Cyrus II rules Persia.
- **550 BCE** — Cyrus defeats the Medes.
- **539 BCE** — Cyrus enters Babylon; the Neo-Babylonian empire ends.
- **525 BCE** — Cambyses conquers Egypt.
- **522–486 BCE** — Darius I rules; the Behistun inscription and the satrapies.
- **480–479 BCE** — Xerxes invades Greece and is defeated at Salamis and Plataea.
- **334–330 BCE** — Alexander conquers the Persian empire.
- **330 BCE** — Persepolis is burned; Darius III is killed.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the Assyrians deport whole populations, and what did the policy assume about loyalty and resistance?
- The annals of Sennacherib and the book of Kings describe the same campaign of 701 BCE. Where they disagree, which details can be checked against other evidence, and which cannot?
- The Cyrus Cylinder has been called a charter of toleration and a piece of ordinary royal propaganda. What kind of document is it, and what does it not say?
- What did the Persian empire keep of Assyrian and Babylonian government, and what did it change?
- Is the alphabet a cause or a consequence of trade and empire?
- The exile is usually told as a catastrophe. What did it make possible?
- Which answer to the organizing question lasted longer — conquest or administration — and why?
