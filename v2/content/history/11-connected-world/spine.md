# A more connected medieval world

**Period:** c. 1200–1500 CE · **Region:** Eurasia and the Mediterranean

## The braided routes

**A world already joined.** — Long-distance exchange across Eurasia did not begin with the Mongols. Roman coins reached India, Chinese silk reached Rome, and the monsoon winds carried Arab and Chinese ships on a seasonal round centuries before 1200. What changed in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries was the scale and safety of the routes. The Mongol conquests joined a belt of territory from Korea to Hungary under rulers who taxed trade rather than strangling it, and for a time a merchant could travel from the Black Sea to the China coast under a single imperial peace. The Indian Ocean remained the busiest highway of all: the monsoon blows south-west from June to September and north-east from November to March, and sailors who understood the reversal could cross the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal on a predictable calendar. Neither network was a single line. The Silk Roads were a shifting braid of tracks; the sea routes a chain of ports, each with its own customs house and merchant quarter. This unit keeps one question in view: how can greater connection transmit both opportunity and disruption? The same road that carried porcelain, silver, and mathematics also carried soldiers, taxes, and, in the 1340s, a bacillus.

**The steppe and the sown.** — The spine of the overland system was the Eurasian steppe, a band of grassland from Manchuria through Mongolia and the Kazakh plain to Hungary. It was poor for grain but rich in pasture, and bred a way of life built on mobility. The Turkic and Mongol pastoralists of the steppe traded horses, hides, and wool for grain, cloth, and metal with the farmers to their south, and when trade failed they raided. In the early thirteenth century the balance broke in favour of a single steppe power.

**What connection was not.** — Movement is not improvement. The routes that carried ideas and goods also carried tribute extracted from conquered peoples, the slave trade of the Black Sea and the Sahara, the impressment of craftsmen, and epidemic disease. The Mongol peace was imposed by conquest and maintained by the threat of massacre. Opportunity and disruption were not two stories but one.

## The Mongols

**Temüjin becomes Chinggis Khan.** — The founder of the Mongol empire was born around 1162 in the Onon valley, in what is now north-eastern Mongolia, and named Temüjin. His father, a minor chief named Yesügei, was poisoned by Tatars when Temüjin was a boy; the family was abandoned by its own people and survived for a time on roots, fish, and game. None of this made his later rise inevitable. Temüjin's achievement was to weld the fractious tribes into a disciplined following by rewarding loyalty over birth and crushing the old aristocratic lineages that resisted him. In 1206, at a great assembly on the Onon, the chiefs proclaimed him Chinggis Khan — a title usually rendered "universal ruler" — and the Mongol state was born.

**The conquests.** — The campaigns that followed were astonishing in speed and reach. Mongol armies entered northern China and took the Jin capital in 1215; they destroyed the Khwarazmian empire in Central Asia between 1219 and 1221 after its ruler killed a Mongol trade mission. Chinggis Khan died in 1227, leaving an empire already larger than any in the world. His successors kept going: Poland and Hungary were invaded in 1241, Baghdad was sacked in 1258, and the Song dynasty of southern China was conquered in 1279. The reasons were military and organisational. The Mongols fielded light cavalry with composite bows, moved in coordinated columns across huge distances, and learned siege warfare from Chinese and Muslim engineers. They organised the army on a decimal system of tens, hundreds, and thousands, promoted commanders by ability, gathered intelligence through merchants and spies, and used massacre — or its reputation — as an instrument to induce surrender.

**Khanates and the orda.** — A *khan* was a ruler; the title is Turkic and Mongol, and "Great Khan" meant the senior member of the dynasty, though seniority was always contested. After Chinggis Khan the empire passed through his sons and grandsons — Ögedei, Güyük, Möngke. When Möngke died in 1259, his brothers Kublai and Ariq Böke fought a civil war, and the unity of the empire broke. By the 1260s four khanates had emerged. The Yuan dynasty ruled China and Mongolia; the Ilkhanate ruled Persia and Iraq; the Chagatai khanate ruled Central Asia; and the western khanate, called by Russian chroniclers the Golden Horde, ruled the steppe from the Volga to the Danube and extracted tribute from the Russian principalities. The word *orda* — the origin of the English "horde" — meant a ruler's encampment and court, and by extension the polity and its army; it was not a wild swarm but a moving capital with officials and craftsmen.

**The yam.** — The most famous institution of the Mongol peace was the *yam*, a relay system of post-houses and horses that let imperial messengers and officials move at great speed. Marco Polo, who served at Kublai Khan's court in the 1270s, describes stations at intervals of twenty-five to thirty miles, each with a large hostel and a reserve of two hundred to four hundred horses; runners with bells, he says, carried urgent news a three-mile stage at a time, and special couriers could cover two hundred miles in a day. His numbers — more than ten thousand post-houses and three hundred thousand horses — are almost certainly inflated, but the system itself is confirmed by Chinese and Persian sources. Riders carried a tablet of authority, the *paiza*, that entitled them to fresh horses and provisions; villagers near a station had to supply the horses and fodder. The yam was an instrument of government — orders, taxes, intelligence — but it also made long-distance travel practical for merchants and envoys.

**Law and religion.** — The Mongols governed a patchwork of faiths. There was a body of customary law and decrees associated with Chinggis Khan, conventionally called the Yassa, though no full text survives and its content is debated. In practice the khans taxed and recruited across religious lines and generally exempted the clergy of the major religions — Buddhist, Christian, Muslim, Taoist — from taxation and military service. This was not religious liberty in a modern sense but a policy of keeping the peace among subject peoples and drawing on every source of skill and revenue. William of Rubruck, a Franciscan friar who reached Möngke Khan's court in 1253, records the khan's own summary of the arrangement: that God has given different ways to different peoples, as a hand has different fingers, and that the Mongols, who had their diviners, lived in peace. The exchange is a rare case of a ruler stating his religious policy in his own words, even as reported by a hostile witness.

## The Silk Roads

**Goods and their routes.** — The overland routes carried luxuries with a high value to weight, because moving goods by pack animal was enormously expensive. Silk, porcelain, and tea moved west; horses, glass, wool, and silver moved east; spices, gems, and cotton textiles moved in every direction. The main trunk ran from the Chinese capital at Chang'an through the Gansu corridor to Dunhuang, around the Tarim basin through Kashgar, across the mountains to Samarkand and Bukhara, on to Merv and Nishapur, and so to Baghdad, Tabriz, and the Black Sea ports, or south to the Persian Gulf. Caravan cities such as Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv, and Tabriz grew rich on the traffic and the services they supplied. Along the roads stood caravanserais — walled inns, often a day's journey apart, where caravans could water their animals, store goods, and sleep behind locked gates. The system depended on local rulers who taxed the traffic but also protected it; when that protection failed, the routes moved.

**Ideas and techniques.** — Goods were never all that moved. Papermaking, which had reached the Islamic world from China in the eighth century, reached Europe through Muslim Spain and Italy; gunpowder and the earliest firearms travelled west along the same roads; printing, astronomy, and mathematics moved in both directions. The historian Thomas Allsen has argued that the Mongol empire worked as a great conduit for the exchange of knowledge and personnel, moving administrators, astronomers, doctors, and craftsmen from one end of Eurasia to the other as the khans demanded. Muslim physicians served in China and Chinese engineers in Persia. This was not a deliberate programme of enlightenment. It was a byproduct of conquest and of the court's appetite for useful skills, but the effect was a real thickening of connections between distant societies.

**The limits of the roads.** — The overland system was fragile. It depended on imperial peace, and after about 1340 that peace decayed: the khanates fragmented, the plague disrupted populations and markets, and the Ming state that replaced the Yuan in 1368 turned its back on much of the overland trade. By the fifteenth century the Silk Roads carried far less traffic and prestige. The maritime routes, by contrast, kept growing.

## The Indian Ocean

**The monsoon and the ships.** — The Indian Ocean was the greatest trading system of the medieval world. Its clock was the monsoon: the south-west wind blew ships east from Arabia and East Africa toward India from spring to summer, and the north-east wind carried them back in winter. Sailors had learned to read the pattern centuries earlier, and the result was a web of ports from the Red Sea and the Swahili coast to Gujarat, Malabar, Bengal, the Malay peninsula, and southern China. Two shipbuilding traditions met in these waters: the Arab and Indian *dhow*, with its lateen sail, and the Chinese junk, with its battened sails and watertight compartments. Goods changed hands at every port along the way.

**Ports, goods, and merchants.** — The great entrepôts were Aden and Hormuz at the mouths of the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, Cambay in Gujarat, Calicut on the Malabar coast, Malacca at the narrows of the Malay peninsula, and Quanzhou, which the sources call Zaytun, on the Chinese coast. Pepper from Malabar, cinnamon and cloves from the islands of south-east Asia, cotton textiles from Gujarat and Bengal, horses from Arabia and Persia, and Chinese porcelain and silk all moved through them. No single state controlled the ocean. Trade was carried by merchant communities — Arab and Persian Muslims, Gujaratis, Tamils, Chinese, and others — who settled in port cities far from home and organised themselves through family firms, credit, and shared law. Ibn Battuta, a Muslim jurist from Tangier who travelled for nearly thirty years, served as a judge in Delhi and the Maldives and described the ports and merchant life of the ocean from the Red Sea to China. His account is a reminder that the network was held together not only by winds and ships but by a shared commercial and legal culture.

## China from Song to Ming

**The Song commercial revolution.** — The China that the Mongols conquered was already the richest society on earth. Under the Song dynasty (960–1279) the population passed a hundred million, and cities such as Kaifeng and Hangzhou held well over a million people. The Song state issued paper money, collected taxes in cash, and ran granaries, arsenals, and a huge bureaucracy staffed by examination-selected scholars. Workshops produced porcelain, silk, and iron on a scale no other region matched; woodblock printing and, by the eleventh century, movable type made books cheaper; and Chinese shipbuilders built the junks that would dominate the sea routes. This was a market economy with banks and credit, and it was the destination that drew merchants from across Asia.

**The Yuan and the fall of the Song.** — Kublai Khan, a grandson of Chinggis, took the title of Great Khan in 1260 and moved his capital to what is now Beijing, which the sources call Khanbaliq or Cambaluc. In 1279 his forces completed the conquest of the Song, and for the first time all of China was ruled by a foreign dynasty. The Yuan kept much of the Chinese administrative machine but placed Mongols and Central Asians above the Chinese, and used Muslim and Central Asian officials to manage finance and trade. Paper money circulated widely, the yam connected the capital to the provinces, and merchants such as Marco Polo reached the court. Mongol rule was resented, and it was also short: rebellions, plague, and fiscal crisis brought the dynasty down in 1368.

**The Ming and Zheng He.** — The Ming dynasty that replaced the Yuan began by driving the Mongols back to the steppe and rebuilding a Chinese state on Chinese terms. Its founder, the Hongwu emperor, moved the capital to Nanjing, restored the examination system, and tried to control trade and contact with foreigners. Yet between 1405 and 1433 the Ming court, under the Yongle emperor and his successors, sent seven great maritime expeditions into the Indian Ocean under the eunuch admiral Zheng He. The fleets were enormous, and they sailed to Champa, Java, Malacca, Calicut, Hormuz, and the East African coast, carrying silk and porcelain and bringing back envoys, giraffes, spices, and tribute. Then the voyages stopped. The reasons are debated: cost, the deaths of the emperors who sponsored them, and a turn toward a maritime ban. What matters is that the decision was made. The most powerful state in the world had the capacity to dominate the Indian Ocean and chose, for its own reasons, not to.

## The Islamic world and the Ottomans

**Baghdad, the Mamluks, and Cairo.** — The Mongol destruction of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid caliphate and killed the last caliph, a catastrophe that reshaped the Islamic world. The Mongols were stopped in Syria in 1260 at the battle of Ain Jalut by the Mamluks, a military elite of slave soldiers who had seized power in Egypt. Under the Mamluks, Cairo became the greatest city of the Islamic Mediterranean, a hub of trade between the Indian Ocean and Europe and a centre of scholarship. The Mamluks controlled the holy cities of Mecca and Medina and the Red Sea route, and their revenues from the spice trade made them the wealthiest rulers of the region.

**The rise of the Ottomans.** — Out of the wreckage of the Seljuk and Mongol order in Anatolia came a new power: the Ottomans, named for Osman, a frontier chief of the late thirteenth century. The Ottomans expanded across Anatolia and into the Balkans, winning a decisive victory over a Serbian-led army at Kosovo in 1389 and crushing a crusading army at Nicopolis in 1396. In 1402 the Central Asian conqueror Timur defeated and captured the Ottoman sultan Bayezid, and the state nearly collapsed; it recovered within a generation. In 1453 Mehmed II besieged and took Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine empire. The fall of Constantinople shocked Europe and was read as the end of an age, but it also made the Ottomans the masters of the eastern Mediterranean and of the routes between Europe and Asia.

**The wider Islamic network.** — The Islamic world was not only a set of states; it was a network held together by pilgrimage, law, and scholarship. The hajj brought Muslims from Spain, West Africa, Egypt, Persia, India, and Central Asia to Mecca every year, and pilgrims carried books, ideas, and news along with them. Sufi orders spread across the same routes, from the Maghrib to Bengal. Timbuktu, on the Niger river, became a centre of learning and of the trans-Saharan gold trade; Delhi, under its sultanate, was a great city of the Indian interior; and scholars moved between them. This was connection of a different kind from the caravan and the ship: slower, more durable, and carried by people for whom travel was itself a religious duty.

## European towns and leagues

**Venice, Genoa, and the Mediterranean.** — The Italian maritime republics were the great middlemen between the Islamic and Byzantine worlds and Latin Europe. Venice and Genoa built fleets of galleys and round ships, fought a series of wars against each other, and established colonies and trading quarters from the Aegean and the Black Sea to Alexandria and the Levant. The Fourth Crusade, diverted to Constantinople in 1204, gave Venice a commercial empire in the eastern Mediterranean. Genoese merchants founded settlements on the Black Sea, including Caffa in the Crimea, which became a major port for grain, slaves, and furs — and, in 1346, a point of entry for the plague. Through these ports passed the spices of the Indian Ocean, the silk of Persia, and the alum and cotton of Anatolia, to be sold on in Bruges, Paris, and London.

**The Hanseatic League.** — In northern Europe a different network grew up around the Baltic and North Sea. The Hanseatic League was an alliance of German merchant towns — Lübeck was its leading city — that pooled privileges, protected convoys, and maintained trading posts, called kontore, in Bruges, London, Bergen, and Novgorod. The Hansa traded Baltic timber, grain, furs, wax, and pitch for Flemish cloth, English wool, and North Sea salt and herring. It was not a state and had no army of its own, but it could blockade a port or boycott a market, and it shaped the economy of northern Europe for centuries. Its existence is a reminder that the connective institutions of the age were not only empires and religions but also leagues of merchants who wrote their own rules.

**Banking and the Medici.** — Long-distance trade needed credit, because a merchant who shipped goods from Alexandria to Bruges could not wait months or years to be paid. Italian bankers developed the bill of exchange, a written order to pay a sum in another city at a later date, which allowed merchants to settle accounts without moving coin and to shift money across borders. The great Florentine families — above all the Medici in the fifteenth century — became bankers to popes and princes. The Medici bank had branches in Rome, Venice, Geneva, Bruges, and London, and its fall in the late fifteenth century showed how quickly such a network could collapse when its debtors defaulted. Banking was a technology of connection, and it was as vulnerable as any other.

## The Black Death and the fourteenth-century crisis

**Famine and a colder climate.** — The fourteenth century began badly. A change in climate, often called the Little Ice Age, brought cooler and wetter weather to much of Europe, and harvests failed. Between 1315 and 1317 the Great Famine killed perhaps a tenth of the population of northern Europe. Population had grown for centuries, and much of the new farmland was marginal; a run of bad harvests was enough to turn plenty into catastrophe.

**The plague arrives.** — The disease was plague, caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*, which lives in rodents and is transmitted to humans by fleas. Recent genetic work on plague victims buried in what is now Kyrgyzstan has identified an ancestral strain dating to the 1330s, which supports the view that the pandemic began in Central Asia and travelled outward along trade routes — the same routes that carried silk and silver. It reached the Black Sea port of Caffa in 1346, where a Genoese colony was besieged by a Mongol army; the besiegers are said to have catapulted plague victims over the walls. From the Black Sea it moved with ships and caravans into Constantinople and the Mediterranean in 1347, and across western Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East over the next several years. The mortality was staggering. Estimates for Europe range from a third to more than half of the population, with wide local variation. The plague did not pass once. It returned in waves for generations.

**Boccaccio's Florence.** — The best-known eyewitness account comes from Giovanni Boccaccio, who was in Florence in 1348 and wrote a description of the plague as the frame of *The Decameron*. He describes the swellings called *gavoccioli* in the groin and armpits, the black spots that followed, the speed of death, and the collapse of ordinary care: the sick abandoned by kin, corpses carried out by hired bearers called *becchini*, trenches filled with bodies piled "as merchandise is stowed in the hold of a ship." His account is literary and shaped by his purposes, but it is also detailed and matches the clinical picture.

**Consequences.** — The plague transformed the societies it struck. With perhaps a third of the labour force dead, land was suddenly abundant and labour scarce. In western Europe wages rose, rents fell, and peasants who survived could demand better terms; landlords tried to hold wages down, and their attempts helped provoke the great revolts of the age — the Jacquerie in France in 1358 and the Peasants' Revolt in England in 1381. Serfdom, already weakening in the west, decayed further, though in eastern Europe landlords responded by tightening it. The Church lost prestige as prayers failed to stop the dying, and flagellant movements and the persecution of Jewish communities — accused of poisoning wells — spread along the same roads as the plague. The effects varied by region, but the fourteenth century marks a real turning point in the relationship between people and land.

**A crisis, not an end.** — It is tempting to read the fourteenth century as the end of the connected world, but that is too simple. Trade recovered, wages and consumption rose for survivors, and the Ottoman and Ming states consolidated power. The routes did not disappear; they shifted. The overland Silk Roads declined while the Indian Ocean and the Atlantic grew. What the crisis showed was the price of connection: the same network that distributed goods and ideas could distribute death, and no state could seal itself off from the consequences. That lesson would be learned again in later centuries.

- **1215** — Mongol forces take the Jin capital in northern China.
- **1219–1221** — Chinggis Khan destroys the Khwarazmian empire in Central Asia.
- **1223** — A Mongol army defeats Russian and Cuman forces at the Kalka river.
- **1227** — Chinggis Khan dies; the empire passes to his successors.
- **1241** — Mongol armies invade Poland and Hungary.
- **1253–1255** — William of Rubruck travels to the court of Möngke Khan.
- **1258** — Mongol forces sack Baghdad and end the Abbasid caliphate.
- **1260** — Kublai becomes Great Khan; the Mamluks defeat the Mongols at Ain Jalut.
- **1279** — The Yuan complete the conquest of the Song; all China is under Mongol rule.
- **1315–1317** — The Great Famine in northern Europe.
- **1346** — Plague reaches the Genoese port of Caffa on the Black Sea.
- **1347–1351** — The Black Death sweeps through the Mediterranean and western Europe.
- **1358** — The Jacquerie revolt in France.
- **1368** — The Ming dynasty overthrows the Yuan in China.
- **1381** — The Peasants' Revolt in England.
- **1389** — The Ottomans defeat a Serbian-led army at Kosovo.
- **1396** — The Ottomans crush a crusading army at Nicopolis.
- **1402** — Timur defeats the Ottoman sultan Bayezid at Ankara.
- **1405–1433** — Zheng He's seven maritime expeditions sail the Indian Ocean.
- **1453** — Mehmed II captures Constantinople; the Byzantine empire ends.

## Questions to carry

- The Mongol yam and the Indian Ocean monsoon were both systems of connection. How did they differ in how they were built and who controlled them?
- Marco Polo describes the yam in the language of wonder. What can his account tell us that an administrative record could not, and what can it not tell us?
- Möngke Khan's statement about different fingers of one hand is reported by a Franciscan who wanted to convert him. How should that shape how we read it?
- The plague travelled along the trade routes that made the age rich. Does that make connection a cause of the disaster, or only its channel?
- Why did Zheng He's voyages stop? Was the Ming turn away from the Indian Ocean inevitable?
- Abu-Lughod argues that a functioning world system existed before European dominance. McNeill argues that connection itself spread disease. Where do these two arguments meet, and where do they conflict?
