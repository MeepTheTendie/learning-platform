# The Industrial Revolution

**Period:** c. 1750–1900 CE · **Region:** Britain, Europe, and the wider world

## A break in the old pattern

**Growth before growth.** — For thousands of years output rose and fell with the harvest, the plague, and the war, but the income of an ordinary person did not climb for long. Thomas Robert Malthus set out the rule in 1798 in his *Essay on the Principle of Population*: population presses on the food supply, and any improvement in living standards is swallowed up by more births. This is the "Malthusian trap," and almost every society in almost every century lived inside it. Then, between about 1750 and 1850, first in Britain and soon across western Europe and North America, output per person began to rise and did not stop. A child born in England in 1700 could expect a life like that of a child born in 1500; one born in 1900 would inherit railways, gaslight, steel ships, and the telegraph. What made the difference is the organising question of this unit: why did sustained growth begin where and when it did?

**The name is an argument.** — "Industrial Revolution" is a later coinage, and it carries a claim. French and German writers used the phrase before Arnold Toynbee's *Lectures on the Industrial Revolution in England* (1884) fixed it in English. The word "revolution" suggests something sudden and total, but many economic historians prefer to speak of industrialisation: a slow, uneven, regional process that took two or three generations and transformed some districts while barely touching others. Both the dramatic word and the gradualist correction are partly right.

**Nothing was foreordained.** — Britain's lead was narrow and, in places, lucky. France had a larger population and a richer state; the Netherlands had more capital per head. That Britain pulled ahead does not mean it had to. Contingency matters: the world's cheapest coal lay beside its best-watered manufacturing districts; the war that ended in 1763 left Britain the dominant Atlantic power; and a generation of brilliant mechanics happened to be born in the English midlands. The outcome was not written into the stars of the English.

## Why Britain first

**Coal, lying near the water.** — Britain had coal in abundance, much of it close to the surface and to navigable water in the manufacturing districts: Northumberland and Durham, Yorkshire, Lancashire, Staffordshire's Black Country, South Wales, and the Scottish Lowlands. Coal broke the old ceiling on energy, which timber had set by how much a country could grow. By 1750 a ton of coal in London cost a fraction of firewood a century before, and the steam engine paid only where coal was cheap. The contrast with China is the hinge of a famous argument: the great manufacturing region of the Yangzi delta lay hundreds of kilometres from the main coalfields of Shaanxi and Shanxi, so the coal could not be got to the mills at a price the mills could bear. Kenneth Pomeranz made this geography the centre of *The Great Divergence* (2000), arguing that England was fortunate in the location of its coal and fortunate again in the resources of the New World.

**The agricultural surplus.** — Factories cannot be fed from the next field alone. Before industry there had to be farms productive enough to release people from the land and cheap enough to feed the towns. In England this happened through enclosure — the consolidation of open fields and common land into private, fenced farms, pushed through Parliament in thousands of individual Acts — and through new methods: the Norfolk four-course rotation of wheat, turnips, barley, and clover, popularised from the 1730s by Charles "Turnip" Townshend, and the selective breeding of sheep and cattle by Robert Bakewell at Dishley from the 1760s. Turnips and clover fed animals through the winter and restored the soil; more animals meant more manure and more meat. English wheat yields rose, and a smaller share of the population grew the food. The cost fell on the rural poor, who lost their common rights and often their land and became wage labourers — a mobile workforce for the mills. How much of the rise in farm output preceded the factories, and how much the towns caused, is still debated; that the two grew together is not.

**High wages and cheap energy.** — Robert C. Allen has offered the sharpest single answer to why Britain. In a 2009 study of the British industrial revolution in its world setting he argues that British workers earned wages several times higher than workers in France, India, or China, while British coal was the cheapest in the world. That combination — expensive labour and cheap power — made it profitable to invent and install machines that replaced people with coal. A spinning machine that cost a great deal and saved a little labour did not pay in France, where labour was cheap; it paid handsomely in Lancashire. The thesis is contested: Joel Mokyr and others reply that wages alone cannot explain why the inventions were made in Britain rather than bought from it, and point instead to a culture of experiment and the spread of technical knowledge.

**Capital and the state.** — Machines and mills need money, and money needs confidence. After the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89, Parliament controlled taxation and the king could no longer default at will; the Bank of England was founded in 1694, and the government's funded national debt gave savers a safe place to put their money. Interest rates fell, and capital became easier to borrow. The patent system, resting on the Statute of Monopolies of 1624, gave inventors a legal monopoly for a term of years — imperfectly enforced, but enough to reward the mechanic who would otherwise be copied at once. Douglass North and Barry Weingast, and later Daron Acemoglu and his collaborators, built a large argument on these institutions, crediting secure property and limited government for the breakthrough. The counter is that France and the Dutch Republic had comparable institutions and capital, and that many British machines were borrowed or stolen from abroad. Institutions were necessary; they were not sufficient.

**Empire and the world market.** — Britain's industry was built for a world market, and the world was shaped by British power. The Navigation Acts, from 1651, tied colonial trade to British ships; the Atlantic economy of sugar, tobacco, and slavery generated fortunes that were reinvested in ports, banks, and mines. Indian cotton cloth — calicoes, painted and printed — had been so popular that Parliament banned and then taxed it, and the fashion it created taught Lancashire what to make. Above all, slave-grown American cotton fed the mills: after Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made short-staple cotton profitable, the crop poured across the Atlantic to Liverpool and Manchester. Pomeranz calls the New World Britain's "ghost acreage" — land and resources beyond the island's own, which let it escape the limits closing in on China. In *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), Eric Williams argued that the profits of slavery financed industrialisation; Patrick O'Brien and others have since shown those profits were modest relative to total investment. The link was real, and its size is contested.

**Institutions and useful knowledge.** — A final strand is what Joel Mokyr calls the "Industrial Enlightenment" (*The Gifts of Athena*, 2002): the spread of practical, testable knowledge and its circulation between savants and craftsmen. The Royal Society (founded 1660) published experiments, and the Lunar Society of Birmingham joined the manufacturer Matthew Boulton, the engineer James Watt, the chemist Joseph Priestley, the potter Josiah Wedgwood, and the naturalist Erasmus Darwin. Dissenting academies taught mathematics and natural philosophy to men barred from Oxford and Cambridge by their religion. In this reading the inventions were the harvest of a society that had learned to ask how things worked and to publish the answer.

## The early machines

**Steam before steam power.** — The first commercially successful steam engine was built in 1712 by Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger of Dartmouth, to pump water from a coal mine at Dudley Castle in Staffordshire. It was an "atmospheric engine": steam filled a cylinder, cold water was injected to condense it, and the resulting vacuum let the pressure of the air drive the piston down, working a beam that lifted the pump. It was crude and wasted most of its fuel, paying its way only at a pit mouth where coal was almost free. But it did the one thing that mattered: it drained mines deeper than horses or water-wheels could reach, and so unlocked more coal. Perhaps a hundred were at work in Britain by the 1730s and more than two thousand by 1800. Steam power began as a coal-mine pump, and its first customer was the coal trade itself.

**Watt's separate condenser.** — In 1765 James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, saw that Newcomen's engine wasted heat by cooling the same cylinder it had just heated. His separate condenser, patented in 1769, kept the cylinder hot and condensed the steam in a second vessel, and the engine became far more efficient. In 1775 Watt went into partnership with Matthew Boulton, who owned the Soho Manufactory near Birmingham; Boulton's famous remark was that he sold "what all the world desires to have — POWER." Their firm added rotary motion in 1781–82, so the engine could turn a shaft and drive machinery rather than only pump; the double-acting engine pushed the piston both ways; and Watt gave the world a unit for his product, the horsepower. Their patent ran to 1800, and its expiry let loose a crowd of rival engine-builders.

**The cotton machines.** — The first modern industry was not steam but cotton, and its machines came in a rush. John Kay's flying shuttle (patented 1733) sped weaving and threw the bottleneck onto spinning. James Hargreaves's spinning jenny (about 1764) spun several threads at once from a single wheel; Richard Arkwright's water frame (patented 1769) used rollers to draw out the fibres and made strong warp thread, and his Cromford mill in Derbyshire (1771) was one of the first true factories; Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) combined the jenny and the frame. Edmund Cartwright patented a power loom in 1785. The numbers are staggering: British imports of raw cotton rose from roughly 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to some 56 million in 1800, and cotton output grew by around 5 per cent a year for decades. By the 1830s cotton goods were Britain's largest export, and Manchester was the centre of the world's cotton trade.

**Why cotton led.** — Cotton was new, with no guild to fix wages or restrict entry. Its raw material was cheap and, after the gin, grown by enslaved people in the American South; its customers were everybody, since cloth was the first purchase of a poor household with a little money. Light and easily shipped, it repaid machinery quickly.

## The factory system and the new working class

**The factory.** — A factory is not merely a large workshop. It gathers many workers under one roof, drives its machines from a single source of power, and imposes a single clock. Before it lay the "putting-out" system, in which a merchant gave fibre to families who spun and wove in their own cottages. The factory took the work out of the home and the worker out of the field, and it turned time into a quantity to be measured, bought, and fined. Bells and fines imposed the clock, and children were apprenticed in their thousands to the early water-powered mills. Andrew Ure, a Glasgow professor and an enthusiast for the new order, wrote in *The Philosophy of Manufactures* (1835) that the factory was "a vast automaton" and that scientific improvement was "philanthropic" because it relieved the workman of muscular toil. In the same book he admitted the system's purpose plainly: the aim was "to supersede human labour altogether" by substituting the labour of women and children for that of trained men.

**The new working class.** — Out of the mill came a new social type, the "operative" or "hand," whose labour was a commodity sold by the day or the week. The first generations were often children: in the 1830s children of six or seven worked twelve to fourteen hours in some mills, and the 1842 report on the mines found girls hauling coal underground. The historian E. P. Thompson argued in *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963) that this class was not a thing handed down by the machine but a relationship made by people — in chapel, in the pub, in the friendly society, in the crowd, and in political struggle. Thompson's emphasis on agency changed the subject: workers were not merely victims. His critics, among them Gareth Stedman Jones and Patrick Joyce, answer that Thompson read a later politics back into early movements, and that religion, locality, and language mattered as much as class. The standard-of-living debate asks a related question: did real wages and health improve or decline in the first industrial generations? The answer depends on whose wages, which years, and which measure.

**Resistance.** — Workers fought back. Between 1811 and 1816 the machine-breakers called Luddites destroyed stocking frames and power looms in the midlands and the north; Parliament replied with the Frame Breaking Act of 1812, which made the offence capital. On 16 August 1819 at St Peter's Field in Manchester, cavalry charged a crowd demanding parliamentary reform; the "Peterloo Massacre" killed about fifteen and wounded hundreds.

## Railways and the transport revolution

**Canals and turnpikes.** — The first transport revolution was in water and roads. In 1761 the Duke of Bridgewater opened a canal from his collieries at Worsley to Manchester, engineered by James Brindley, and the price of coal in the town fell by half. Over the next seventy years canals and improved turnpike roads knitted the country together and made heavy goods — coal, iron, pottery, grain — cheap to move inland. The "canal mania" of the 1790s was the first great transport bubble.

**The railway.** — The railway was the canal's child and its destroyer. In 1825 George Stephenson's locomotive *Locomotion No. 1* hauled the first public steam railway, the Stockton and Darlington; in 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester line opened, and Stephenson's *Rocket* showed that steam could move goods and passengers at unheard-of speed. The 1840s brought "Railway Mania," when Parliament authorised thousands of miles of line and investors bought shares in schemes that would never be built. By 1850 Britain had some six thousand miles of railway. The consequences reached far beyond transport: the price of goods fell as markets widened; fresh food and daily newspapers travelled by train; the electric telegraph ran beside the tracks; and the railways imposed a single standard time on a country that had kept local sun-time, because a timetable cannot run on a dozen clocks. The railway was the age's largest single investment, teaching the world to organise capital, contractors, and schedules on a national scale.

## Cities and their costs

**Urbanisation.** — Britain became the first urban nation in history. In 1801 about a fifth of the people of England lived in towns of more than ten thousand; by 1851 more than half did. Manchester grew from perhaps twenty thousand people in 1750 to seventy-five thousand in 1801 and over three hundred thousand by 1851. Friedrich Engels, working in his father's Manchester factory, described the city in *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) as a place where rich and poor were "sharply separated," and led the reader through courts built over the Irk where "a privy without a door" stood at the entrance and the inhabitants passed through "foul pools of stagnant urine and excrement." He called the district a "Hell upon Earth."

**The sanitary report.** — The costs of crowding were measured by Edwin Chadwick, whose *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain* (1842) showed that filth, bad drainage, and bad water killed the poor in their own streets, and that the ratepayers paid for the funerals. Cholera arrived in 1831–32 and returned in 1848–49 and 1853–54; in the worst Manchester districts contemporaries calculated average life expectancy at around twenty-six years. The response was slow: local boards of health, and eventually sewers and clean water. The sanitary movement shows evidence — the survey, the statistic, the map — as a weapon of reform.

## The second industrial revolution

**Steel.** — After 1850 the pace changed again, and historians call the next phase the "second industrial revolution." Its base was steel. In 1856 Henry Bessemer patented a converter that blew air through molten iron to burn out its carbon, making steel in minutes rather than days. The Siemens–Martin open-hearth furnace (1864) allowed larger and more controlled heats, and the Gilchrist–Thomas basic process (1878) let steel be made from phosphoric ores that had been useless before. Steel rails replaced iron, ships were built of steel, and the price fell sharply. World steel production rose from a few hundred thousand tons a year in 1860 to tens of millions by 1900.

**Chemicals and electricity.** — The same decades produced chemical and electrical industries. Ernest Solvay's ammonia-soda process (1861) made soda cheap for glass, soap, and textiles; William Perkin's accidental mauveine (1856) began the synthetic dye industry, which German firms such as BASF (1865) and Bayer (1863) turned into a science-based business with their own laboratories. Michael Faraday's experiments (1831) had shown how to make electricity from motion; the dynamo turned that into power, and in 1879–80 Joseph Swan and Thomas Edison produced practical incandescent lamps. Edison's Pearl Street station in New York began supplying electric light in 1882, and the internal-combustion engine (Nikolaus Otto, 1876; Gottlieb Daimler and Karl Benz, 1885–86) put the new fuels into motion.

**New leaders.** — The second revolution had new winners. Germany and the United States overtook Britain in steel and chemicals in the 1890s, helped by large home markets, tariff protection, technical universities, and the industrial research laboratory. Alfred Chandler's *The Visible Hand* (1977) described the rise of managerial capitalism: the big corporation with salaried managers, integrated production and distribution, and the capital to build on a scale no family firm could match. Britain's early start now became a handicap — old plant, old industries, and a financial system more interested in foreign bonds than domestic factories.

## The social question

**The Factory Acts.** — Parliament regulated the factories slowly and against resistance. The Health and Morals of Apprentices Act of 1802 limited the hours of pauper apprentices; the Cotton Mills Act of 1819 barred children under nine and capped the day at twelve hours for those under sixteen; the Factory Act of 1833 was the first with real teeth, appointing paid inspectors, limiting children of nine to thirteen to eight hours and young persons of thirteen to eighteen to twelve, and requiring some schooling. The Mines Act of 1842 kept women and girls out of underground work and boys under ten; the Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited the day for women and young persons to ten hours; later Acts in 1867 and 1878 extended the rules to workshops and other trades. These laws were not gifts: they followed the reports of commissioners and inspectors and the pressure of organised workers, evangelicals, and Tory paternalists. The factory inspector's report is a new kind of source: the state gathering facts about its own people and using them to legislate.

**Trade unions and socialism.** — Workers organised. The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 made unions illegal; they were repealed in 1824, and the unions returned at once. In 1834 six farm labourers of Tolpuddle in Dorset were transported to Australia for administering an oath, and became the "Tolpuddle Martyrs"; in the same year Robert Owen founded the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union, which collapsed within months. Chartism, the first mass working-class political movement, demanded the six points of the People's Charter in 1838 and carried great petitions to Parliament in 1839, 1842, and 1848, all rejected. After mid-century the "New Model" unions of skilled engineers and carpenters joined in the Trades Union Congress in 1868. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, writing in the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) and in Marx's *Capital* (volume one, 1867), argued that the factory system created the class that would one day overthrow it; the First International was founded in 1864. Marx's prediction was an argument about history, not a description of it, and Europe's working classes did not follow the script he wrote for them.

**Reformers and critics.** — Not everyone who condemned the age wanted to overturn it. Samuel Smiles, in *Self-Help* (1859), taught that character and perseverance, not class struggle, raised a man; his books sold in the hundreds of thousands and offered a liberal, individualist answer to the "condition of England question" that Thomas Carlyle had named in *Chartism* (1839). Against Smiles stood Owen's co-operation, the Christian socialists, and the revolutionary socialists.

## The world transformed

**The great divergence.** — Before 1750 the most advanced regions of China, India, and Europe were not so far apart in output per head. After 1800 the gap widened into what Pomeranz called the "great divergence." Indian weavers, whose cloth had clothed the world, were ruined by machine-made British cotton; by the late nineteenth century India, once the greatest exporter of cotton goods, had become a market for Lancashire. The Opium Wars (1839–42 and 1856–60) forced China open to foreign trade and ceded Hong Kong. The Industrial Revolution was not only machines; it reordered the world economy around the first industrialisers.

**The spread of industry.** — Industry spread, but unevenly. Belgium, led by the English-born John Cockerill at Seraing from 1817, was the first continental follower; France industrialised more slowly, with a large peasantry and small firms; the German states built the Zollverein customs union in 1834 and, after unification in 1871, the great steel and chemical firms of Krupp, Siemens, and BASF. The United States began with Samuel Slater's mill at Pawtucket in 1790 and the Lowell mills of the 1820s. Latecomers rarely left it to the market: they used tariffs, banks, and schools, following Alexander Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* (1791) and Friedrich List's *National System of Political Economy* (1841). Japan's Meiji Restoration of 1868 sent the Iwakura Mission to study the West and built state factories to catch up. The state, not only the entrepreneur, made industrial nations.

**Was it a revolution?** — Historians still divide. Toynbee's "revolution" and the gradualist reply are both alive; so are the arguments over coal and geography (Pomeranz), wages and prices (Allen), knowledge and culture (Mokyr), and institutions (North). No single cause commands agreement, and the honest answer is that several causes reinforced one another in one small island at one particular moment — and that the moment might not have come at all.

- **1733** — John Kay patents the flying shuttle.
- **1761** — The Duke of Bridgewater's canal opens, halving the price of coal in Manchester.
- **1764–1779** — Hargreaves's spinning jenny, Arkwright's water frame, Crompton's spinning mule.
- **1769** — James Watt patents the separate condenser; Richard Arkwright patents the water frame.
- **1775** — Boulton and Watt begin their partnership at Soho.
- **1785** — Edmund Cartwright patents the power loom.
- **1798** — Malthus publishes the *Essay on the Principle of Population*.
- **1811–1816** — Luddite machine-breaking in the midlands and the north.
- **1819** — The Peterloo Massacre at St Peter's Field, Manchester.
- **1825** — The Stockton and Darlington railway opens, the first public steam railway.
- **1830** — The Liverpool and Manchester railway opens.
- **1833** — The Factory Act appoints paid inspectors and limits child labour.
- **1834** — The Tolpuddle Martyrs are transported; Owen founds the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union.
- **1838–1848** — Chartism and the three great petitions for the People's Charter.
- **1842** — Chadwick's Sanitary Report; the Mines Act bars women and girls from underground work.
- **1845** — Engels publishes *The Condition of the Working Class in England*.
- **1847** — The Ten Hours Act.
- **1848** — Marx and Engels publish the *Communist Manifesto*.
- **1856** — Bessemer patents his steel converter.
- **1861** — Solvay's ammonia-soda process.
- **1867** — Marx publishes *Capital*, volume one.
- **1868** — The Trades Union Congress is founded; the Meiji Restoration begins in Japan.
- **1882** — Edison's Pearl Street station begins supplying electric light in New York.
- **c. 1890** — German and American steel output overtakes Britain's.
