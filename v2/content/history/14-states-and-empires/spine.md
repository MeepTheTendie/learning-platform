# States, parliaments, and imperial rivalry

**Period:** c. 1600–1763 CE · **Region:** Europe and the Atlantic empires

## War and the making of the fiscal state

**The cost of war.** — Between 1600 and 1763 the states of Europe fought almost without pause, and how they paid for the fighting changed how they were governed. A great power around 1600 might put 30,000 men into a campaign; by 1700 France kept 300,000 or more under arms in wartime, and by 1760 Europe's combined armies numbered perhaps a million. Cannon made the tall medieval wall useless, so engineers rebuilt fortresses in the low, thick, angled style the Italians called the *trace italienne*. The race went to whoever could move money fastest.

**The military revolution debate.** — Historians call this cluster of changes the "military revolution." Michael Roberts proposed the phrase in 1955, arguing that the drill and tactics of the Dutch and Swedish armies around 1600 forced states to keep permanent, paid forces and to build the administration that could pay them. Geoffrey Parker widened the thesis in *The Military Revolution* (1988) to include fortification, artillery, and sea power. David Parrott attacked it in *The Business of War* (2012), arguing that much of the fighting was contracted out to private colonels who raised and paid their own regiments and profited from plunder and office. The dispute decides what we mean by a state: an organisation that monopolises violence, or one that bargains with the men who own the soldiers.

**Taxes, credit, and the fiscal state.** — What is not in dispute is the money. The label historians use is the "fiscal-military state," which John Brewer applied to Britain after 1688: a state whose institutions — treasury, excise board, central bank — were built to raise and service debt for war. The English excise was the key. An excise is a tax on goods produced and sold inside the country, levied at the point of manufacture or sale rather than on land; the English excise fell on beer, malt, salt, candles, and soap. Unlike a land tax, assessed on landholders and politically painful to raise, an excise could be collected by salaried officials at a few breweries and warehouses. It was steady, hard to evade, and could be pledged to lenders. A government that can promise a creditor a stream of excise revenue borrows at three or four per cent; one that can promise only a king's goodwill borrows at ten per cent or not at all.

**The question.** — How did taxation, credit, military organisation, and political bargaining change state power? The answer lies in institutions and resources rather than rulers' personalities: who could tax, who had to consent, who would lend, and who could be compelled to fight. The same pressures produced different settlements in France, England, the Dutch Republic, Spain, Russia, and Prussia, and the differences decided which states could sustain war and empire.

## Absolutism in France

**What absolutism meant.** — Absolutism is the name historians give to a theory and a style of rule in which the monarch claims to make law and command the realm without sharing sovereignty with a representative assembly, answerable, in the theory, to God alone. The bishop Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet preached that the king is the image of God on earth and that resistance to him is a sin. Practice was never absolute in the literal sense. A king ruled through law courts, provincial estates, guilds, and inherited privileges, and every new tax was a negotiation. Louis XIV's claim was unlimited, but his reach stopped where privilege, distance, and money ran out.

**Versailles and the nobility.** — Louis XIV took personal rule in 1661, after the death of Cardinal Mazarin, and governed without a first minister for the rest of his life. He built Versailles, enlarging his father's hunting lodge into a palace of some 2,300 rooms, with gardens by André Le Nôtre. Versailles was a machine for managing the nobility: pensions, offices, and ceremonial rank were distributed at court, drawing the great nobles there and away from their provincial power bases. William Beik argued in *Absolutism and Society in Seventeenth-Century France* (1985) that Louis ruled not against the elites but through them, buying cooperation with offices, pensions, and tax exemptions.

**Intendants and the machinery of rule.** — The king's most important agents in the provinces were the intendants. An intendant was a royal official — *intendant de justice, police et finances* — sent from the centre into a province to supervise justice, taxation, and policing. Unlike old office-holders, who had bought their posts and owned them as inheritable property, an intendant held a commission revocable at the king's pleasure. There were about thirty by the later seventeenth century. They supervised the *taille*, the main direct tax, which fell on commoners and not on nobles or clergy. The intendant is the concrete face of absolutist administration: an agent of the centre who could reach into a province without owning it. Louis's finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert (d. 1683) regularised the taxes and made the farmers of the revenue, the *fermiers généraux*, a powerful and hated class, but France's tax base of some twenty million people was limited by privilege and the cost of collection.

**The limits of absolutism.** — In October 1685 Louis revoked the Edict of Nantes, ending the toleration of Protestant worship granted in 1598 and issuing the Edict of Fontainebleau. The revocation forced the emigration of perhaps 200,000 Huguenots, many of them craftsmen and merchants who carried their skills to the Dutch Republic, Prussia, and England. War was the greater limit: Louis fought four great wars between 1667 and 1714, and by 1694 famine and a collapsing currency had brought the kingdom close to bankruptcy. When Louis died in 1715 the treasury was exhausted and the crown's credit in ruins. Absolutism had made him not all-powerful but the largest single debtor in Europe.

## The English road: crown against Parliament

**Divine right and the purse.** — In England the conflict ran the other way, and it ran through money. James I, who came to the throne in 1603, and his son Charles I, who succeeded in 1625, held a high view of royal authority, but an English king could not levy a new tax without Parliament, and Parliament would not grant one unless satisfied about grievances. Charles raised "ship money," an old naval levy, as a general tax without a parliamentary grant, and he governed without Parliament from 1629 to 1640, enforcing religious uniformity through Archbishop Laud. When Laud tried to impose a new prayer book on Scotland in 1637, the Scots revolted, and Charles had to call Parliament to pay for an army. That Parliament, the Long Parliament of 1640, dismantled much of the king's prerogative, and the quarrel slid into war.

**Civil war and the execution of the king.** — The civil war began in 1642. Parliament's forces were reorganised into the New Model Army (1645), a paid, disciplined, religiously radical standing force — the very thing the crown had been accused of wanting. At Naseby in 1645 the royalists were broken. Charles was captured, escaped, and allied with the Scots, who invaded in 1648. The army purged Parliament and put the king on trial. On 30 January 1649 Charles I was executed before his own Banqueting House: a sitting king publicly killed by his own subjects, without precedent in Europe. The monarchy and the House of Lords were abolished, and from 1653 to 1658 Oliver Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector. In 1660 Parliament restored Charles II.

**The Restoration and the Glorious Revolution.** — Charles II reigned from 1660 to 1685, helped because the excise, introduced during the civil war, gave the crown an independent revenue. His brother James II, a Catholic, succeeded in 1685 and quickly lost the political nation: he used the royal dispensing power to appoint Catholics to office, kept a standing army, and prosecuted bishops who refused to read his declaration of indulgence. In 1688 peers invited William of Orange, husband of James's Protestant daughter Mary, to intervene. William landed, James fled to France, and a Convention Parliament declared he had abdicated and offered the throne to William and Mary, with a Declaration of Right enacted as the Bill of Rights in December 1689. The Bill made it illegal for the crown to suspend or dispense with laws without Parliament, to levy money without a parliamentary grant, and to keep a standing army in peacetime without consent, and it guaranteed free elections, frequent Parliaments, and freedom of speech in Parliament. It was not democratic: it spoke of the "ancient rights and liberties" of the subject, left the franchise to the propertied, and perhaps one adult man in five could vote.

**The financial revolution.** — With the crown under Parliament's purse, England could do what France could not: borrow cheaply on a large scale. After 1688 Parliament met every year and voted supply. In 1694, to fund the war against France, the government chartered the Bank of England, which lent the crown £1.2 million in return for the right to issue notes and manage the public debt. A funded national debt followed, with government stock traded in London. P. G. M. Dickson called this the "financial revolution" in 1967. By 1710 the state borrowed at around six per cent; by the 1740s, three per cent consols were a safe asset. Parliamentary control of taxation, in the hands of men who lent to the state and sat in the Commons, produced credibility, and credibility produced cheap credit.

**Whose Parliament?** — It is easy to read the English story as a march toward liberty. That reading must reckon with facts: the same Parliament that limited the crown passed the game laws and the Navigation Acts, governed Ireland as a dependency, and protected property in land and in enslaved people. The settlement of 1689 was a bargain among the propertied. It widened the political nation compared with France; it did not include the poor, and it did not end the slave trade. The English road produced a powerful state, not a democracy.

## The Dutch Republic

**A republic of merchants.** — The Dutch Republic was the great anomaly of the age: a wealthy, urban, largely Protestant confederation with no king. The seven northern provinces of the Low Countries revolted against Spain, formed the Union of Utrecht in 1579, and abjured Philip II in 1581. Each province kept its own assembly, and the States General at The Hague handled war, foreign policy, and common finances. The stadholder, a prince of Orange, commanded the army and navy in most provinces, but real power lay with the regents, the patrician families who dominated the town councils of Holland and Amsterdam. Amsterdam was the entrepôt of Europe and its bourse; the Wisselbank, founded in 1609, made the city a clearing house for bills of exchange.

**Companies and empire.** — In 1602 the States General chartered the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, the VOC, granting it a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and the power to make war, conclude treaties, and found colonies; the West India Company followed in 1621. The VOC was the first great joint-stock company to combine permanent capital, tradable shares, and sovereign powers. It founded Batavia in 1619, and for most of the seventeenth century the Dutch carried the largest share of Europe's Asian trade. In the Atlantic the WIC seized part of Brazil and ran slave-trading posts on the African coast. The republic's wealth rested on shipping, banking, religious toleration, and the labour of sailors and enslaved people.

**Decline.** — The golden age did not last. The republic was small, about two million people against France's twenty, and its federal structure made it slow to raise taxes and coordinate defence. Three Anglo-Dutch wars (1652–54, 1665–67, 1672–74) and the French invasion of 1672 broke its military reputation; the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 confirmed its decline as a great power, though Amsterdam remained a financial centre. Simon Schama's *The Embarrassment of Riches* (1987) reads Dutch culture as an anxious balancing of wealth and virtue.

## Spain and the limits of silver

**The silver monarchy.** — Spain had been the greatest power of the sixteenth century. Silver from Potosí and Zacatecas poured through Seville, and the crown ruled an empire stretching from Milan and the Netherlands to Mexico and Peru. But Castile, the kingdom that paid, was poorer than its empire suggested. The expulsion of the moriscos in 1609 removed some 300,000 people from Valencia and Aragon, many of them farmers and artisans, and the silver that arrived was pledged in advance to foreign bankers. Spain's problem was not a lack of treasure but a fiscal system that could not keep enough of it.

**Olivares and the revolts of 1640.** — The count-duke of Olivares, minister to Philip IV, tried to make each kingdom pay its share through a Union of Arms. The plan failed. Catalonia and Portugal revolted in 1640, and Portugal never returned to the Spanish crown. The Peace of the Pyrenees in 1659 ceded Artois and Roussillon to France and marked the end of Spanish primacy in Europe. J. H. Elliott's *Richelieu and Olivares* (1984) shows a reformer defeated by the constitutional privileges of the kingdoms he was trying to unite: the crown could not tax what it could not reach.

**The last Habsburg.** — Charles II of Spain, who reigned from 1665 to 1700, was childless, and his death left the whole Spanish inheritance as the prize of Europe. His will named Philip of Anjou, Louis XIV's grandson, as heir, and the result was the War of the Spanish Succession. Spain kept its empire and its American silver, but it was now a junior partner of France, and the asiento — the contract to supply enslaved Africans to the Spanish colonies — passed to Britain at Utrecht. The silver that had once bought Spanish power was buying the power of others.

## Russia and Peter the Great

**A different starting point.** — Russia in 1600 was a vast, thinly populated realm with a small bureaucracy, a serf population bound to the land, and no outlet to the Baltic or the Black Sea. The Romanov dynasty, founded in 1613, ruled through a court and a service nobility, and the code of 1649, the *Ulozhenie*, had fixed peasants to their lords. Peter I, who ruled from 1682 to 1725, set out to change all of it by force and imitation.

**Peter's war and reforms.** — Peter's great war was the Great Northern War against Sweden, from 1700 to 1721. He was crushed at Narva in 1700, rebuilt his army on European lines, and won the decisive battle at Poltava in 1709. The Treaty of Nystad in 1721 gave Russia the Baltic provinces and a window on the sea. In 1703 he founded St Petersburg on the Neva, built on marshland at enormous cost in lives, and moved the capital there. He created a navy and a regular army filled by conscription and replaced household taxation with a poll tax on every male soul, which raised more revenue and bound peasants more tightly to their lords and to the state. The Table of Ranks, issued in 1722, graded military, civil, and court service into fourteen ranks and allowed non-nobles to reach noble status through service.

**The cost.** — Peter's reforms were paid for by the peasantry and deepened serfdom; the state grew stronger, most of its subjects did not. Russia became a great power without taxing its elites on equal terms.

## Prussia and the army

**An army with a state.** — Brandenburg-Prussia was a poor, scattered collection of territories with no natural frontiers. The Great Elector, Frederick William (r. 1640–88), built a standing army, won full sovereignty over East Prussia, and used the army to make his scattered lands a single state. His grandson Frederick William I (r. 1713–40) made the army the centre of everything. He raised it to some 80,000 men, introduced the canton system, which drew each regiment's recruits from a district, and created the General Directory in 1723 to centralise administration. The officer corps was drawn from the Junker nobility, who held a near-monopoly of commissions in exchange for service and for paying taxes on their estates.

**Frederick the Great.** — Frederick II, who succeeded in 1740, invaded Silesia at once and kept it through two wars, making Prussia a great power and beginning the long rivalry with Austria. A small, poor state could buy military weight with discipline and organisation — at the price of tying its nobility and peasantry into a single military order.

## The Atlantic empires and the slave trade

**Empires of settlement and extraction.** — The Atlantic world was built by Spain and Portugal first, then by the Dutch, English, and French. Two kinds of colony took shape: settler colonies with their own assemblies, such as New England and Virginia, and extractive colonies ruled more directly from the metropole, such as New Spain and Brazil. By 1700 the Atlantic was a single economy, bound by sugar, tobacco, rice, and, above all, enslaved people. The Navigation Acts, beginning in 1651, reserved colonial trade to English ships, and the wars of the period were fought as much over these routes as over European territory.

**The trade in human beings.** — The transatlantic slave trade carried roughly twelve and a half million Africans across the ocean between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, of whom about ten and a half million survived the crossing; mortality on the "middle passage" averaged about fifteen per cent. Britain became the largest carrier in the eighteenth century. The Spanish colonies received enslaved people through the asiento, a contract the crown sold to foreign merchants; Portugal and Brazil ran their own routes. In the Caribbean, sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans produced the most valuable commodity in European trade: Saint-Domingue, Jamaica, and Barbados were among the richest places on earth, and their wealth was built on the labour of people who had no rights at all.

**Colonies and the coming war.** — The British North American colonies held about 1.5 million people by 1760; New France held about 70,000, and the disparity told in war. But the colonies were not simply tools of London. They had their own assemblies, militias, and ideas about taxation and consent, and the arguments Englishmen had used against Charles I and James II would, after 1763, be turned against the Parliament that had just won the war.

## The great wars and the balance of power

**The War of the Spanish Succession.** — Charles II of Spain died in 1700, and Louis XIV accepted the Spanish crown for his grandson. William III of England and the Dutch built a Grand Alliance to stop France from uniting the two crowns. The war was fought in the Low Countries, Italy, Spain, and at sea, and the peace of Utrecht in 1713, followed by Rastatt in 1714, settled the succession: Philip V kept Spain and its empire but renounced any claim to the French throne; the Spanish Netherlands and Spain's Italian possessions passed to Austria; and Britain took Gibraltar and Minorca, Newfoundland, Hudson Bay, and Acadia, and won the asiento. The settlement is usually described as a "balance of power": no single crown was to dominate Europe, and the guarantors were the maritime powers. The balance was not natural; it was a bargain imposed by war.

**The mid-century wars.** — The War of the Austrian Succession began in 1740 when Frederick II seized Silesia and ended at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. In 1756 the alliances reversed: Austria and France, enemies for two centuries, allied against Prussia and Britain in the "Diplomatic Revolution." The Seven Years' War, from 1756 to 1763, was the first truly worldwide war. In Europe, Frederick fought Austria, Russia, and France to a standstill. In North America, Britain took Quebec in 1759 and Montreal. In India, Robert Clive's victory at Plassey in 1757 opened Bengal to the East India Company. In the Caribbean, Britain captured Havana in 1762. The Treaty of Paris in 1763 gave Britain Canada, Florida, and the eastern half of the Mississippi valley; France kept Guadeloupe and Martinique and its fishing rights but surrendered its Indian factories except for trading posts. Britain was now the greatest power in the world.

**The bill.** — The war had cost Britain enormously: the national debt rose from about £16 million in 1700 to roughly £133 million in 1763. In 1764 and 1765 Parliament began to tax the American colonies to help pay for it, and the argument that had justified Parliament against the Stuarts — no taxation without consent — was turned against the Parliament that had won the war.

## What changed, and what did not

**Institutions and resources, not personalities.** — The organizing question was how taxation, credit, military organisation, and political bargaining changed state power. Louis XIV's personality mattered less than France's tax base and the limits privilege placed on it; Charles I's stubbornness mattered less than the fact that an English king could not tax without Parliament. The states that grew strongest could tax broadly, borrow cheaply, and bargain effectively with the people who paid and fought. Absolutism and constitutionalism were two answers to the same problem, and neither was "modern" in the present sense: France's absolutism preserved privilege and limited its own revenue, while England's Parliament represented the propertied and protected slavery. Russia and Prussia built powerful armies on serfdom and noble service; Spain had silver but not a fiscal system; the Dutch had credit but not enough people or unity.

**Contingency.** — None of this was foreordained. The Dutch could have held their commercial lead; Spain could have reformed its Castilian taxes; the Stuarts could have won the civil war; France could have kept Canada. Historians disagree about how much was structural and how much was decided by particular battles, deaths, and decisions. The institutions built to fight these wars proved more durable than the wars themselves: the fiscal-military state, the funded debt, the professional army, and the chartered company outlasted every king who used them.

## Questions to carry

- Why did war become so much more expensive between 1600 and 1763, and which states found a way to pay for it?
- Louis XIV is called an absolute monarch, yet his revenue was limited by privilege. What does "absolute" actually describe, and what did it not?
- The English Bill of Rights is often read as a step toward liberty. What did it leave out, and who was excluded from the settlement of 1689?
- How did the Dutch Republic become so rich, and why did that wealth not translate into lasting military power?
- Peter the Great and Frederick William I built great armies. Who paid for them, and at what social cost?
- Britain won the Seven Years' War and then lost its American colonies within twenty years. How much of that was decided by the debt the war created?
