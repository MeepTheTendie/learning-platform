# Land, churches, and medieval authority

**Period:** c. 900–1300 CE · **Region:** Western Europe and the eastern Mediterranean

## The land and the people who worked it

**The manor.** — Most people lived on the land, and the unit that organised their work was the manor: an estate held by a lord, made up of his own fields, called the demesne, and the tenancies of the peasants who farmed the rest. The village lay inside it, ringed by open fields divided into long strips scattered across two or three great fields. In a three-field village one field carried winter grain, a second spring grain, and the third lay fallow. Every villager's strips were mixed among every other's, so ploughing, sowing, and harvest were done together. Beyond the fields lay the common pasture, meadow, and wood, where villagers grazed pigs and gathered wood and turf — rights without which a family could not keep a cow, a pig, or a fire.

**The plough and the team.** — The mouldboard plough cut a furrow, turned the soil, and buried weeds; it was heavy and took a team of eight oxen. Few peasants owned eight, so the team was shared and the strips ploughed cooperatively, binding villagers together as tightly as any lord bound them. By the twelfth century horses, shod and collared, were used more. The plough, the mill, the oven, and often the winepress and brewery belonged to the lord, who charged for their use — the banalities, among the most resented of his rights.

**Serfs and free tenants.** — A serf, or villein, was personally unfree: bound to the manor, unable to leave, marry outside it, or be ordained without the lord's leave. He owed labour on the demesne, often two or three days a week; he paid merchet to marry a daughter, heriot, the best beast, at death, and tallage, a tax the lord could raise at will; his land passed to his heir only on an entry fine. A free tenant owed rent, in money or kind, and perhaps a few days' work, but could leave, sell his land, and sue in the king's courts. The line was real in law and blurred in practice: a free man might hold servile land, a serf hold by money rent.

**The village and its year.** — The village was a few dozen households living by the agricultural year: autumn ploughing, sowing, the late-spring hungry gap, haymaking, the August harvest, when the lord called his boon works, and the November slaughter of beasts there was no fodder to keep. People ate rye or maslin bread, pottage, cheese, eggs, bacon, and ale brewed at home. Meat was a festival food. Women brewed, baked, milked, spun, and worked the harvest, and a widow could hold land and answer for it in the manor court. That court, the hallmote, met every few weeks to register land transfers, fine petty offences, and enforce services; the villagers ran it through the reeve and jurors under the lord's steward.

## Lordship, vassals, and fiefs

**Fief and vassal.** — A fief was land, or a right, office, or rent, held of a lord in return for service — most often military. A vassal was a man bound in fidelity to a lord. The bond was made in a ceremony: the vassal placed his joined hands between the lord's and became his man, which was homage, then swore on relics to keep faith, which was fealty. The lord granted the fief and promised protection. This was dependence, not a contract between equals, and its language was that of the household. The fief was the vassal's to hold and pass to his heir, but remained the lord's land; breach of faith forfeited it, and a failed line meant escheat.

**The incidents of lordship.** — Because the fief was the lord's, he had claims on it at every turn. An heir taking up land paid a relief, an inheritance tax. A minor heir's land fell into wardship, its income kept by the lord, who also controlled the heir's marriage. The lord could demand aids to ransom him, knight his eldest son, or marry his eldest daughter. He held a honorial court where his tenants did suit. A great lord was himself the king's vassal, so the same man could be lord to one and vassal to another, and a man might hold land of several lords at once.

**The military fee.** — A knight's fee was the land thought sufficient to support a knight and his equipment. A lord owing the king forty knights might keep some land and grant the rest out in fees, each owing one knight. From the later twelfth century kings accepted money instead of personal service — scutage, shield money — and hired soldiers, which suited a king fighting abroad. The records of service and payment, not romances, are the main evidence for vassalage.

**Was there "feudalism"?** — This picture was taught for generations as feudalism, and is now disputed. The word was coined by later lawyers and historians, not by the people it describes; no medieval word names the system. Marc Bloch, in *Feudal Society* (1939), treated feudalism as a whole type of society — fragmented authority, ties of dependence, the fief; F. L. Ganshof, in *Feudalism* (1944), gave a narrower legal definition. Elizabeth A. R. Brown, in "The Tyranny of a Construct" (1974), argued that feudalism is a construct imposed on a varied past and that using it as an explanation smuggles in conclusions. Susan Reynolds, in *Fiefs and Vassals* (1994), argued the tidy model of fief and vassal was largely the creation of twelfth-century lawyers and later scholars, while the evidence shows property and lordship arranged in many ways, with hereditary right common and personal dependence less central than the model assumes. Others reply that some word is needed for the tangled reality of lordship. The honest position: "feudalism" names practices that existed in wildly different combinations, and a system that may never have existed. It was not one uniform machine.

## The church and its authority

**Reform and Cluny.** — The church was the largest institution in western Europe and the one most in need of reform. In the tenth century kings and lords appointed bishops and abbots, offices were bought and sold — the sin of simony, named for Simon Magus — and many clergy lived with wives or concubines. The monastery of Cluny, founded in 910 in Burgundy, was the engine of reform: exempt from its local bishop and lord and placed directly under the pope, strict in prayer and work, and mother to hundreds of daughter houses. The Cistercians, founded at Cîteaux in 1098 and made famous by Bernard of Clairvaux, reacted against Cluniac wealth with a simpler life. The Peace and Truce of God tried to protect churches, peasants, and merchants and limit fighting to certain days.

**Canon law.** — The reform papacy built a government, and its instrument was law. Around 1140 Gratian compiled the *Decretum*, a systematic collection of some four thousand texts arranged to resolve contradictions. It became the foundation of canon law — the church's own body of law, taught across Europe and enlarged by later collections. The church claimed courts over marriage, wills, oaths, and tithes; built appeals to Rome; sent legates; and gathered the cardinals into a body that elected the pope. By the thirteenth century the papacy was a monarchy in all but name. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 required annual confession and communion, defined transubstantiation, and forbade clergy to take part in trials by ordeal, helping push secular courts toward the jury.

**The investiture conflict.** — Reform meant a struggle with kings, who appointed bishops and abbots and invested them with ring and staff. In 1075 Pope Gregory VII set down in the papal register twenty-seven propositions, the Dictatus Papae: that the Roman church was founded by God alone; that the pope alone could depose and reinstate bishops; that he could depose emperors; that his decree could be annulled by no one and he judged by no one; that he could absolve subjects from their oath of fidelity to wicked rulers. This claimed authority over all Christian society, spiritual and temporal. The emperor Henry IV had Gregory deposed by a council of German bishops; Gregory excommunicated Henry and released his subjects from their oaths, and the German lords turned against the king.

**Canossa.** — In the winter of 1077 Henry crossed the Alps to find the pope, who had taken refuge at Canossa in the lands of Countess Matilda. Gregory's own letter tells what happened: the king came to the gate barefoot in wretched woollen garments and stood there three days, beseeching absolution with tears, until the pope removed the excommunication. It looked like a papal triumph, and is remembered as one; it was also a success for Henry, who kept the pope from travelling to Germany to judge him with his rebellious nobles. The conflict was patched up only in 1122 at Worms: the emperor gave up investiture with ring and staff but kept a voice in electing bishops and received their lands from them. The church won the symbols; the king kept the substance.

**The church in ordinary life.** — The church framed every life: baptism, marriage, confession, burial, and its weapons of excommunication and interdict. The parish church, its bell and graveyard, centred village life, and the tithe, a tenth of produce, was owed to it. The church owned perhaps a third of the land of western Europe; bishops and abbots were great lords who held fiefs and owed knights, and monasteries were among the most efficient estates in the land. The same field paid the lord's rent, the priest's tithe, and the peasant's bread.

## Kingship, law, and the realm

**The Capetians.** — In 987 the great men of the west Frankish kingdom elected Hugh Capet king. His family held only the Île-de-France around Paris and Orléans and for two centuries was weaker than many of its own vassals — the dukes of Normandy, Aquitaine, and Burgundy, the counts of Flanders, Champagne, and Anjou. The king was first among equals, but he alone was anointed at Reims, and kingship was sacred. Step by step the Capetians turned the title into power. Louis VI (r. 1108–1137) subdued the robber barons of the royal lands with his adviser Suger of Saint-Denis. Philip II Augustus (r. 1180–1223) conquered Normandy from King John in 1204, won at Bouvines in 1214, and appointed royal bailiffs and prévôts to administer justice and collect revenue. Under Louis IX (r. 1226–1270) the Parlement of Paris became a permanent court of appeal.

**The Plantagenets and the common law.** — The Capetians' great rival was the house of Anjou, the Plantagenets, who ruled England, Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine, and Aquitaine. Henry II (r. 1154–1189) governed this sprawling collection from England with tireless attention to law. He sent royal justices on circuit, so the king's justice travelled to the litigant. He offered standard writs and procedures: the petty assizes, by which a free man could recover land or claim his inheritance; the grand assize, which let a defendant choose trial by sworn neighbours instead of battle; and the assize of Clarendon (1166), which required local juries to report suspected criminals — the ancestor of the grand jury. Out of this grew the common law, common to the realm against local custom. Henry's attempt to bring clergy under royal courts provoked Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury, murdered by four knights in his cathedral in 1170; Henry had to do penance and yield.

**Magna Carta.** — Henry's son John (r. 1199–1216) lost Normandy to Philip Augustus and quarrelled with Pope Innocent III over the archbishopric of Canterbury. The pope laid England under interdict and excommunicated John; in 1213 John submitted, surrendered England and Ireland to the pope, and received them back as a papal fief. His barons, taxed and angered by arbitrary justice, rebelled and forced him to seal a charter at Runnymede on 15 June 1215. Magna Carta's sixty-three clauses are a settlement among the elite, not rights for all. Clause 1 frees the English Church; clause 12 says no scutage or aid without the common counsel of the realm, ancestor of consent to taxation; clause 39 promises no free man shall be imprisoned or dispossessed except by lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land; clause 40 promises justice will not be sold, refused, or delayed; clause 61 lets a committee of twenty-five barons seize the king's castles if he breaks the charter. Villeins, the majority, are not "free men." The charter was annulled within weeks and reissued after John's death; its later fame as a foundation of English liberty is a story about the seventeenth century as much as the thirteenth.

**The empire and the eastern Mediterranean.** — Beyond the western kingdoms lay the Holy Roman Empire, a loose union of German and Italian lands whose emperor claimed to continue Charlemagne's. Frederick Barbarossa (r. 1152–1190) fought the Italian communes and the popes; his grandson Frederick II (r. 1215–1250) ruled Sicily with a centralised administration. In the east the Byzantine Empire remained the richest state in the Mediterranean until the Fourth Crusade shattered it in 1204. The Iberian kingdoms advanced south against Muslim rule. Kingship grew everywhere, through law, taxation, and writing, and unevenly.

## Towns, guilds, and trade

**The revival of towns.** — From the tenth century the population climbed, land was cleared and farmed more intensively, and the surplus peasants sold fed people who did not farm at all. Old Roman towns revived and new ones grew at bridges, fords, castles, and monasteries. By 1300 Europe may have held seventy or eighty million people, its largest cities tens of thousands each. Towns were places of liberty in a narrow sense: a serf who lived in one for a year and a day might be treated as free. Townsmen formed communes, sworn associations that bought or fought charters giving them self-government, their own courts, and freedom from tolls. In Italy communes became city-states; north of the Alps, chartered boroughs with mayors and councils.

**Guilds.** — Trade and craft were organised in guilds. A merchant guild held the town's trading privileges; craft guilds regulated who might work at a trade and how, setting quality, prices, hours, and training: a boy served as apprentice for years, worked as a journeyman for wages, and only then, with a fee and a masterpiece, became a master. Guilds were also religious confraternities, mutual aid societies, and militia companies, paying for masses, burying the dead, and staging mystery plays. They could be narrow and jealous, but they gave a craftsman a place and an identity the manor did not.

**Trade and money.** — Long-distance trade tied the Mediterranean to the North Sea. Italian merchants carried spices, silk, and sugar from the Levant; Flemish towns wove English wool; the fairs of Champagne brought the two together. The Baltic and North Sea traded timber, pitch, furs, grain, and herring, and the German towns that dominated it formed the Hanseatic League. Silver pennies were the common coin, but by the thirteenth century gold returned — the florin of Florence (1252), the ducat of Venice (1284) — and merchants used bills of exchange to avoid carrying coin. Jews, barred from most occupations, lent money and were persecuted for it; the Templars and Lombard bankers financed kings. The church forbade usury but found ways around it.

## The crusades

**Clermont, 1095.** — In November 1095 Pope Urban II held a council at Clermont and, after settling church reform, called on the knights of the west to aid the eastern Christians and recover Jerusalem. The Byzantine emperor Alexios I had asked the west for help against the Seljuk Turks; Urban turned the request into something far larger. Those who went, he promised, would receive remission of their sins, and their property would be under the church's protection. The call gave the papacy the leadership of Christian society it claimed, offered forgiveness to warriors whose trade was violence, and channelled their private warfare outward. The cry the chroniclers heard was *Deus vult* — God wills it.

**The First Crusade and Jerusalem.** — The expedition of 1096 was not one army but several; a popular movement led by Peter the Hermit was destroyed in Anatolia. The princes — Godfrey of Bouillon, Baldwin, Bohemond, Raymond of Toulouse — took Nicaea in 1097, crossed Anatolia, and captured Antioch in June 1098 after a desperate siege. In July 1099 they reached Jerusalem. The city fell on 15 July, and the killing was terrible. Fulcher of Chartres, who was there, wrote that in the Temple of Solomon almost ten thousand were killed, that the crusaders' feet were coloured to the ankles with blood, and that neither women nor children were spared. The victors carved out four states — Edessa, Antioch, Tripoli, and the kingdom of Jerusalem — defended by castles and by the new military orders, the Templars and Hospitallers, monks who were also soldiers and who became bankers and landowners.

**The later crusades.** — The crusader states were never secure. Edessa fell in 1144; the Second Crusade failed. In 1187 Saladin destroyed the crusader army at Hattin and retook Jerusalem; the Third Crusade (1189–1192) recovered the coast but not the city, and Richard I and Saladin agreed that pilgrims might visit. The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) never reached the Holy Land: diverted first to Zara and then to Constantinople, it sacked the greatest Christian city in the world, divided the Byzantine Empire, and set up a Latin empire that lasted until 1261. Crusading was also turned against Christians at home — the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) destroyed the Cathars of Languedoc, and the Teutonic Knights campaigned against the pagan Baltic peoples. Acre, the last mainland foothold, fell in 1291.

## The twelfth-century renaissance

**The recovery of learning.** — From the eleventh century western scholars rediscovered ancient learning, much of it through Arabic. In Toledo, Sicily, and southern Italy, translators turned Greek philosophy and science and their Arabic commentaries into Latin: Euclid's geometry, Ptolemy's astronomy, Galen's medicine, and above all Aristotle, whose logic and natural philosophy transformed what could be taught. Justinian's Roman law, long forgotten, was recovered and studied at Bologna by glossators. These texts gave the schools a vast new subject matter and a method.

**The universities.** — Out of the cathedral schools grew the universities of the thirteenth century. A university was at first a guild — *universitas* means corporation — of masters or students. Bologna was student-run, where students hired and paid the masters; Paris was a masters' university, strong in theology; Oxford followed Paris. A university with a papal or imperial charter was a *studium generale*, its degrees recognised elsewhere. The course began with the trivium — grammar, rhetoric, dialectic — and quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music — then the higher faculties of law, medicine, and theology. The method was scholastic: the master commented on a text, posed a question, and argued it out in disputation, setting authorities against one another. Peter Abelard's *Sic et Non* (c. 1122) set contradictory statements of the fathers side by side; Peter Lombard's *Sentences* (c. 1150) became the standard theology textbook. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), a Dominican, wrote the *Summa Theologiae*, setting out Christian doctrine as questions with objections and replies and using Aristotle to argue that reason and faith could not finally contradict each other.

**Art and letters.** — The same century produced a new architecture. The abbey church of Saint-Denis, rebuilt under Abbot Suger in the 1140s, used pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and stained glass to fill the interior with light; the style we call Gothic spread to the cathedrals of Chartres, Paris, Reims, Amiens, and Salisbury. In literature poets wrote in the vernacular: the *chansons de geste*, greatest of them the *Song of Roland*; the troubadours' songs of love; Chrétien de Troyes's Arthurian romances; Geoffrey of Monmouth's *History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136).

## Heresy and inquisition

**Heresy.** — The church's authority was defined by what it excluded. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries new movements appeared that it condemned as heresy. The most important were the Cathars, or Albigensians, strong in the towns of Languedoc. They held a dualist belief: the material world was evil, made by an evil god, and only the spirit was good; their perfect ones lived in extreme asceticism. Their criticism of the church's wealth found a wide hearing. The Waldensians, followers of Peter Waldo of Lyon, who gave away his goods about 1173, were condemned not for doctrine but for preaching without permission. Heresy was dangerous because it was popular and because it attacked the church's wealth and its claim to be the only road to salvation.

**Inquisition and the friars.** — The church answered with preaching, force, and a new court. The Albigensian Crusade, launched in 1209, killed and dispossessed the Cathars and broke the independence of southern France. Alongside it the church developed the inquisition: first bishops investigating their dioceses, then, from 1231, a permanent papal inquisition entrusted mainly to the mendicant orders. Procedure began with denunciation and ended with confession, penance, or, for the obstinate, delivery to the secular power to be burned; torture was authorised in 1252. The friars were the other side of the same coin. Francis of Assisi (1181/82–1226) renounced his wealth and founded the Franciscans; Dominic de Guzmán founded the Dominicans in 1216 to preach against heresy. The friars begged, preached in the towns, heard confessions, taught in the universities, and staffed the inquisition.

## Chronology

- **910** — The monastery of Cluny is founded in Burgundy.
- **987** — Hugh Capet is elected king of the west Franks; the Capetian dynasty begins.
- **1066** — William of Normandy conquers England.
- **1075** — The Dictatus Papae is entered in the papal register; the investiture conflict opens.
- **1077** — Henry IV does penance at Canossa and is absolved by Gregory VII.
- **1095** — Urban II calls the First Crusade at the Council of Clermont.
- **1099** — The crusaders take Jerusalem; the crusader states are founded.
- **1122** — The Concordat of Worms settles the investiture conflict.
- **c. 1140** — Gratian compiles the *Decretum*, the foundation of canon law.
- **1154** — Henry II becomes king of England; the common law takes shape.
- **1170** — Thomas Becket is murdered in Canterbury Cathedral.
- **1187** — Saladin defeats the crusaders at Hattin and retakes Jerusalem.
- **1204** — The Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople.
- **1209–1229** — The Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars of Languedoc.
- **1215** — Magna Carta is sealed at Runnymede; the Fourth Lateran Council meets.
- **1231** — The papal inquisition is established.
- **c. 1265–1274** — Thomas Aquinas writes the *Summa Theologiae*.
- **1291** — Acre falls; the crusader presence on the mainland ends.

## Questions to carry

- If "feudalism" was not one system, what did tie lords and vassals together, and how much did it vary from place to place?
- How did the church turn spiritual authority into land, courts, and taxes — and how did that wealth weaken its spiritual claim?
- At Canossa, who won? What does the answer depend on?
- Magna Carta protected "free men." Who were the free, and what did the charter leave untouched?
- Why did the call to recover Jerusalem end by sacking Constantinople?
- The same century produced the universities and the inquisition. How can both be understood as products of the church's authority?
