# The Cold War, the end of empire, and after

**Period:** c. 1945 to the present · **Region:** The world

## The world the war made

**The last conferences.** — In February 1945, with the Red Army already across Poland and Germany not yet defeated, Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Joseph Stalin met at Yalta, on the Black Sea. They agreed that Germany would be divided into zones of occupation, that the liberated peoples of Europe would choose their own governments, that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan within three months of Germany's surrender, and that the United Nations would be built around a Security Council in which each of the great powers held a veto. The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections; Stalin read it as a piece of paper. When the three leaders met again at Potsdam, outside Berlin, in July and August 1945, Roosevelt was dead and Churchill had been replaced mid-conference by Clement Attlee. The war in Europe was over; the war in the Pacific was not. The conference settled the Oder–Neisse line as Poland's western border, approved the expulsion of millions of Germans from the east, and issued an ultimatum demanding Japan's unconditional surrender. Nothing at Potsdam settled the future of Germany itself, and nothing decided the future of eastern Europe. Those two blanks became the Cold War.

**The United Nations.** — Delegates from fifty nations signed the Charter at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, and it came into force on 24 October. The Charter outlawed aggression and promised human rights, but it did not subject the great powers to the law they wrote. The Security Council's five permanent members — the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, China, and France — could each veto any substantive resolution. The United Nations could therefore act only where the great powers agreed, as it did in 1950 when the Soviet delegate happened to be absent, and could do nothing where they did not. A second plan, for international control of atomic energy, failed outright: the American Baruch Plan of 1946 proposed to share atomic technology only after a system of inspection, which the Soviet Union, racing toward its own bomb, refused.

**The bomb.** — The United States tested the first atomic device at Alamogordo, New Mexico, on 16 July 1945, and used the weapon on Hiroshima on 6 August and Nagasaki on 9 August. Japan surrendered on 2 September. The decision to use the bomb has been argued over ever since: some historians, following Truman's own account, hold that it avoided an invasion of Japan that would have cost hundreds of thousands of lives; others, pointing to intercepted Japanese peace feelers and to the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August, argue that Japan was already seeking terms and that the bomb was meant partly to impress Stalin. The debate is unresolved, and the two positions rest on different readings of the same documents. What is not in dispute is the consequence: a weapon that could destroy a city in one blow, held at first by one power, and then, after the Soviet test of 29 August 1949, by two.

## Two blocs

**The Iron Curtain.** — On 5 March 1946, in Fulton, Missouri, with Truman on the platform, Churchill said that "from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." The metaphor named a fact already taking shape. Soviet troops occupied Poland, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria; communist parties took power, at first in coalitions and then alone. In Czechoslovakia a coup in February 1948 ended the last democracy in the Soviet sphere. Churchill and Stalin had in October 1944 divided the Balkans into "percentages" of influence on a scrap of paper; the paper was cynical and, in the event, roughly accurate. In 1947 Stalin created the Cominform to coordinate the parties of the bloc. Eastern Europe was not a monolith — Yugoslavia under Tito broke with Moscow in 1948, and Albania later sided with China — but it was a sphere, and the West could not change it.

**Containment.** — George Kennan, the American chargé in Moscow, sent a "Long Telegram" on 22 February 1946 arguing that Soviet policy was driven by an insecurity that would not yield to reassurance, and that the answer was a patient, long-term "containment" of Soviet expansion. He published the argument, unsigned, as "The Sources of Soviet Conduct" in Foreign Affairs in July 1947. Containment became policy in the Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947, when Truman asked Congress for aid to Greece and Turkey. "I believe," he said, "that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." Congress voted about $400 million. The Greek civil war, in which communists had fought first the Germans and then the restored monarchy, ended in a royalist victory in 1949, and Turkey passed under American protection. The doctrine turned a regional crisis into a standing commitment.

**The Marshall Plan.** — On 5 June 1947, in a short speech at Harvard, Secretary of State George C. Marshall offered American money to a Europe that would organise its own recovery and cooperate across borders. The European Recovery Program spent about $13 billion between 1948 and 1952. It was both generous and self-interested: it rebuilt markets for American goods, steadied fragile democracies, and drew western Europe into an American-led order. The Soviet Union refused to join and forbade its satellites to do so, founding instead the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance in 1949. The plan also had a political edge: in Czechoslovakia the decision was taken under Soviet pressure, and the coup followed. In Germany the Western powers merged their zones and introduced a new currency in June 1948; Stalin responded with a blockade of West Berlin. For eleven months the Western Allies supplied the city by air, flying in about 2.3 million tons of food and coal, and in May 1949 Stalin lifted the blockade. That year Germany divided into two states, and on 4 April 1949 twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty, pledging that an attack on one was an attack on all. The Soviet answer came only in 1955, when West Germany joined NATO and the Warsaw Pact was formed.

## The bomb and the balance of terror

**Deterrence.** — Once both superpowers held nuclear weapons, the logic of war changed. The United States tested a hydrogen bomb in 1952 and the Soviet Union in 1953, each far more powerful than the bombs of 1945. By the 1960s American strategists spoke of "mutual assured destruction": any first strike would be answered by a second that would destroy the attacker's cities, so that a rational leader would not strike at all. The doctrine was never as tidy as its acronym. John Foster Dulles, Eisenhower's secretary of state, threatened "massive retaliation" against any aggression, which left allies wondering whether Washington would trade New York for Paris. The arms race went on regardless. Sputnik, the first satellite, went up in October 1957, and the apparent "missile gap" helped elect Kennedy in 1960. Eisenhower, in his farewell address of 17 January 1961, warned against the "military-industrial complex" — the permanent alliance of arms makers, officers, and politicians that the standoff had created. The two sides did agree to limit testing in 1963 and signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1968, but the arsenals kept growing; by the mid-1980s they held some 60,000 warheads between them.

**Near misses.** — Deterrence worked by frightening people, and several times it nearly failed through accident or misreading. On 26 September 1983, at the height of a war scare, a Soviet early-warning satellite reported five incoming American missiles. The duty officer, Stanislav Petrov, judged the report a malfunction and did not pass it up the chain; he was right. In November that year a NATO exercise, Able Archer, was read in Moscow as preparation for a real first strike. The Cuban crisis of 1962 was the closest call of all.

## The first wars

**Korea.** — On 25 June 1950 North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel. The Security Council, with the Soviet delegate absent in protest at the seating of Nationalist China, authorised a United Nations force under American command. Douglas MacArthur's landing at Inchon in September threw the North Koreans back, and the United Nations forces pushed toward the Yalu River, the border with China. In October Chinese troops intervened in force and drove them south again. Truman removed MacArthur in April 1951 after the general publicly demanded a wider war. An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953, leaving the peninsula divided near where it had begun. Some 36,000 Americans, perhaps 600,000 Chinese and North Korean soldiers, and two to three million Korean civilians died. The war made containment global: the United States quadrupled its defence budget, kept troops in Europe, and began to arm allies across Asia.

**Vietnam.** — The French, trying to hold their Indochinese colony, were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954. The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel and promised elections in two years; they were never held, and the United States backed a separate government under Ngo Dinh Diem in the south. American involvement grew from advisers to hundreds of thousands of troops after the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of August 1964. The Tet Offensive of January 1968, though a military failure for the communists, broke the American assumption that the war was being won and turned opinion at home against it. The last American troops left in 1973, and Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. About 58,000 Americans and more than a million Vietnamese died. The war split the Western alliance, fed a generation of protest, and left a lasting American reluctance to commit ground forces abroad.

## The end of the European empires

**India and Pakistan.** — The largest empire in history came apart first in its largest possession. The Indian National Congress, led after 1947 by Jawaharlal Nehru and before that by Mohandas Gandhi, had demanded self-rule for decades; Gandhi's campaigns of non-cooperation and his Salt March of 1930 made mass protest a method. The Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, fearing a Hindu-majority state, demanded a separate Pakistan. Britain, exhausted by war, sent Lord Mountbatten as viceroy with a deadline, and independence came at midnight on 14–15 August 1947. The country was partitioned along the Radcliffe Line, drawn in weeks by a lawyer who had never been to India. Perhaps twelve to fifteen million people crossed the new borders in both directions, and between several hundred thousand and a million were killed in the violence that accompanied the transfer. Gandhi was assassinated on 30 January 1948. Kashmir was left disputed, and remains so.

**Southeast Asia.** — The Philippines became independent in 1946, Burma and Ceylon in 1948, and Indonesia in 1949 after four years of fighting the Dutch, who had tried to reclaim the archipelago after the Japanese surrender. Ho Chi Minh had proclaimed Vietnamese independence on 2 September 1945 in a speech that quoted the American Declaration of Independence; France did not accept it, and the war ran for another thirty years. Malaya became independent in 1957 and Malaysia in 1963; Singapore separated in 1965.

**Africa.** — Ghana became independent on 6 March 1957 under Kwame Nkrumah, the first British colony in tropical Africa to do so. In 1960, the "Year of Africa," seventeen countries became independent, including Nigeria and the Belgian Congo. The Congo's independence on 30 June 1960 collapsed within days: the army mutinied, the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded with Belgian support, and the prime minister, Patrice Lumumba, was murdered in January 1961. United Nations troops intervened and stayed for four years. In Algeria, where a large settler population had made the French presence hard to surrender, a nationalist insurgency that began in 1954 fought a brutal war of ambush, torture, and reprisal; Charles de Gaulle, returning to power in 1958, first promised the settlers victory and then negotiated the Évian Accords of March 1962, after which Algeria became independent and most of the settlers left. The war killed perhaps 300,000 to a million Algerians. Britain's prime minister, Harold Macmillan, told the South African parliament on 3 February 1960 that "the wind of change is blowing through this continent." The Suez Crisis of 1956 had already shown that Britain and France could no longer act in Africa without American consent.

**The non-aligned.** — The new states did not simply choose sides. At Bandung, Indonesia, in April 1955, twenty-nine Asian and African governments met and denounced colonialism and racial discrimination. In 1961, at Belgrade, Nehru of India, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Tito of Yugoslavia, Sukarno of Indonesia, and Nkrumah of Ghana founded the Non-Aligned Movement. The United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 1514 on 14 December 1960, declaring that "all peoples have the right to self-determination" and that "inadequacy of political, economic, social or educational preparedness should never serve as a pretext for delaying independence." The new states often found themselves courted, and sometimes used, by both superpowers.

## The Chinese Revolution

**Mao and the People's Republic.** — China's civil war between the Nationalists under Chiang Kai-shek and the Communists under Mao Zedong had been interrupted by the Japanese invasion, then resumed. On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China in Beijing; Chiang's government withdrew to Taiwan. The revolution was the largest in history. Landlords' estates were redistributed to peasants, and in the countryside perhaps a million or more people were killed in the process. In 1958 Mao launched the Great Leap Forward, a scheme of collective farms and backyard steel furnaces intended to overtake Britain in a decade; it produced a famine that killed tens of millions between 1958 and 1962. In 1966 he set loose the Cultural Revolution, mobilising students as Red Guards against officials, teachers, and intellectuals; it lasted a decade and wrecked the party and the schools. China had intervened in Korea in 1950 and become the patron of Asian revolutions, but the Sino-Soviet split of the early 1960s divided the communist world, and in 1969 Chinese and Soviet troops fought on the Ussuri River. The split opened the way for Nixon's visit to China in February 1972, which turned the Cold War triangle.

## The Cuban Missile Crisis

**Thirteen days.** — In April 1961 a CIA-backed invasion of Cuba at the Bay of Pigs failed; in June Kennedy met Nikita Khrushchev at Vienna and came away shaken; in August the East German authorities began building a wall through Berlin to stop the flow of refugees. In October 1962 American reconnaissance discovered Soviet nuclear missiles under construction in Cuba. Kennedy imposed a naval "quarantine," and for thirteen days the two leaders faced each other while their forces prepared. On 28 October Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret promise to withdraw Jupiter missiles from Turkey. The crisis produced a telephone hotline between Washington and Moscow in 1963 and the first nuclear test-ban treaty. It also fixed a lesson in both capitals: that the risk of accident or misjudgment was itself a danger. The single most dangerous moment may have been aboard the Soviet submarine B-59, where an officer, Vasili Arkhipov, refused to consent to the launch of a nuclear torpedo.

## Détente and its limits

**Relaxation.** — From the late 1960s the two sides sought to manage their rivalry. West Germany's chancellor Willy Brandt pursued Ostpolitik, recognising the postwar borders and opening contacts with the East. The United States and the Soviet Union signed the first Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty and the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 1972, and in 1975 thirty-five states signed the Helsinki Final Act, which recognised the borders of Europe but also committed the signatories to respect human rights and to allow contacts across the divide. Helsinki's "third basket" became a weapon: dissidents in the Soviet bloc cited it, and the West used the follow-up conferences to press individual cases. Détente had limits. In 1968 the Soviet Union had crushed the Prague Spring and proclaimed the Brezhnev Doctrine, the right to intervene in any socialist state. In December 1979 it invaded Afghanistan to prop up a failing client. Jimmy Carter withdrew the second arms treaty from the Senate, the United States boycotted the Moscow Olympics of 1980, and a new round of missile deployment in Europe began. The "Second Cold War" was on.

## 1989 and the collapse

**Gorbachev.** — Mikhail Gorbachev became general secretary of the Soviet Communist Party in March 1985. His reforms — glasnost, or openness, and perestroika, or restructuring — were meant to revive a stagnating economy and a corrupt party, not to end the system. The Chernobyl disaster of April 1986 exposed the cost of secrecy. In 1987 Gorbachev and Reagan signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, eliminating a whole class of missiles, and in 1989 Soviet troops left Afghanistan. Gorbachev refused to use force to hold the bloc together — the joke ran that he had replaced the Brezhnev Doctrine with a "Sinatra Doctrine," letting each country do it its way. In 1989 the communist governments of eastern Europe fell in a matter of months. Hungary opened its border in the summer; Poland held round-table talks and elected a non-communist government; on 9 November the Berlin Wall was opened and then demolished; Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution ended communist rule in weeks; in Romania Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed on Christmas Day. Germany reunited on 3 October 1990. In August 1991 hardliners in Moscow tried to seize power; the coup failed, and on 25 December the Soviet Union ceased to exist. Boris Yeltsin's Russia took its place.

**Why it ended.** — The question is still argued. John Lewis Gaddis, in The Cold War: A New History (2005), emphasises the West's patient pressure — containment, the arms build-up, the appeal of free institutions — and the internal contradictions of a system that could not reform. Odd Arne Westad, in The Global Cold War (2005), argues that the conflict was decided as much in the postcolonial world as in Europe, and that the Soviet model failed where it mattered most: in the effort to win the future in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Others stress Gorbachev's own choices, the strength of nationalism in the non-Russian republics, and the long stagnation of the Brezhnev years. No single cause commands agreement, and "collapse" is itself a contested word.

## After the Cold War

**The unipolar moment.** — The Soviet Union's fall left the United States without a rival. In 1991 an American-led coalition expelled Iraq from Kuwait; in 1992 the Maastricht Treaty created the European Union; in 1994 the North American Free Trade Agreement took effect, and the World Trade Organization was founded in 1995. Markets, capital, and information crossed borders as never before, and a phrase — "the Washington Consensus" — named the package of liberalisation that international lenders urged on poorer countries. Francis Fukuyama's essay "The End of History?" (1989) asked whether liberal democracy had any serious rival left; his critics answered that history had other plans. The decade also brought the break-up of Yugoslavia and the wars in Bosnia (1992–95) and Kosovo (1999), and the genocide in Rwanda in 1994, in which some 800,000 people were killed in about a hundred days while the world's institutions stood aside.

**11 September and after.** — On 11 September 2001, hijackers from the Islamist network al-Qaeda flew two airliners into the World Trade Center in New York and one into the Pentagon, killing about 2,977 people; a fourth plane crashed in Pennsylvania. NATO invoked Article 5, its mutual-defence clause, for the first time. An American-led invasion of Afghanistan in October 2001 drove the Taliban from power but did not end the war. In March 2003 the United States invaded Iraq, claiming that it held weapons of mass destruction; none were found. The wars that followed lasted twenty years, cost hundreds of thousands of lives, and ended with the Taliban back in Kabul in 2021. The detention camp at Guantánamo Bay, opened in 2002, became a lasting controversy over the limits of law in a conflict without a clear front.

**The rise of China and the multipolar turn.** — Deng Xiaoping's reforms after 1978 turned China from a poor command economy into the world's workshop, and in 2001 it joined the World Trade Organization. Its economy became the second largest on earth. In 2013 it launched the Belt and Road Initiative, a programme of ports, railways, and loans across Asia, Africa, and Europe. Russia, under Vladimir Putin from 2000, rebuilt a centralised state, annexed Crimea in 2014, and in 2022 invaded Ukraine, prompting the largest war in Europe since 1945. The financial crisis of 2008, which began in American mortgages and spread worldwide, weakened confidence in the order the West had built. A new rivalry between the United States and China took shape, and commentators reached for the old phrase "Cold War" — though the two economies were far more entangled than the superpowers of 1947 ever were.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the alliance that won the war break apart so quickly, and how much of the Cold War was made by choices rather than circumstances?
- Nuclear weapons made a great-power war ruinous. Did they keep the peace, or only change where the wars were fought?
- India won independence at midnight; Algeria and Vietnam fought for years. What explains how differently empires ended?
- Historians disagree about why the Soviet Union broke up. Which explanation — Western pressure, internal decay, or Gorbachev's choices — does the evidence best support?
- The order of 1945 was built around a few great powers. What has replaced it, and what has not?
