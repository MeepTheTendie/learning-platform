# Learning, printing, and oceanic expansion

**Period:** c. 1400–1600 CE · **Region:** Italy · the Atlantic · the Indian Ocean

## The recovery of the past

**A manuscript pulled from a shelf.** — In 1417 the papal secretary Poggio Bracciolini, travelling through the monasteries north of Rome, asked to see the library and found a manuscript of the Latin poet Lucretius, the *De Rerum Natura*, which explains the world as atoms and void and denies the gods any interest in human affairs. It had survived in perhaps a single copy, unread for centuries, and might easily have been lost. Poggio copied it and sent it to his friends, and the poem returned to circulation. The monks had preserved the book; what Poggio added was a network of readers who wanted it. The recovery of the classical past was not an event but a habit — hunting for manuscripts, comparing copies, correcting them, and passing them on.

**Petrarch and the sense of an interval.** — The habit began with Francesco Petrarca, or Petrarch (1304–1374), crowned poet laureate on the Capitoline hill in Rome in 1341. He collected the Latin authors — Virgil, Cicero, Livy, Seneca — and read them as living teachers rather than dead authorities, writing letters addressed to Cicero and Virgil as though they might answer. Looking at the ruins of Rome, he saw a great civilisation separated from his own by centuries he regarded as dark. That judgement — a bright antiquity, a long decline, and now the possibility of rebirth — was the seed of the word "Renaissance." It was a story about the past, not a fact about it, and later ages hardened it into a period label.

**Humanism and the *studia humanitatis*.** — The word *humanism* is a later label for a movement that never had a founder. It grew from teachers who called themselves *umanisti* and from a course of study they named the *studia humanitatis*, the "studies of humanity": grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, read in the original Greek and Latin. The purpose was not information but formation — to make a person who could speak and write and judge well, joining wisdom to eloquence, the quality the Romans called *eloquentia*. The motto was *ad fontes*, "to the sources": go back to the oldest and best manuscripts rather than trusting later compilations. This was as much a religious programme as a literary one. Lorenzo Valla (c. 1407–1457) used the new philology — the close study of language, date, and style — to show that the Donation of Constantine, on which the papacy rested a claim to rule parts of Italy, was a forgery.

**Greek comes west; the school and the chancery.** — In 1397 a Byzantine scholar and diplomat named Manuel Chrysoloras arrived in Florence to teach Greek, and a generation of Italians learned to read Homer and Plato in the original. The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 drove more Greek scholars and manuscripts westward; Cardinal Bessarion gave his collection to Venice. Humanism spread through institutions. Vittorino da Feltre's school at Mantua and Guarino of Verona's curriculum at Ferrara shaped European grammar schools for centuries. In Florence the chancery was staffed by humanists; Coluccio Salutati and Leonardo Bruni used their Latin to defend the city against Milan and the pope.

## Patrons, artists, and the city

**The Medici and the price of culture.** — The greatest patrons of fifteenth-century Florence were bankers. Cosimo de' Medici (1389–1464) ran the family bank, with branches across Europe, and spent a fortune on churches, libraries, and scholars; his grandson Lorenzo (1449–1492), called "the Magnificent," gathered poets, philosophers, and artists at his table. Marsilio Ficino translated the complete works of Plato into Latin, and the young Pico della Mirandola wrote an oration on the dignity of man. Patronage was never charity: it bought reputation, influence, the loyalty of clients, and the prayers of the religious houses that received the money.

**Brunelleschi's dome.** — The cathedral of Florence, Santa Maria del Fiore, had been built with a vast octagonal opening at its crossing and no way to cover it. The gap was roughly forty metres across, far wider than any dome built since antiquity, and no one knew how to build centring strong enough to hold it. In 1420 Filippo Brunelleschi (1377–1446) won the commission with a plan that avoided centring: he raised the dome in rings of herringbone brickwork that supported themselves as they rose, bound by stone ribs and chains, and designed hoisting machines to lift the materials. He had studied the ruins of Rome, above all the Pantheon, and he solved a modern problem with ancient methods. The dome was completed in 1436. It is a useful case of what the Renaissance was: not the copying of antiquity, but the use of antiquity to do something no ancient had done.

**The workshop and the contract.** — Most art was made in workshops under contract. A patron and a master signed an agreement fixing the subject, size, materials, price, and date; a contract might specify that the Virgin's robe be painted in ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli and more expensive than gold. Around 1425 Masaccio used linear perspective in his *Trinity* in Florence, and in 1435 Leon Battista Alberti set out its rules as a branch of mathematics. Over the fifteenth century the leading artists climbed from craftsmen to courtiers. The story of Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519), Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564), and Raphael Sanzio (1483–1520) was largely the invention of Giorgio Vasari's *Lives of the Artists* (1550, enlarged 1568), which told the history of art as a decline after Rome and a slow rebirth. Vasari's book shaped how the period has been seen ever since; it is a source, and an argument.

## The press

**An invention is a system.** — Printing with movable type was not new in the world. Chinese printers had set pages from movable characters of baked clay by the 1040s, and Korean craftsmen were casting metal type by the 1230s. What Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith of Mainz, put together around 1450 was a system that could work at commercial speed: a hand mould for casting letters in quantity and to a uniform height, an oil-based ink that adhered to metal, a press adapted from the screw presses used for wine and oil, and a steady supply of paper. The most famous product was the 42-line Bible, printed at Mainz about 1455. It helps to keep three things apart. The invention was the combination of techniques. The diffusion was the spread of printing workshops across Europe. The application was what the printers chose to print.

**The diffusion of the press.** — Printing spread along the routes of trade and scholarship. From Mainz it reached Cologne, Strasbourg, Basel, Nuremberg, and then Venice, Paris, Lyon, and London. By 1500 presses had been set up in more than 250 towns, and the output of the first fifty years — books printed before 1501 are called incunabula, from the Latin for "swaddling clothes" — ran to some twenty million copies. Venice was the greatest printing city of Europe, and there Aldus Manutius from 1495 produced small, cheap octavo editions of the Greek and Latin classics and made Aristotle and Homer available to readers who could never have afforded a manuscript.

**What printers printed.** — The first best-sellers were not pagan classics but religious and practical books: Bibles, psalters, missals, law books, school grammars, calendars, and printed forms of indulgence. Humanist editions followed, then news-sheets and pamphlets. Print changed scholarship in ways easy to underestimate. A manuscript differed from copy to copy, and a reader could never be sure another reader held the same words; a printed text was identical in every copy and available in quantity, so a scholar could cite a page that a colleague in another city could turn to. The historian Elizabeth Eisenstein argued in *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979) that this fixity and abundance amounted to a communications revolution. Her critics, among them Adrian Johns in *The Nature of the Book* (1998), answer that early printed books were not automatically trustworthy, and that confidence in them had to be built slowly.

**Erasmus and the republic of letters.** — Desiderius Erasmus (c. 1466–1536), a Dutch scholar who lived in Paris, Basel, and Louvain, was the first author whose livelihood depended on the press. His *In Praise of Folly* (1511) mocked the clergy, the schools, and the princes in a voice that delighted readers across Europe, and his Greek edition of the New Testament (1516) put the Gospels before scholars in the original language. The scholars of Europe spoke of the *respublica litterarum*, the "republic of letters," a community held together by Latin, the post, and the printed book. When Martin Luther challenged the Church in 1517, the machinery was ready: his writings and the pamphlets that answered them were reprinted in tens of thousands of copies within a few years, and no bishop could recall them. Print did not cause the Reformation — the grievances and the theology came first — but it changed the speed and scale of every argument that followed, and it forced the Church to answer with its own weapon, the Index of Prohibited Books (1559).

## The Indian Ocean, already busy

**A sea with a calendar.** — Long before any European ship rounded Africa, the Indian Ocean was the busiest sea on earth. Its rhythm was the monsoon: from roughly April to September the wind blows from the south-west, carrying ships eastward from Arabia and East Africa toward India; from November to March it reverses and blows them home. Sailors had read the pattern for centuries, and the result was a web of ports — Calicut and Cochin on the Malabar coast, Cambay in Gujarat, Hormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, Aden at the mouth of the Red Sea, and Malacca at the narrows of the Malay peninsula. Pepper, cloves, cotton cloth, horses, porcelain, and silk all moved through them, carried by merchant communities who settled far from home and did business through family firms, credit, and a shared commercial law. The Portuguese did not enter an empty ocean. They entered a crowded one.

**Ships and instruments.** — The ships of the Indian Ocean suited it. The Arab and Indian dhow carried a lateen sail, a triangle slung from a long yard that let a ship beat upwind; the Chinese junk had battened sails, a stern rudder, and watertight compartments. Into these waters the Portuguese brought the caravel, a small, handy vessel with square and lateen sails. Their navigators used the magnetic compass, known in the Mediterranean by the twelfth century and ultimately a Chinese invention; the mariner's astrolabe and quadrant, which came from the Islamic world; and the portolan chart. None of these was a Portuguese invention. The Portuguese advantage lay in the organisation of crown-sponsored voyages, in cannon mounted in a ship, and in a willingness to use force at sea that most merchants could not easily match.

**Portugal reaches round Africa.** — Portugal was a small kingdom whose long Atlantic coast made the sea its natural road. In 1415 a Portuguese army took Ceuta, on the Moroccan side of the strait of Gibraltar, opening a window onto the Saharan gold and slave trades. Prince Henry, a son of the king, sponsored voyages down the African coast; Portuguese sailors settled the uninhabited Madeira and Azores and pushed past Cape Bojador in 1434. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa, which he called the Cape of Storms and the king renamed the Cape of Good Hope. Nine years later Vasco da Gama left Lisbon with four ships, rounded the Cape, and, with an Arab pilot, reached Calicut in May 1498.

**Calicut and the meaning of arrival.** — The ruler of Calicut was the zamorin, a Hindu king whose city was one of the great emporia of the pepper trade. The Portuguese account of the first meeting says that when the visitors were asked what they had come for, they answered that they sought Christians and spices. The gifts they had brought were mean, and the court was unimpressed. The encounter is a good place to be precise about words. The Cape route was new to the Europeans; it was not new to anyone else. Calicut had traded with the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and China for centuries, and its merchants knew the winds, the ports, and the prices better than the newcomers did. Vasco da Gama did not discover the Indian Ocean; he arrived in it, as one more trader among many, and the difference he made was that he came with cannon and a royal claim to a monopoly.

**Albuquerque's forts.** — Afonso de Albuquerque, governor of the Portuguese possessions in the east from 1509, gave the enterprise its shape. He took Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Hormuz in 1515 — not colonies in the later sense but fortified ports and trading posts, *feitorias*, held by small garrisons and linked by the sea. The crown required ships in the region to buy a *cartaz*, a pass, and tried to force the spice trade through its own channels. This *Estado da Índia* was always short of men, ships, and money, and it never controlled the ocean. Asian merchants sailed around the Portuguese posts, and the old ports kept trading. The Portuguese took a larger share of the spice trade and inserted themselves as armed toll-keepers; they did not replace the system they had joined.

## The Atlantic crossing

**Columbus's idea.** — Christopher Columbus was a Genoese sailor, born about 1451, who had sailed in the Atlantic trade and had married into a Portuguese family with connections to the Madeira sugar islands. He believed, wrongly, that the earth was much smaller than it is and that Asia reached far enough east to be within a few weeks' sail of Europe; a letter from the Florentine physician Paolo Toscanelli in 1474 encouraged the idea. The Portuguese crown declined to back him. Castile, fresh from the conquest of Granada in January 1492 and eager for a share of the eastern trade, agreed, and the Capitulations of Santa Fe gave Columbus the titles of admiral, viceroy, and governor of whatever he found, and a tenth of the profits. The contract shows what the voyage was: a commercial venture with a royal monopoly, not a scientific expedition.

**The voyage of 1492.** — Columbus left the port of Palos on 3 August 1492 with three ships — the *Santa María*, the *Pinta*, and the *Niña* — and a crew of about ninety. He reprovisioned in the Canaries and struck west on 6 September. On 12 October a lookout sighted land: an island of the Bahamas that its Taíno inhabitants called Guanahaní. Columbus named it San Salvador, took possession of it, as his own letter says, "with the royal standard unfurled," and went on to Cuba, which he believed to be the mainland of Cathay, and to the island he named La Española, Hispaniola. On Christmas Eve the *Santa María* ran aground and was wrecked; Columbus left thirty-nine men in a fort called La Navidad and returned to Spain. He made three more voyages and never admitted that he had found a continent unknown to him.

**Tordesillas.** — The kings of Spain and Portugal, both Catholic, asked the pope to settle their competing claims to the new lands. A series of papal bulls in 1493 drew a line, and the two crowns adjusted it by the treaty of Tordesillas in 1494: a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, with Spain to the west and Portugal to the east. The line ran through the coast of South America and gave Portugal its claim to Brazil. The treaty is often treated as a tidy act of diplomacy. It was also a claim of breathtaking scope: two European kings divided the inhabited world between them, and no one thought to ask the people who lived there.

**The Caribbean as a laboratory.** — In the islands the Spanish built the institutions that would govern their empire. The crown granted settlers the *encomienda*, a right to exact tribute and labour from a group of indigenous people in return for protecting them and instructing them in Christianity. In practice it was forced labour: the people of a village were assigned to a Spaniard, who set them to pan gold or work on his land, and the distinction between protection and ownership quickly disappeared. The Taíno population of Hispaniola, subjected to disease, hunger, overwork, and violence, collapsed within a generation; the estimates are disputed, but the direction is not. The crown tried to regulate the system with the Laws of Burgos in 1512, but the settlers had little interest in restraint. The Spanish priest Bartolomé de las Casas, who had held an encomienda and gave it up, spent the rest of his life denouncing the destruction it brought.

**The slave trade begins.** — As the indigenous population fell, the colonists looked for other labour. Sugar, introduced to the Caribbean from the Canaries and Madeira, needed large gangs of workers in the tropical heat, and from the first years of the sixteenth century African slaves were carried across the Atlantic to supply them. In 1518 the crown granted the first *asiento*, a contract to supply a fixed number of slaves to the colonies. The crossing, known later as the Middle Passage, packed men, women, and children into ships for weeks, and a large share of the captives died on the way. Las Casas, in his early years, suggested that African labour might spare the Indians, a proposal he came to regret. By 1600 the number of Africans carried to the Americas already ran into the hundreds of thousands.

## Conquest and exchange

**Cortés and the Mexica.** — In 1519 Hernán Cortés led some five hundred men from Cuba to the Gulf coast of Mexico. He scuttled his ships to prevent retreat and marched inland. His greatest asset was not his soldiers but his interpreters and his allies. A Nahua woman named Malintzin, called Doña Marina or La Malinche, translated between Spanish, Maya, and Nahuatl, and the Tlaxcalans, an enemy people of the Mexica, joined him with thousands of warriors. In November 1519 the Spaniards entered Tenochtitlan, the island capital of the Mexica empire, as guests of its ruler Moctezuma. Within months the Spaniards were driven out in a night of fighting they remembered as *La Noche Triste* in June 1520. A smallpox epidemic, carried by the Spaniards, swept the city and killed far more than the fighting. Cortés returned with a fleet built on the lake, and on 13 August 1521 the last Mexica ruler, Cuauhtémoc, surrendered. The Mexica empire was a tribute empire; many of the peoples it ruled hated it, and its subject cities joined the invader.

**Pizarro and the Inca.** — Francisco Pizarro, an illiterate soldier who had already failed twice on the South American coast, landed with fewer than two hundred men in 1532. The Inca empire was in the middle of a civil war between the emperor Atahualpa and his brother Huáscar. At Cajamarca, high in the Andes, Pizarro invited Atahualpa to a meeting and ambushed him, killing thousands of his attendants and taking the emperor prisoner. Atahualpa offered a ransom of a room filled with gold and two rooms with silver; Pizarro accepted the treasure and executed him in 1533 anyway. The Spaniards then took Cusco, the Inca capital. The Inca state's roads, storehouses, and system of organised labour, which had held a mountain empire together, also helped a small force seize the centre and claim its revenues. Resistance continued for decades.

**Why did so few conquer so many?** — The old answer was that a handful of Europeans with guns, horses, and steel defeated vast empires, and that the superior civilisation simply won. Historians now tell a more crowded story. The Spaniards had steel weapons, horses, and firearms, and these mattered in battle. Epidemic disease mattered far more: smallpox, measles, and influenza, to which the peoples of the Americas had no immunity, arrived with the invaders and often ran ahead of them, killing rulers and emptying towns. And the decisive factor in almost every campaign was indigenous allies, the thousands of warriors from peoples who had their own reasons to fight the Mexica or the Inca. The historian Alfred Crosby, in *The Columbian Exchange* (1972), showed how central the biological dimension was, and Matthew Restall, in *Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest* (2003), has argued that the story of a few heroic men is a myth and that the conquest was long, incomplete, and carried on indigenous backs. The debate is not closed, and each argument is a case, not a verdict.

**The Columbian exchange and silver.** — Crosby gave a name to the traffic that followed 1492: the Columbian exchange. From the Americas came maize, the potato, the tomato, cacao, tobacco, the turkey, and probably syphilis; from the Old World came wheat, rice, sugar, coffee, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and the pathogens smallpox, measles, and influenza. The traffic was not even. The Americas received diseases to which their peoples had no resistance, one of the greatest demographic disasters in recorded history; the Old World received new foods that helped feed a growing population. The exchange remade diets and landscapes on both sides of the ocean, and killed most of the people it reached. In 1545 a mountain of silver was found at Potosí, in the Andes, and in 1546 another at Zacatecas, in Mexico. The crown worked them with mercury amalgamation and with forced indigenous labour, the *mita*, and the silver flowed to Seville and, from 1565, west across the Pacific by the Manila galleon to China. For the first time the commerce of the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean, and the Pacific were joined in a single circuit. The new routes did not merely carry goods; they reorganised who worked for whom, and where wealth ended up.

- **1453** — Constantinople falls to the Ottomans; Greek scholars move west.

- **1492** — Granada falls; Columbus crosses the Atlantic.

- **1516** — Erasmus publishes his Greek New Testament.
- **1517** — Luther issues his Ninety-five Theses; print carries the dispute across Europe.

## Questions to carry

- The humanists said they were recovering antiquity. What was actually new in what they did with it?
- Print existed in East Asia before Gutenberg. What, if anything, was different about the press that reached Europe in the 1450s, and why did its effects differ?
- Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. In what sense was that a discovery, and in what sense only a new route for Europeans?
- The encomienda was granted as protection and instruction in Christianity. What did it become in practice, and why does the gap between the grant and the practice matter?
- Cortés had a few hundred men; the Mexica had a city of perhaps two hundred thousand. Why did the conquest succeed, and which explanations are best supported by the evidence?
- Crosby named the Columbian exchange; Restall attacked the myth of the few conquerors. Where do their arguments reinforce each other, and where do they pull apart?
- The same ships that carried spices and silver carried slaves. What does that say about the connection between trade and violence in this period?
- What might have happened if the Chinese or Ottoman states had chosen to contest the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean more directly? Was European dominance of the sea inevitable?
