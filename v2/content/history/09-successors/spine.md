# Successors to the ancient empires

**Period:** c. 395–900 CE · **Region:** Byzantium · Arabia · the Latin West

## The empire divided

**A death at Milan.** — On 17 January 395 Theodosius I died and the empire passed to his two sons: Arcadius took the east from Constantinople, Honorius the west from Milan and later Ravenna. Contemporaries did not read this as an ending. Diocletian had split the empire before; the division looked like administration. It lasted because the two halves faced different enemies, spoke different languages, and stopped needing each other. Within a century the west had no emperor; the east still collected taxes in Greek and called itself Roman a thousand years later. The question here is not who "fell" but how one late Roman world became several.

**Three sequences.** — The usual story runs in one line: Rome falls, the Middle Ages begin, Europe is made. That line hides the two most important developments of the period, which happened outside Latin Europe. Three sequences run in parallel. In the east the Roman empire survives and re-forms around Constantinople. In Arabia a new religious movement unites the peninsula and then swallows most of the Roman and Persian east. In the west small Germanic kingdoms inherit Roman land and Roman churchmen. The three trade, borrow, and fight. At the end they are not stages of one story but three answers to one question: what is an empire, and who may rule it?

## The eastern empire: law, church, and the imperial idea

**An empire of the city.** — Constantinople, founded by Constantine in 330 on the site of Byzantium, commanded the strait between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. It could be supplied by sea and controlled trade between Europe and Asia. Its people spoke Greek, though their emperors called themselves Roman to the end. The eastern provinces — Anatolia, Syria, Palestine, Egypt — were the richest in the old empire and paid the taxes that kept army and bureaucracy alive. The emperor was an autocrat whose legitimacy rested on the army, the church, and the belief that his rule was providential.

**Justinian and the law.** — Justinian (r. 527–565) left his greatest monument in a book. Roman law had grown for centuries into a tangle of statutes, opinions, and commentaries that even lawyers could not navigate; his commission under the quaestor Tribonian arranged it as the Corpus Juris Civilis, the "body of civil law": the Codex, the Digest, the Institutes for students, and the Novels issued later. The Corpus preserved Roman law in a form that could be copied and taught, and its later revival in medieval Italy shaped the legal systems of most of Europe. Its authors meant to make the empire governable; that is why the book outlived it.

**Hagia Sophia.** — In January 532 a riot at the chariot races, the Nika riot, burned central Constantinople and nearly drove Justinian out. He rebuilt on a colossal scale. Hagia Sophia, the church of the Holy Wisdom, was begun in 532 and dedicated on 27 December 537, designed by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidorus of Miletus. Its 31-metre dome appears to float above a ring of windows; Procopius said it seemed to hang from heaven on a golden chain. It fell in 558 and was rebuilt higher. The church was also a claim that the emperor's power and God's favour were the same thing.

## Justinian's reconquest and the Persian wars

**The western expeditions.** — Justinian also tried to recover the west. In 533 his general Belisarius destroyed the Vandal kingdom in north Africa in a single campaign; a long war against the Ostrogoths in Italy (535–554) then ruined the peninsula and ended in a hollow eastern victory, while a small foothold was won in southern Spain. The reconquest was expensive and stretched the army along frontiers it could not all hold. In 541–542 plague swept the Mediterranean and killed a large share of the population.

**The last war with Persia.** — The permanent rival was Sassanid Persia, which ruled Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau from Ctesiphon. In 602 the emperor Maurice was killed by a usurper, Phocas, and the Persian king Khusro II invaded, claiming to avenge him. The Persians took Syria, sacked Jerusalem in 614 and carried off the relic of the True Cross, and in 626 besieged Constantinople itself. It looked as though the empire would end.

**Heraclius.** — Heraclius, emperor from 610, rebuilt the army and in 624 carried the war into Persia. In December 627 he won at Nineveh; in February 628 Khusro II was deposed and killed by his own son, and the Persians made peace. In 630 Heraclius restored the True Cross to Jerusalem. He had saved the empire and exhausted it. Both Rome and Persia had spent twenty-five years and their reserves, and neither had recovered when the armies of the new religion came out of Arabia in the 630s. The eastern provinces fell because both great powers had just fought each other to a standstill. Heraclius died in 641, having lost Syria, Palestine, and Egypt.

## Arabia before and after Muhammad

**The peninsula.** — Arabia is not all desert. The Hijaz in the west had oases and caravan towns; Yemen in the south-west had cities and irrigation; the east had gulf ports. Arabs were organised in tribes and clans, and their wealth came from herding, oasis farming, and caravans carrying incense, leather, and spices north to Syria and Iraq. Mecca, a town of the Quraysh, was a pilgrimage centre around the Ka'ba, a shrine of many gods. Judaism and Christianity were established in Yemen, the northern oases, and among some tribes, and Jewish and Christian communities existed in Mecca and Medina.

**Muhammad.** — Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570, of the Hashim clan of the Quraysh. Orphaned young, he became a merchant and married Khadija, a wealthy widow. Around 610, meditating in a cave on Mount Hira, he began to receive what he and his followers understood as revelations from God through the angel Gabriel. The message was simple and radical: there is one God, creator and judge, and the rich of Mecca were wrong to trust in wealth and idols. The Meccan elite, whose position rested on the pilgrimage trade and the old cults, opposed him; some followers were persecuted, and some emigrated to Christian Ethiopia.

**The hijra.** — In 622 Muhammad and his followers left Mecca for the oasis town of Yathrib, later called Medina, "the city," some 350 kilometres north. This migration is the hijra, from which the Islamic calendar counts its years. It was a founding, not merely a flight: in Medina Muhammad became a judge and political leader as well as a prophet, arbitrating between feuding clans and between Arab and Jewish groups. The agreements attributed to this period, sometimes called the Constitution of Medina, treat the believers as a single community, an umma, with obligations cutting across tribal lines. From this point the movement had a territory, a treasury, and an army.

**The Quran.** — The Quran, "the recitation," is the collected revelations in 114 suras. Muslims hold it to be the literal word of God, recited by Muhammad and memorised and written down by his followers. For the historian it is the earliest and most important text of the new movement, but it is not a chronicle: it addresses and argues with its hearers and assumes a world of Jewish and Christian scripture that its audience partly knew. It speaks of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Mary, and insists that Muhammad continues the line of the prophets. Its legal and social passages, mostly from the Medina period, became the foundation of Islamic law. The sira and the hadith were compiled later and are evidence of what early Muslims remembered, not neutral transcripts.

## The conquests and the caliphate

**The succession.** — Muhammad died in 632 with no son and no clear instruction about who should lead. His companions chose Abu Bakr, his father-in-law, as caliph — a word meaning "successor" or "deputy." The title avoided any claim to prophecy: Muhammad was the last prophet, and the caliph was merely leader of the community. Not everyone accepted the choice, and the disagreement eventually split Islam into Sunni and Shi'a. Abu Bakr's first task was to hold the tribes together; the ridda wars brought back those who refused tribute, and the state that emerged was united and armed.

**The great conquests.** — What followed was one of the fastest conquests in recorded history. Under Abu Bakr (632–634) and Umar (634–644), Arab armies moved into Byzantine Syria and Palestine and Sassanid Iraq. At Yarmuk in 636 they defeated a larger Byzantine army and took Syria; Jerusalem surrendered in 638; Egypt was taken by 642; and at Nahavand in 642 the Persian army was broken, ending the Sassanid state by 651. Within twenty years the Arabs ruled from the Sahara to the Iranian plateau. Historians still argue why. The mutual exhaustion of Rome and Persia after 628 is certain, as are the mobility of the Arab armies and the resentment of many Syrian and Egyptian Christians at Byzantine taxation. Conversion was not the engine: most conquered peoples remained Christian, Jewish, or Zoroastrian for generations.

**The caliphate and its subjects.** — The conquered were not required to convert. Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians were classed as protected peoples, keeping their religion and internal affairs in return for submission and a poll tax, the jizya, and a land tax, the kharaj. This gave the rulers revenue and their subjects a place, and it meant the Muslim community was a ruling minority in a mostly non-Muslim empire. The caliphate was a government, not a church, with a treasury, an army, and provincial governors, borrowing from the Byzantine and Persian administrations it displaced. After 661 the Umayyad caliphs ruled from Damascus.

**The Umayyads at Damascus.** — The Umayyads took power in 661, after the murder of Ali and a civil war. Mu'awiya, the first Umayyad caliph, had governed Syria and ran the empire through alliances, cash, and a Syrian army. The Umayyads pushed west across north Africa into Spain, where the last Visigothic kingdom fell in 711, and east into Central Asia and the Indus valley. Their rule was Arab and at first privileged: non-Arab converts, the mawali, paid taxes and served but were treated as second-class. The state took a more clearly Islamic face under Abd al-Malik (685–705), who built the Dome of the Rock, minted the first distinctively Islamic coinage, and made Arabic the language of administration. In 717–718 an Umayyad army besieged Constantinople and failed.

**The Abbasid revolution.** — In 750 the Umayyads were overthrown in a revolution that began in Khurasan, in north-east Iran. Its agents were the mawali and others excluded by Arab privilege, and its military leader was a shadowy figure called Abu Muslim, who raised the black banners of the Abbasid family. At the battle of the Zab in 750 the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, was defeated and killed; one Umayyad prince escaped to Spain and founded a state at Córdoba. The revolution moved the centre of the Islamic world east, from Syria to Iraq, and changed who could hold power: the new elite was Muslim and increasingly non-Arab. The first Abbasid caliphs built a new capital at Baghdad in 762.

## Baghdad, the translation movement, and al-Khwarizmi

**The round city.** — Baghdad was founded by the caliph al-Mansur as Madinat al-Salam, the "City of Peace," on the Tigris, where the river routes of Iraq met the caravan roads to Iran and Syria. It was laid out as a great circle with the caliph's palace and the mosque at the centre, and it grew far beyond the plan within a generation. Under Harun al-Rashid (786–809), the court was famous for its wealth, its poets, its musicians, and its scholars; his advisers from the Barmakid family, and the embassies he exchanged with Charlemagne, are remembered in legend as the height of the Abbasid age. In 803 he destroyed the Barmakids, and after his death in 809 his sons fought a civil war that broke the unity of the caliphate.

**Translation.** — The Abbasid court sponsored one of the largest translation projects in history. From the eighth to the tenth century, scholars in Baghdad translated Greek, Persian, and Indian works into Arabic — Aristotle, Euclid, Galen, Ptolemy — often by way of Syriac, the language of the Christian scholars of Iraq. A library and research centre known as the House of Wisdom, especially associated with the caliph al-Ma'mun (813–833), gathered manuscripts and supported translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq. The result was not passive copying: Arabic scholars corrected, extended, and argued with the texts, and the mathematics and astronomy they produced were carried to Latin Europe centuries later. Paper, brought west from China and made in Samarkand by the eighth century, made books cheaper.

**Al-Khwarizmi.** — The most influential of the early Abbasid mathematicians was Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, who worked in Baghdad in the first half of the ninth century and died around 850. His book on calculation with Indian numerals introduced the decimal place-value system, and the Latin translation of his name gave Europe the word "algorithm." A second book, on restoring and balancing equations, gave algebra its name, from al-jabr, "restoration." He also compiled astronomical tables and a geographical work describing the known world. He drew on Indian, Greek, and Persian learning, and passed it on in a form later scholars could use and improve.

## The Latin West: successor kingdoms

**A Roman house, under new owners.** — In 476 a Germanic commander named Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, and sent the imperial insignia to Constantinople. What followed was still a real contraction: cities shrank, long-distance trade thinned, taxes grew harder to collect, and literacy narrowed until, outside Italy and a few towns, it was mostly the business of clergy. Germanic peoples had been settling inside the frontiers for generations, and by around 500 the west was divided among kingdoms: the Ostrogoths under Theodoric in Italy, the Visigoths in Spain and southern Gaul, the Vandals in north Africa, the Burgundians in the Rhône valley, the Franks in northern Gaul, and the Anglo-Saxons in Britain. Most kept Roman law for their Roman subjects, and they were the new owners of a Roman house, living in its rooms and using its furniture.

**Clovis and the Franks.** — The most important of the new kingdoms for later European history was the Frankish, and its key figure was Clovis, who ruled from 481 to 511. He defeated the last Roman commander in Gaul, conquered most of what is now France, and then made a decision that changed the balance of power in the west: he converted to Catholic, Nicene Christianity, probably around 496, rather than to the Arian Christianity that most other Germanic kings professed. This aligned him with the Gallo-Roman bishops, the effective local government in the towns, and gave his dynasty, the Merovingians, a claim to defend the orthodox faith. On Clovis's death the kingdom was divided among his sons, and the Merovingian kings grew weaker as their officials, the mayors of the palace, grew stronger.

## The Franks and the making of Carolingian power

**Charles Martel and the mayors.** — By the early eighth century the Merovingian kings were figureheads, and real power lay with the mayors of the palace of the Pepinid family. The most famous was Charles Martel, mayor from 718 to 741, who in 732 defeated a Muslim raiding force from al-Andalus between Poitiers and Tours. Later writers made this the salvation of Christendom, but it was one raid among many. What mattered more was Charles's work of building a following of armed retainers by granting them land. His son Pepin the Short took the next step: in 751 he deposed the last Merovingian king, Childeric III, with the pope's approval, and in 756 his campaigns against the Lombards gave the pope lands in central Italy, the foundation of the Papal States.

**Charlemagne's conquests.** — Pepin's son Charles, called Charlemagne, "Charles the Great," ruled from 768 to 814 and made the Frankish kingdom the largest state in the west since Rome. He conquered the Lombard kingdom of Italy in 774; fought the pagan Saxons of northern Germany in brutal wars from 772 to 804, which included forced conversion and, in 782, a mass execution of prisoners at Verden; pushed into Bavaria; campaigned unsuccessfully in Spain, where his rearguard was ambushed at Roncevaux in 778, an event later turned into the Song of Roland; and destroyed the Avar kingdom in the east. His empire reached from the Atlantic to the Elbe.

**Governing an empire.** — Charlemagne ruled through counts, who raised troops, held courts, and collected dues. To watch them, he sent out the missi dominici — "the lord's envoys," usually a pair of high officials, one a churchman and one a layman, who travelled through a district, heard complaints, and reported back. This was a stopgap, not a bureaucracy, but a real attempt at central control. At the base of the system was the bond between a lord and his armed follower, the vassal: a free man who swore fidelity to a lord, served him, often as a mounted warrior, and in return received protection and usually the use of land, the benefice or fief. Such personal contracts held the empire together more than any office did.

**Reform and learning.** — Charlemagne also standardised weights, measures, and coinage, issued laws called capitularies, and gathered scholars to his court, above all Alcuin of York, who reorganised the palace school and the copying of texts. Scribes developed a clear new handwriting, Carolingian minuscule, and most surviving classical Latin literature survives because Carolingian monks copied it. Einhard, a courtier, wrote a Life of Charlemagne modelled on Suetonius, describing the emperor's appearance, dress, habits, and love of learning. This renewal of letters was small and limited to a narrow elite, but it preserved a great deal.

## The coronation of 800 and the Treaty of Verdun

**Christmas Day, 800.** — On 25 December 800, in St Peter's basilica in Rome, Pope Leo III placed a crown on Charlemagne's head and the assembled Romans acclaimed him emperor. Einhard later wrote that Charles would not have entered the church that day if he had known what the pope intended. Historians doubt that claim, which made the emperor look modest and the pope presumptuous, but they cannot agree on what happened or who planned it. What is clear is what it claimed: that the imperial title in the west had been vacant, that the pope could confer it, and that a Frankish king now stood beside the Byzantine emperor as a Roman emperor. The Byzantines were furious, and only in 812 recognised him as emperor, though not as emperor "of the Romans."

**The empire divided again.** — Charlemagne's son Louis the Pious ruled from 814 to 840 and tried to keep the empire whole, but his sons rebelled and then fought one another. In 843 the Treaty of Verdun divided it into three: Charles the Bald took the west, roughly the future France; Louis the German took the east, roughly the future Germany; and their brother Lothair took a long middle strip from the Low Countries to Italy, with the imperial title. The oaths sworn at Strasbourg in 842 are the earliest surviving text in something like Old French and Old German. The middle kingdom did not last, but the division did. The Carolingian empire had lasted less than a century as a single state; it was held together by persons, not institutions.

## Continuities: administration, religion, exchange

**Administration.** — The three worlds inherited different parts of Rome. In the east, the imperial bureaucracy, the tax system, and Roman law survived, and the Corpus Juris Civilis remained the law of the empire. In the caliphate, the new rulers took over Byzantine and Persian tax offices and provincial governors and ran them in Arabic. In the west, central administration shrank, but the church kept Roman ideas of office, written record, and law. In all three, the rulers were a small literate elite who wrote in a sacred or classical language — Greek, Arabic, or Latin — and the survival of writing is the clearest thread connecting the period to the empire it came from.

**Religion.** — The period is also the story of three religions, each claiming the same ancestry. Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all looked to Abraham, and each held that it possessed the true continuation of his faith. Christians and Muslims both revered Jesus, though they disagreed profoundly about who he was. Jews lived under Christian and Muslim rule, sometimes tolerated and protected, sometimes persecuted. The Christian church itself divided slowly along the old imperial frontier, with the Greek-speaking east and the Latin-speaking west drifting apart in language, custom, and authority, though the formal schism between Rome and Constantinople came only in 1054. The religious disputes of the period were also disputes about political power, because in all three worlds the ruler claimed to defend the true faith.

**Exchange.** — The old story, argued by Henri Pirenne in the 1930s, was that the Islamic conquests closed the Mediterranean and ended ancient trade, so that the west had to rebuild from scratch around the north. Archaeology has complicated that picture: trade did not stop, it changed hands and shrank. Goods still moved — papyrus and oil and wine, silk and spices, furs and amber and slaves. Abbasid silver dirhams, minted in Iraq and Iran, have been dug up by the thousand in Scandinavia and Russia, carried north along the Volga and Dnieper rivers by Viking traders. Jewish merchants, the Radhanites, moved between the Islamic world, Byzantium, and the Frankish lands. The west was poorer than the east, but it was not sealed off.

**Three claimants, and no straight line.** — By 900 three rulers claimed a universal inheritance: the Byzantine emperor, who called himself Roman; the Abbasid caliph, who claimed to lead the community of the faithful; and, after 800, the western emperor. Each drew on Rome and on scripture. None of this was on rails. The eastern empire could have fallen to the Persians or Avars in 626; the Arab conquests could have stopped at the desert edge; the Umayyads could have survived the discontent of 750; Charlemagne's empire could have held together.

- **626** — Persians and Avars besiege Constantinople.

## Questions to carry

- The eastern empire is called "Byzantine," but its people called themselves Roman. What is at stake in the name?
- Justinian's law code outlived his empire. Why do legal texts sometimes travel further than the states that made them?
- Both Rome and Persia exhausted themselves before 632. How much of the Arab conquest does that fact explain, and how much does it leave out?
- The Quran is a revelation, not a chronicle. What can it tell a historian, and what must the historian look for elsewhere?
- Pirenne argued that Islam closed the Mediterranean. What does the archaeological evidence of coins and goods do to his argument?
- Charlemagne's coronation in 800: did the pope make the emperor, or the emperor the pope? What does each reading assume?
- The Treaty of Verdun divided an empire among heirs. Was that a failure of the Carolingians, or simply how inheritance worked?
