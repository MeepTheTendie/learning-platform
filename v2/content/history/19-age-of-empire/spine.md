# The age of empire

**Period:** c. 1830–1914 · **Region:** Africa, Asia, and the imperial powers

**A map redrawn in one lifetime.** — In 1830 the interior of Africa was known to Europeans mainly from the coast; only Algeria, seized by France that year, was a European colony of any size. By 1914 the continent had been partitioned: France held most of West and Equatorial Africa, Britain a chain of possessions from Egypt to the Cape, Belgium the Congo, and Germany, Portugal, Italy, and Spain the rest. The same decades saw Britain complete the conquest of India, France take Indochina, and Japan seize Taiwan and Korea. The Ottoman, Persian, and Chinese empires survived, but borrowed from European banks, submitted to European tariffs and consular courts, and lost territory to European armies. In 1880 perhaps a tenth of Africa was under European rule; by 1900 perhaps nine tenths. What drove the great powers to divide most of the world among themselves in a single generation?

**A new word for a new thing.** — Contemporaries called this "imperialism," and historians call the last third of the nineteenth century the "new imperialism." Older empires — Spanish and Portuguese in the Americas, British in India, Dutch in the East Indies — had been built over three centuries, mostly by chartered companies and by states that ruled indirectly through local rulers. The new imperialism was compressed into about forty years, aimed at Africa and Asia, and more often took the form of direct rule or a protectorate flying a European flag. Historians distinguish "formal empire," where a state claimed sovereignty, from "informal empire," where it controlled through loans, advisers, and gunboats without annexing. Much of Latin America and China belonged to an informal empire; the Congo Free State was a formal one. Expansion took different forms, which is one reason no single explanation has satisfied everyone.

## The new imperialism and its causes

**Industry's appetite for materials.** — Industrial Europe needed raw materials it could not grow or dig at home: cotton from Egypt and India, palm oil from West Africa (soap and machinery lubricant), cocoa from the Gold Coast, tin from the Malay states, copper from the Congo, and rubber from the Amazon and then the Congo and Malaya, as bicycles and automobiles created demand for tyres. In the 1890s the Congo's wild rubber vines were harvested under a quota enforced by soldiers of the Congo Free State, and Congo rubber entered the world market in thousands of tons a year until the price fell about 1906 and Malayan plantations took over. The appetite for rubber did not by itself cause the conquest of the Congo, but it made the conquest pay and shaped the brutality with which it was run.

**Capital looking for a return.** — The British economist J. A. Hobson argued in 1902 that the driving force was the financier, not the factory: because income was distributed unequally at home, too much capital lay in too few hands, and that surplus sought investment abroad where it could earn more. Colonial loans, railway bonds, and mining shares were the instruments, and their holders had an interest in the governments that would guarantee them. Disraeli's purchase of the Suez Canal shares in 1875 is a small case: British capital bought a share in a strategic waterway. Hobson's argument remains a starting point, but it fits some cases better than others. Many colonies were unprofitable, and the timing of annexations often followed local crises rather than the bond market.

**Steam, quinine, and the wire.** — Technology did not cause the partition, but it made it possible at a speed earlier generations could not manage. Steamships shortened the voyage to Africa and Asia and carried troops and heavy guns upstream. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, cut the passage to India by thousands of miles and made Egypt strategically vital. Quinine, extracted from cinchona bark and taken daily, let Europeans survive the malaria that had killed them in the tropics. The electric telegraph reached India by cable in 1870 and went around Africa by 1879, so a governor could ask London a question and get an answer the same day. Breech-loading rifles and, after 1884, the Maxim gun gave small European forces a firepower advantage over much larger armies. These advantages did not guarantee victory, but they made the conquest of large territories thinkable with small forces.

**Nationalism, prestige, and race.** — The powers also competed for reasons that had little to do with trade. After its defeat by Prussia in 1870–71, France looked to colonial conquest to restore its standing; Germany, unified in 1871, wanted colonies to prove it had arrived; Italy, unified in 1861, sought an empire and found one in Libya and the Horn of Africa. Colonies were symbols of rank in a Europe that measured states by their place in a hierarchy. The language of justification was increasingly racial. Ideas drawn from Darwin and misapplied to human societies — Social Darwinism — pictured nations and "races" in a struggle in which the strong survived and the weak were displaced. Writers and politicians spoke of a "civilizing mission" (the French mission civilisatrice) and a duty to bring order, commerce, and Christianity to peoples described as backward or childlike. Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899), published as the United States took the Philippines, put the sentiment in verse. Such ideas helped make the conquests seem necessary and honourable; they are best read as an ideology of the period, not a neutral description of it.

## The Scramble for Africa

**A continent before the rush.** — In 1870 most of Africa was governed by African states and peoples: the Sokoto Caliphate in what is now northern Nigeria, the Asante and Dahomey kingdoms, the Zulu kingdom and the Boer republics, and the Ethiopian empire. Europeans held coastal footholds and traded with the interior, but did not rule it. Between 1880 and 1900 almost the whole continent was claimed; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. The reasons varied: gold and diamonds in the south, the collapse of Ottoman and Egyptian authority in the Nile valley, Anglo-French rivalry, and the fear that a rival would take a territory first.

**The Berlin Conference.** — The conference met in Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885 at the invitation of the German chancellor Otto von Bismarck. Fourteen states sent representatives; no African ruler was present. The General Act it produced did not divide Africa — the partition had already begun and continued after it closed — but it set rules. It declared freedom of trade in the Congo basin and freedom of navigation on the Congo and Niger and, in Articles 34 and 35, laid down that a power taking land on the African coast must notify the other signatories and must "insure the establishment of authority ... sufficient to protect existing rights." That clause, the rule of "effective occupation," was decisive: a paper claim to a coastline was no longer enough. A power had to send officials, soldiers, and posts into the interior to hold what it claimed. The Act also spoke of watching "over the preservation of the native tribes" and of "bringing home to them the blessings of civilization" — the language of the civilizing mission, written into the founding document of the partition.

**The Congo Free State.** — In 1885 the conference recognised the Congo basin as the personal property of King Leopold II of the Belgians, who had employed the explorer Henry Morton Stanley to stake it out and ran it through a private body, the International African Association. Leopold's agents divided the territory into concessions and set quotas for ivory and, later, wild rubber. Village chiefs were held responsible for deliveries; failure was punished by burning villages, taking hostages, and flogging with the chicotte, a whip of twisted hippopotamus hide. Men were killed, and hands were cut off as proof that ammunition had been used. The numbers are disputed; the historian Adam Hochschild estimated a fall of perhaps ten million during Leopold's rule, while other scholars give lower figures. The system rested on forced labour and was documented at the time: the British consul Roger Casement reported on it in 1904, the journalist E. D. Morel campaigned against it, and Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) drew on his own voyage up the river. Belgium annexed the Congo in 1908, changing the flag but not the forced labour.

**Gold, diamonds, and the Boer War.** — In the south the scramble was driven by minerals. Diamonds were found near Kimberley in 1867 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, and the mines drew British capital and immigrant labour into the Boer republics of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. Cecil Rhodes, who made a fortune at Kimberley and became prime minister of the Cape Colony, wanted the mineral wealth and the interior for Britain. In December 1895 he sponsored a raid into the Transvaal led by Leander Jameson, which failed; a Boer ultimatum and war followed in October 1899. The British expected a short campaign and met a guerrilla war that lasted until 1902. To break it, the army burned farms, destroyed crops, and interned Boer civilians and their African servants in concentration camps, where disease killed some 26,000 to 28,000 Boer women and children and perhaps 20,000 or more Africans. The Treaty of Vereeniging (1902) ended the war, and the Union of South Africa was created in 1910 as a self-governing British dominion in which whites held political power.

**The limits of the scramble.** — At Adwa in the Ethiopian highlands on 1 March 1896 an Italian army invading from Eritrea was destroyed by the forces of Emperor Menelik II, forcing Italy to recognise Ethiopian independence in the Treaty of Addis Ababa. The partition was not a single unstoppable force but a series of contests whose outcomes depended on local power, distance, and the willingness of European governments to fight one another.

## British India: the Raj and the rebellion of 1857

**A company that became a state.** — The English East India Company, founded in 1600, ruled India for a century before the Crown took over. Its victory at Plassey in 1757 gave it Bengal, and from that base it expanded through war, treaty, and the annexation of states whose rulers died without a natural heir — the "doctrine of lapse" applied by Governor-General Dalhousie in the 1850s. By mid-century it governed most of the subcontinent, directly or through princes who accepted its authority. Its army was largely Indian: by 1857 some 230,000 sepoys served alongside about 40,000 British troops.

**The rebellion of 1857.** — The immediate trigger was a new rifle cartridge. The Enfield's paper cartridge had to be bitten before loading, and the grease on it was rumoured to be made from the fat of cows and pigs — offensive to Hindus and Muslims alike. On 10 May 1857 sepoys at Meerut rose, marched to Delhi, and proclaimed the aged Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader. The revolt spread across the north: Cawnpore, Lucknow, Jhansi, where the Rani Lakshmibai fought, Kanpur, Arrah. It was not one movement with one aim: some rebels fought for the emperor, some against the land revenue or the missionaries, and many Indians fought for the British. The British retook the north with a ferocity both sides remembered: towns were sacked, rebels were tied to the mouths of cannon and blown apart, and the last Mughal was tried and exiled to Burma. Estimates of the dead run into the hundreds of thousands. The rebellion failed, but it ended the Company's rule.

**The Raj.** — In 1858 the Government of India Act transferred India to the Crown. A secretary of state sat in London, a viceroy governed in Calcutta and later Delhi, and Queen Victoria promised that Indians would be treated fairly and admitted to office without regard to religion. In 1877 she was proclaimed Empress of India at a durbar in Delhi. The Raj built railways — the first line opened in 1853, and by 1900 India had tens of thousands of kilometres of track — and telegraphs, and created a civil service recruited by examination in London. It also presided over repeated famines — those of 1876–78, 1896–97, and 1899–1900 killed millions. Dadabhai Naoroji, an Indian merchant and scholar who moved to London in 1855 and in 1892 became the first Indian elected to the British Parliament, argued that India's wealth was drained to Britain through taxation, pensions, and the "home charges" paid for administration and debt, and that this drain, rather than Indian backwardness, explained the country's poverty. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, became the forum for this argument and for the demand, at first modest, for a larger Indian voice in government.

## China: opium, rebellion, and the powers

**The opium trade and the first war.** — For most of the eighteenth century European merchants paid for Chinese tea and silk largely in silver, which China's government was content to receive. To balance the trade, British merchants from India sold opium, grown in Bengal and smuggled into China, and by the 1830s the traffic had reversed the flow of silver and created a large population of addicts. In 1839 the emperor sent an official, Lin Zexu, to Canton, where he confiscated and destroyed more than 20,000 chests of opium. Britain sent a naval force, and the First Opium War (1839–42) ended with the Treaty of Nanking. China ceded Hong Kong, opened five ports to British residence and trade, paid an indemnity of twenty-one million dollars, and agreed to a fixed tariff. The treaty did not even mention opium; the trade continued. Article 3 cedes Hong Kong "to be possessed in perpetuity," Article 4 makes China pay for the opium Lin had destroyed, and Article 6 makes China pay twelve million dollars for the cost of the expedition against it.

**The second war and the Taiping.** — A second war, fought by Britain and France between 1856 and 1860, produced more treaty ports, the legalisation of the opium trade, and the destruction of the Summer Palace outside Beijing. In the same years China was torn apart from within. The Taiping Rebellion began in 1850 under Hong Xiuquan, who had failed the imperial examinations and, after reading Christian tracts, believed himself the younger brother of Jesus Christ. His movement captured Nanjing in 1853 and made it the capital of a "Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace," controlling much of the Yangtze valley at its height. The Qing defeated it with the help of European-officered armies, but the war lasted until 1864 and killed, by most estimates, between twenty and thirty million people. In its wake officials began the Self-Strengthening Movement, buying Western guns and ships and building arsenals, while leaving the examination system and the imperial court largely untouched.

**From spheres of influence to the Boxers.** — China's defeat by Japan in 1894–95 exposed how little the self-strengthening had achieved. European powers and Japan then demanded leaseholds and "spheres of influence" — Germany at Qingdao, Russia at Port Arthur, Britain at Weihaiwei, France at Guangzhouwan — and in 1899 the United States circulated the "Open Door" notes asking that trade within them remain open to all. In 1899 a movement of peasants and artisans, called by foreigners the "Boxers," rose against foreign influence and the Christian missions. It spread to Beijing, where the Boxers besieged the foreign legations in the summer of 1900. An army of eight nations marched to relieve the siege, looted Beijing, and imposed the Boxer Protocol of 1901, requiring China to pay an indemnity of some 450 million taels and to permit foreign troops near the capital. The Qing tried a final round of reforms, but the dynasty fell in 1911 and a republic was proclaimed in 1912.

## Japan: the Meiji Restoration and its imperial turn

**The unequal treaties and the Restoration.** — Japan had been governed since 1603 by the Tokugawa shoguns, who limited foreign trade and contact. In July 1853 an American squadron under Commodore Matthew Perry entered Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open its ports; Perry returned in 1854, and Japan signed the Treaty of Kanagawa, followed by similar "unequal treaties" with Britain, Russia, and the Netherlands that set low tariffs and allowed foreigners to be tried in their own consular courts. In 1868 a coalition of samurai from Satsuma and Chōshū seized power in the name of the young Emperor Meiji and issued a Charter Oath promising deliberative assemblies, the end of feudal divisions, and the pursuit of knowledge "throughout the world." The government abolished the domains in 1871, replaced them with prefectures, ended the samurai's hereditary stipends, and in 1873 introduced universal male conscription. It built railways, telegraphs, and factories and adopted the slogan "rich country, strong army" (fukoku kyōhei). A rebellion of discontented samurai was crushed at Satsuma in 1877, and in 1889 the emperor granted a constitution creating a parliament, the Diet. Japan had done in a generation what China had attempted and failed to do.

**An empire of its own.** — Japan's new army and navy were soon used against its neighbours. In 1894–95 Japan defeated China in a war over Korea and took Taiwan; Russia, Germany, and France then forced it to give back the Liaodong peninsula, a humiliation that fuelled later rivalry. In 1902 Japan signed an alliance with Britain, and in 1904–05 it went to war with Russia, destroying the Russian fleet at Tsushima and winning the peace at Portsmouth in 1905 — the first defeat of a European great power by an Asian state in the modern era. Japan took Russia's lease on the Liaodong peninsula and its Manchurian railway, made Korea a protectorate in 1905, and annexed it in 1910. The Meiji state, which had begun by resisting foreign domination, ended by building an empire that rivalled those of Europe, and its example was studied by nationalists across Asia as proof that the European advantage was not permanent.

## The Ottoman Empire and the Eastern Question

**The "sick man of Europe."** — European diplomats called the Ottoman Empire the "sick man of Europe," and the problem of its break-up they called the Eastern Question. The empire stretched from the Balkans to North Africa, but it was losing provinces to nationalism: Greece won independence in the 1830s, and Serbia, Wallachia, and Moldavia gained autonomy. Russia wanted the Straits and access to the Mediterranean and posed as protector of the empire's Orthodox subjects; Britain wanted to keep Russia away from the route to India. In the Crimean War (1853–56) Britain, France, and the Ottoman Empire fought Russia and won, and the Treaty of Paris of 1856 admitted the empire to the "concert of Europe" and forbade interference in its internal affairs. The guarantee did not last. In 1877–78 Russia defeated the Ottomans again, and the Congress of Berlin of 1878, chaired by Bismarck, cut the empire's European territories back sharply, gave Cyprus to Britain, and recognised the independence of Serbia, Romania, and Bulgaria.

**Reform, debt, and the loss of Egypt.** — The Ottoman government tried to reform itself. The Tanzimat reforms of the mid-century reorganised the army and administration, introduced new courts and schools, and promised equality before the law to non-Muslims; in 1876 a constitution and a parliament were briefly introduced. But reform cost money, and the empire borrowed heavily from European banks. In 1881 a public debt administration controlled by European creditors was set up to collect customs and taxes and pay the bondholders, a direct loss of fiscal sovereignty. Egypt, nominally an Ottoman province, was a separate case: the Suez Canal opened in 1869, the khedive's debts forced him to sell his shares to Britain, and in 1882, after a nationalist revolt led by Ahmed Urabi, Britain occupied the country and governed it in fact. France took Tunisia in 1881, and Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia in 1908. The Young Turks seized power in 1908 and restored the constitution, but the empire lost most of its remaining European territory in the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, and when it entered the First World War on the side of Germany in November 1914 the Eastern Question had not been answered, only postponed.

## Anti-colonial resistance and early nationalism

**Wars of resistance.** — The partition was never peaceful and never complete. The Zulu kingdom fought the British in 1879, winning at Isandlwana before being defeated at Ulundi; the Mahdist state in the Sudan, founded by Muhammad Ahmad in 1881, killed General Charles Gordon at Khartoum in 1885 and held out until Kitchener's army destroyed it at Omdurman in 1898; Samori Ture resisted the French in West Africa until his capture in 1898; the Herero and Nama peoples of German South-West Africa rose in 1904 and were crushed by a campaign of extermination, with perhaps 65,000 Herero and 10,000 Nama dead. In German East Africa the Maji Maji rebellion of 1905–07 was answered with scorched earth and mass starvation.

**The beginnings of nationalism.** — Alongside armed resistance grew a politics of organised nationalism, at first small and often loyalist. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, met annually, petitioned the government, and produced economic arguments such as Naoroji's; only in the twentieth century would it demand independence. In China, Sun Yat-sen founded the Revive China Society in 1894 and the United League (Tongmenghui) in 1905, and the revolution of 1911 brought down the Qing and produced a republic in 1912. In Egypt, the ideas of Ahmed Urabi were carried forward by Mustafa Kamil and a nationalist press. The first Pan-African Conference met in London in 1900. The language of self-determination that Europe used about itself was being turned, slowly, against the empires.

## The empires at their height

**The world of 1914.** — By 1914 steamship lines, submarine cables, and railways tied the colonies to the metropoles, and the leading powers ruled empires on which, they believed, their prosperity and prestige depended. The partition was not total: Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent, and settler dominions such as Canada, Australia, and New Zealand had become self-governing. The First World War, which began among the European powers in August 1914, drew the colonies into a conflict not of their making and set in motion forces that would, within a few decades, begin to dismantle the empires.
