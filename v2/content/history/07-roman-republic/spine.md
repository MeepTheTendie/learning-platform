# Rome: city, republic, Mediterranean power

**Period:** c. 509–27 BCE · **Region:** Italy and the western Mediterranean

## The site, the legend, and the kings

**A ford on the Tiber.** — Rome began at a crossing. The Tiber runs to the sea some fifteen miles south-west of the city, and just above the last navigable point it bends around a small island, the Insula Tiberina, where the water is shallow enough to bridge. The first dependable ford of the lower river sits below the Palatine, one of a ring of hills — Palatine, Aventine, Capitoline, Quirinal, Viminal, Esquiline, Caelian — above the flood plain. Whoever held the ford and the hill could tax the road and watch both banks: not a harbour, but a choke point between Latium and Etruria.

**Romulus and Remus.** — The Romans told themselves their city was founded by twins. Rhea Silvia, a Vestal virgin and daughter of the king of Alba, bore Romulus and Remus and named Mars as their father. The king, Amulius, had them thrown into the Tiber; the river was in flood, and the basket caught in a fig tree at the foot of the Palatine. A she-wolf suckled them, a shepherd raised them, and as young men they killed Amulius and set out to found a city. They quarrelled over which hill and which name. Each watched the sky for birds; Remus saw six vultures first, Romulus twelve after, and the dispute turned to blows. In the version Livy thought more common, Remus jumped over the wall Romulus was building, and Romulus killed him. The traditional date was 21 April 753 BCE. Livy, writing seven centuries later, notes that some said the "she-wolf" was a prostitute, since *lupa* meant both.

**Aeneas and the Trojan claim.** — A second legend gave Rome a Trojan ancestry. Aeneas escaped the sack of Troy and reached Latium, where he married the king's daughter and founded Lavinium; his son Iulus founded Alba Longa, from which Romulus and Remus were said to descend. Caesar and Augustus claimed Iulus as an ancestor, and Vergil's *Aeneid* made the story Rome's founding epic. Both legends were doing political work long after the events they describe.

**What the ground shows.** — Archaeology tells a plainer story. There were settlements on the Palatine from at least the eighth century BCE; the earliest burials in the Forum go back to about 1000 BCE. In 1988 Andrea Carandini excavated a wall on the north slope of the Palatine, dated to the middle of the eighth century, and argued it was the wall of Romulus; the claim is contested, for it may be a sixth-century fortification or a boundary marker. An inscription on a black stone pavement in the Forum, the Lapis Niger, cut around 570–550 BCE, is among the oldest Latin texts known. The bronze she-wolf in the Capitoline Museums, long thought Etruscan, is now dated to the eleventh or twelfth century CE — medieval, not ancient. The evidence can show a village becoming a city; it cannot show twins or a wolf.

**The Etruscan kings.** — Roman tradition counted seven kings between 753 and 509 BCE: Romulus; Numa Pompilius, who gave the city its religious law; Tullus Hostilius; Ancus Marcius; Lucius Tarquinius Priscus; Servius Tullius; and Tarquinius Superbus. The last three, at least, are Etruscan names, and the sixth-century city bears Etruscan marks: the fasces, bundles of rods carried before a magistrate as a sign of the power to punish; the purple-bordered toga; and the augury that read the gods' will in the flight of birds. The Etruscans, whose cities lay to the north in Tuscany, dominated sixth-century Italy, and Rome sat on their southern edge. Servius Tullius was credited with the census and with the military assembly of the centuries, the framework that outlasted the kings.

**The fall of the kings.** — Tradition dated the expulsion of Tarquinius Superbus to 509 BCE and made it a political revolution: the king's son raped Lucretia, a noble matron, who killed herself; her husband's friend Lucius Junius Brutus swore to drive out the royal family, and the people swore with him. The king was expelled, and two annual magistrates were elected in his place. It has a rival: the Etruscan king Lars Porsenna of Clusium attacked Rome, and both Pliny and Tacitus say he took the city, which would make 509 BCE less a liberation than a change of master. What is clear is that by the end of the sixth century Rome was governed by annual magistrates and a council of leading families, and that the word *rex*, king, became an insult.

## The republic's institutions

**The consul and imperium.** — Two consuls were elected each year, and each held *imperium*, the power to command. Imperium was originally military: the right to lead an army, to take the auspices and so learn whether the gods approved, to coerce and punish, and, in the field, to decide life and death. The consuls carried the fasces, whose axes marked the power of execution. Inside the city the power was hedged: a citizen could appeal (*provocatio*) against a magistrate's sentence, and each consul could veto the other, so the two were colleagues, not rivals for sole command. Outside the city the consul was nearly absolute — and it was in the provinces that the republic's power most often escaped its checks.

**The senate.** — The senate was a council of about three hundred men, drawn by the censors from former magistrates and held for life. It could not pass laws, but its advice (a *senatus consultum*) carried the weight of *auctoritas*, the prestige of experience, and in practice guided the state. It controlled the treasury, decided which provinces went to which magistrates, received ambassadors, and conducted foreign policy; its members filled the juries of the great courts.

**The assemblies.** — Power also lay with the people, but in assemblies organized by wealth and birth. The *comitia centuriata*, the army assembly, was divided into 193 centuries based on the census classes; the wealthiest centuries voted first, and once a majority was reached the voting stopped, so that the votes of the rich usually decided. It elected the consuls, praetors, and censors and declared war. The *comitia tributa*, organized by thirty-five territorial tribes, elected the lesser magistrates and passed laws; the *concilium plebis*, the assembly of the plebeians alone, elected the tribunes and passed plebiscites.

**The tribunes.** — The office of tribune of the plebs was created, on the traditional account, in 494 BCE, when the plebeians withdrew from the city in protest. Ten tribunes were elected each year by the plebeian assembly, and they were sacrosanct: anyone who harmed one was outlawed, and the plebeians swore to defend them. Their weapon was *intercessio*, the veto: a tribune could block a magistrate's act, a senate decree, or a law, and one tribune could block another. The tribunate gave the common citizens a voice and a shield; in the late republic it became the instrument by which ambitious men — the Gracchi, Saturninus, Clodius — drove their programmes through.

**The dictator and the cursus honorum.** — In emergencies the consuls could appoint a dictator, a single magistrate with supreme imperium, for no more than six months, with a subordinate master of the horse. The classic case is Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus, called from his plough in 458 BCE to rescue a surrounded army, who resigned after sixteen days. Ordinary ambition ran along the *cursus honorum*, the sequence of offices: quaestor (finance), aedile (public works and games), praetor (justice), and consul, with the censorship as a crowning honour. The families that regularly reached the consulship formed the *nobilitas*; a man who reached it first in his family was a *novus homo*, a "new man," like Marius or Cicero.

## The Conflict of the Orders

**Patricians and plebs.** — Early Rome was divided between patricians and plebeians. The patricians were the hereditary families who held the priesthoods and, at first, the magistracies; the word is linked to *patres*, "fathers," and their claim was descent. The plebs were everyone else — the great majority of citizens, not necessarily poor, for some plebeians were rich, but without access to office or to the gods' favour as the patricians defined it. The struggle between them, the Conflict of the Orders, ran for more than two centuries, a contest over who could hold power, marry whom, and know what the law said.

**Written law and the Twelve Tables.** — The plebeians' first demand was for law that could be read. In 451–450 BCE a board of ten men, the decemvirs, published the Twelve Tables, the first Roman law code, set up in the Forum for all to see. Fragments survive, showing a hard, practical body of rules about debt, property, injury, and family: a father's power over his household, the penalty for breaking a bone, the obligation of a debtor. The point was not that the law was gentle but that it was public: a rule written down could not be invented by a patrician judge to suit himself.

**The stages of the settlement.** — Step by step the plebeians won access. In 445 BCE the *lex Canuleia* allowed patricians and plebeians to marry. In 367 BCE the Licinian-Sextian laws provided that one of the two consuls must be a plebeian and set limits on the public land a man could occupy. In 326 BCE, on the traditional date, the *lex Poetelia* abolished *nexum*, the bond by which a debtor could be seized and made a slave for what he owed. In 300 BCE the *lex Ogulnia* opened the colleges of augurs and pontiffs to plebeians, and in 287 BCE the *lex Hortensia* made plebiscites binding on the whole people.

**What the settlement did not do.** — The Conflict of the Orders ended with a new governing class, the *nobiles*, made up of the patrician and plebeian families that held the consulship. A handful of plebeian houses joined the elite; the mass of plebeians gained legal protection, the right to stand, and the tribunate as a route to office, but not a share in the decisions that mattered. That is the pattern the Gracchi would later expose: the machinery of the republic could admit new families to the top and protect citizens from arbitrary punishment, but it did not distribute land, money, or power.

## Citizen, ally, slave

**What citizenship meant.** — Roman citizenship (*civitas*) was a bundle of rights: the vote (*suffragium*), the right to marry a citizen (*conubium*), the right to own and transfer property under Roman law (*commercium*), and the right of appeal against a magistrate. Rome extended the citizenship in layers. Some communities held full citizenship; some held citizenship without the vote (*civitas sine suffragio*); the Latin towns held Latin rights, a middle status that let their leading men become Roman citizens by holding office; the colonies were planted as either Roman or Latin. To be an ally (*socius*) was to be outside all of this: the Italian allies supplied soldiers, sometimes more than Rome itself, and received no vote in return.

**The allies and the Social War.** — By the late second century the Italian allies bore a heavy share of the fighting and complained of arbitrary treatment by Roman magistrates, who could demand supplies, billets, and obedience without accountability. In 91 BCE a tribune, Marcus Livius Drusus, proposed to grant them citizenship; he was assassinated. The allies rose in revolt — the Social War, 91–87 BCE. They formed their own state, called Italia, with a capital at Corfinium renamed Italica, and fought Rome with Roman tactics. Rome won the battles but conceded the point: the *lex Julia* of 90 BCE gave citizenship to allies who had not revolted, and the *lex Plautia Papiria* of 89 extended it more widely. Within a generation the free population of Italy south of the Po was Roman.

**Slaves and the estates.** — The conquests brought captives by the hundred thousand. A slave was property, bought and sold like an ox; the great market at Delos was said to handle ten thousand a day. Slaves worked the mines of Spain and Macedonia, the households of the rich, the workshops, and, above all, the great estates. The *latifundia* — large farms of hundreds or thousands of acres, often worked by slave gangs — spread through Italy as small farmers sold out or were driven off. Displaced peasants drifted to Rome, where they became a volatile urban crowd dependent on the grain dole. Slave revolts broke out in Sicily in 135 and 104 BCE, and in 73 BCE Spartacus, a Thracian gladiator, led some seventy thousand escaped slaves across Italy; Crassus and Pompey crushed the revolt, and six thousand survivors were crucified along the Appian Way.

## The conquest of the Mediterranean

**The First Punic War (264–241 BCE).** — Rome's first war outside Italy was with Carthage, the Phoenician city on the coast of what is now Tunisia, over Messana in Sicily. Rome had no fleet, so it built one, using a captured Carthaginian ship as a model, and turned sea fighting into land fighting with a boarding bridge called the *corvus*. After more than twenty years, a Roman fleet under Gaius Lutatius Catulus destroyed the Carthaginian fleet at the Aegates Islands in 241 BCE. Carthage gave up Sicily, which became Rome's first province, and paid an indemnity; Rome seized Sardinia and Corsica three years later.

**Hannibal and the Second Punic War (218–201 BCE).** — Carthage rebuilt its power in Spain, and in 219 BCE Hannibal, its general, took the city of Saguntum, a Roman ally. In 218 he crossed the Alps with an army and a handful of surviving elephants and defeated the Romans at the Trebia. In 217 he destroyed a Roman army at Lake Trasimene, and in 216 at Cannae he encircled and killed perhaps fifty thousand Romans in a single day. Rome refused to make peace. Quintus Fabius Maximus avoided pitched battle and wore Hannibal down; Scipio Africanus took Spain, then carried the war to Africa and defeated Hannibal at Zama in 202 BCE. The peace of 201 stripped Carthage of Spain and its fleet and imposed a crushing indemnity.

**The Greek east and the destruction of Carthage.** — With Carthage humbled, Rome turned east. It defeated Philip V of Macedon at Cynoscephalae in 197 BCE, and in 196 proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks" at the Isthmus. It broke the power of Antiochus III of Syria at Thermopylae in 191 and Magnesia in 190. In 168 BCE Aemilius Paullus destroyed the Macedonian army at Pydna and abolished the kingdom; in 146, the same year Rome destroyed Corinth and made Greece a province, Scipio Aemilianus burned Carthage to the ground at the end of the Third Punic War. The senator Cato ended every speech with *Carthago delenda est*, "Carthage must be destroyed." In 133 BCE the king of Pergamon left his kingdom to Rome, which made it the province of Asia.

**How the provinces were run.** — The republic never designed an empire; it improvised. A province was governed by a proconsul or propraetor, a former magistrate whose imperium was extended for a year or more, assisted by a small staff and a quaestor. His jobs were to keep order, collect revenue, and hear cases, and he had almost no supervision while in office. Taxes were farmed out to *publicani*, contractors who paid the treasury in advance and collected what they could from the provincials; the profit lay in the gap. Provincial communities could sue a governor for extortion before a Roman court, but the courts were staffed by senators and the penalties were light. In 70 BCE Cicero prosecuted Gaius Verres, governor of Sicily, for plundering the province; Verres went into exile before the verdict.

## Empire at home

**The land question.** — Victory brought money, slaves, and grain into Italy, and the small farms that had supplied the legions could not compete. By the 130s BCE the soldier-farmer was disappearing, and the public land (*ager publicus*) once distributed to citizens was held in large blocks by the rich. In 133 BCE Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, a tribune of the plebs and grandson of Scipio Africanus, proposed to enforce an old limit of five hundred *iugera* (about three hundred acres) on public land and redistribute the surplus to the poor. The senate opposed him; his fellow tribune Marcus Octavius vetoed the bill; Gracchus had Octavius deposed by the assembly and the law passed. When he stood for a second tribunate, a mob led by the pontifex maximus Scipio Nasica beat him to death with clubs and stool legs and threw his body into the Tiber.

**Gaius Gracchus and the equestrians.** — Ten years later Gaius Gracchus, the younger brother, was elected tribune and pushed a wider programme: a law selling grain to the poor at a fixed low price, the transfer of the extortion courts from senators to equestrians (the wealthy non-senatorial class, including the tax farmers), new colonies, and citizenship for the Latin allies. He too was killed, in 121 BCE, with some three thousand supporters.

**Marius and the army.** — Gaius Marius was a "new man" from Arpinum who became consul in 107 BCE and was given the command in the Jugurthine War (111–105 BCE) against the Numidian king Jugurtha, whom his subordinate Sulla captured. Marius then defeated the Cimbri and Teutones at Aquae Sextiae in 102 and Vercellae in 101 BCE. His decisive change was to recruit the poor who owned no property (the *capite censi*) into the legions. These men had no farm to return to; they looked to their general for pay and for land when they retired. The legions became professional, and their loyalty ran to the man who led them rather than to the state that hired them.

**Sulla and the first march on Rome.** — In 91 BCE the Social War broke out. In 88 BCE the senate gave Sulla, then consul, the command against Mithridates VI of Pontus; Marius and a tribune, Sulpicius Rufus, had the command transferred to Marius. Sulla did what no Roman had done: he marched his army on Rome, drove his opponents out, and then left for the east. When he returned in 83 BCE, civil war followed; Sulla won at the Colline Gate in 82 BCE and became dictator. He posted lists of the proscribed, whose killing was rewarded and whose property was confiscated, and he had thousands killed. He doubled the senate to six hundred, reformed the courts and the cursus honorum, then in 79 BCE laid down the dictatorship and retired; he died the next year. He had shown that a Roman army could take the city, and that one man could remake the constitution.

**Pompey, Caesar, and the end.** — In the 60s BCE Gnaeus Pompeius, who had cleared the pirates from the Mediterranean in 67 and defeated Mithridates in the east, was the most powerful man in Rome; Marcus Licinius Crassus was the richest. In 60 BCE Pompey, Crassus, and Julius Caesar formed a private alliance, later called the First Triumvirate, to share the consulship and the provinces. Caesar, consul in 59 BCE, took command in Gaul and between 58 and 50 BCE conquered it, invaded Britain twice, and defeated the great revolt of Vercingetorix at Alesia in 52 BCE. Crassus was killed by the Parthians at Carrhae in 53 BCE, and Pompey and Caesar fell out. In 49 BCE the senate ordered Caesar to lay down his command; he crossed the Rubicon and began a civil war. He defeated Pompey at Pharsalus in 48 BCE; Pompey fled to Egypt and was killed. Caesar was made dictator, reformed the calendar and the senate, extended citizenship, and was assassinated on the Ides of March, 15 March 44 BCE, by senators led by Brutus and Cassius who believed they were saving the republic.

**After Caesar.** — The murder settled nothing. In 43 BCE Caesar's heir Octavian, Mark Antony, and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate, this time legally established, and they proscribed their enemies; Cicero was killed. Brutus and Cassius were defeated at Philippi in 42 BCE, and the empire was divided. Antony took the east and allied himself with Cleopatra VII of Egypt; Octavian took the west and turned Roman opinion against Antony. In 31 BCE Octavian's fleet defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium, and in 30 BCE they killed themselves as he took Alexandria. In 27 BCE he announced that he had restored the republic, returned his extraordinary powers to the senate and people, and accepted in return the name Augustus and a bundle of powers that made him, in fact, the ruler.

## Why the republic ended

**Polybius' answer, and its limits.** — Polybius, a Greek hostage who watched Rome in the second century BCE, thought the republic's strength lay in its mixed constitution: consuls, senate, and people each checked the others, so that no one part could dominate. He also believed that every constitution decays in a cycle, and that Rome would eventually follow. His account is a model of how institutions can produce stability — and a warning that they do so only while the balance holds. Later writers blamed moral decline, luxury, and the loss of the old discipline; Sallust made the fear of Carthage the thing that had kept Rome virtuous, so that victory itself brought corruption.

**Named arguments.** — Historians still disagree, and the disagreement is the point. Ronald Syme, in *The Roman Revolution* (1939), argued that the republic was never a balanced constitution but the rule of a narrow oligarchy, and that what fell was not liberty but one faction's monopoly of power; the "restoration" of 27 BCE was a revolution wearing the old words. Harriet Flower, in *Roman Republics* (2010), argued that there was never a single "Roman Republic" to fall: Rome had a succession of republics with different rules, and the regime Sulla built was already a new thing, so that the search for one cause of one collapse is misplaced. P. A. Brunt, in *Social Conflicts in the Roman Republic* (1971), put the strain on land, the allies, and the poor, and on a ruling class unwilling to share. What no serious account does is name a single cause: the size of the empire, the professional army, the failure to extend citizenship in time, the violence in the Forum, and the ambition of individuals all pressed at once, and at each crisis a different choice might have changed the outcome.

## Chronology

- **21 April 753 BCE** — Traditional date for the founding of Rome by Romulus.
- **509 BCE** — Traditional expulsion of the kings; the consulship begins.
- **494 BCE** — First plebeian secession; the tribunes of the plebs are created.
- **451–450 BCE** — The decemvirs publish the Twelve Tables.
- **367 BCE** — The Licinian-Sextian laws require one plebeian consul.
- **287 BCE** — The *lex Hortensia* makes plebiscites binding on all citizens.
- **264–241 BCE** — First Punic War; Sicily becomes the first province.
- **218–201 BCE** — Second Punic War; Hannibal crosses the Alps; Scipio wins at Zama.
- **146 BCE** — Carthage and Corinth are both destroyed.
- **133 BCE** — Tiberius Gracchus is tribune and is killed.
- **123–121 BCE** — Gaius Gracchus is tribune and is killed.
- **107 BCE** — Marius becomes consul and reforms army recruitment.
- **91–87 BCE** — The Social War; Rome grants citizenship to the Italian allies.
- **88 BCE** — Sulla marches on Rome.
- **82–79 BCE** — Sulla is dictator and remakes the constitution.
- **60 BCE** — Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus form the First Triumvirate.
- **49 BCE** — Caesar crosses the Rubicon; civil war begins.
- **44 BCE** — Caesar is assassinated on the Ides of March.
- **31 BCE** — Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra at Actium.
- **27 BCE** — Octavian takes the name Augustus and announces the restoration of the republic.

## Questions to carry

- The founding legends explain Rome's name and its destiny. What would count as evidence for or against them, and what does the archaeology actually show?
- What did *imperium* give a consul, and what limited it? Why did the limits matter more inside the city than in the field?
- Polybius called the constitution a balance of three parts. Who was actually represented in the assemblies, and who was not?
- Rome granted citizenship in layers — full, without the vote, Latin rights, alliance. What did that layering do to the Italian allies, and why did it fail in 91 BCE?
- The Gracchi were killed in the Forum. Was their programme a cause of the republic's fall, or a symptom of a strain already there?
- Marius recruited the poor into the legions, and Sulla marched on Rome. Did the army destroy the republic, or only reveal how much it depended on the men who commanded it?
- Syme, Flower, and Brunt give different accounts of the end. Which parts of the evidence does each explain best, and where do they conflict?
