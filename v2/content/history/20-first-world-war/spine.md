# The First World War

**Period:** c. 1914–1918 · **Region:** Europe and the wider world

## The shot at Sarajevo

**A Sunday in Bosnia.** — On the morning of 28 June 1914 the Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, rode through Sarajevo in an open car with his wife Sophie. The date was St Vitus's Day, and in the Serbian calendar the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, a defeat that Serbian nationalism had turned into a founding grievance. A bomb thrown earlier that morning had bounced off the car and exploded behind it, wounding bystanders; the Archduke had gone on to the town hall, complained about the reception, and then decided to visit the wounded in hospital. His driver, not told of the change of route, turned into Franz Josef Street and stopped. Gavrilo Princip, a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb, stepped forward and fired twice. Franz Ferdinand was hit in the neck, Sophie in the abdomen. Both died within minutes.

**Who fired, and who armed him.** — Princip belonged to Young Bosnia, a movement of students and young radicals who wanted Bosnia-Herzegovina, annexed by Austria-Hungary in 1908, joined to Serbia. The pistols and bombs came from the Black Hand, a secret society of Serbian army officers led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, who had already been involved in the murder of the Serbian king in 1903. The weapons reached Princip through Milan Ciganović and Major Vojislav Tankosić, and frontier officials helped move the assassins into Bosnia. How far the Serbian government knew of the plot remains disputed; the prime minister, Nikola Pašić, appears to have had some warning of a plan but not of its target or date. Austria-Hungary treated the assassination as proof that Belgrade was waging an undeclared war, and that the moment had come to settle accounts with Serbia.

**The question.** — The killing of one heir did not, by itself, require a European war. The question worth carrying through the next four years is this: how did a quarrel in the Balkans become a war that reached from the North Sea to the Caucasus and from Africa to the Pacific, and why did the fighting last more than four years rather than a few weeks? The answer lies in two things at once — the alliance system that pulled one power after another into the crisis, and the military and industrial facts that made the fighting, once begun, so hard to stop.

## The armed camps

**The Triple Alliance.** — After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, the German chancellor Otto von Bismarck built a system of alliances to keep France isolated. The League of the Three Emperors, with Austria-Hungary and Russia, collapsed in 1887 over rivalry in the Balkans; the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia lapsed in 1890, when the young Kaiser Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck. The Triple Alliance of 1882 bound Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy to aid one another if one was attacked by two or more powers. Italy would prove an unreliable partner and entered the war in 1915 on the Allied side. By 1914 the alliance was the cornerstone of German policy: Austria-Hungary was Germany's only dependable ally, and Germany had promised in 1909 to support it in a war with Serbia even if that war drew in Russia.

**The Triple Entente.** — On the other side stood a looser set of understandings rather than a formal alliance. France and Russia signed a military convention in 1892 and a formal alliance in 1894, agreeing that if either was attacked by Germany the other would mobilise. Britain, alarmed by Germany's naval building and by the kaiser's erratic diplomacy, settled its colonial quarrels with France in the Entente Cordiale of 1904 and with Russia in the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907. The Entente was not a treaty of alliance: Britain kept its freedom of action, and its foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, insisted in 1914 that Britain had no obligation to fight. But the staff talks between the British and French armies, begun in 1906, had gone further than most of the cabinet knew.

**The plans.** — Each power had a war plan that made rapid mobilisation the test of honour. The German plan, drawn up by Count Alfred von Schlieffen and modified by Helmuth von Moltke the Younger, aimed to knock out France in six weeks by swinging the main force through neutral Belgium and Luxembourg, then turn east against the slow-mobilising Russians. France had Plan XVII, an offensive into Alsace-Lorraine that assumed the moral superiority of the attack. Russia, whose railways were thin, planned to attack Germany and Austria-Hungary at the same time. The plans did not create the crisis, but they meant that once mobilisation began, the statesmen lost control of the timetable; to halt the trains was, in the language of the time, to surrender.

**The naval race.** — From 1898 Germany built a battle fleet to challenge the Royal Navy, and Britain answered with the Dreadnought in 1906 and a large building programme. The rivalry poisoned Anglo-German relations and pushed Britain toward France, even though the German fleet played only a small part in the land war.

**The Balkan wars.** — Between 1908 and 1913 the Ottoman Empire lost almost all its European territory. Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina in 1908. Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, and Montenegro drove the Ottomans out of Macedonia in the First Balkan War of 1912, then fell out over the spoils in the Second Balkan War of 1913. Serbia emerged roughly twice its former size and confident of its army; Austria-Hungary, fearing that a strong Serbia would attract its own South Slav subjects, was determined to cut it down. By 1914 the Balkans were where two empires and their great-power patrons confronted each other.

## The July Crisis

**The blank cheque.** — On 5 and 6 July 1914, Austria-Hungary sent a senior official to Berlin to ask what Germany would do if it moved against Serbia. The German reply, given by the kaiser and Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg, was that Austria-Hungary could count on Germany's full support, whatever the consequences. This "blank cheque" is central to the debate over responsibility: it encouraged Vienna to be harsh and removed the restraint Berlin might have exercised. On 23 July Austria-Hungary delivered its ultimatum to Belgrade.

**The ultimatum.** — The note blamed the Serbian government for tolerating a subversive movement, accused Serbian officers and officials of arming the assassins, and demanded ten things. Some were humiliating but possible: to suppress anti-Austrian publications, to dissolve the Narodna Odbrana, to purge anti-Austrian teachers. Others touched the essence of sovereignty: Serbian officials named by Vienna were to be dismissed, and Austrian organs were to take part in the judicial inquiry on Serbian soil. Serbia had 48 hours to reply. On 25 July Pašić's government accepted nine of the ten demands but refused to let Austrian officials conduct proceedings inside Serbia. Austria-Hungary called the reply unsatisfactory, and on 28 July, exactly one month after Sarajevo, it declared war.

**The cascade.** — What followed was the alliance system in motion. Russia, unwilling to see Serbia crushed and under pressure from its own nationalists, ordered partial mobilisation on 29 July and general mobilisation on 30 July. Germany demanded that Russia stop, then declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. On 4 August German armies crossed into Belgium, whose neutrality had been guaranteed by treaty in 1839; Britain declared war that night. By the middle of August the war had already spread beyond the continent: the British and French empires mobilised, and Japan, bound to Britain by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, declared war on Germany on 23 August. The Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers in late October. A Balkan quarrel had become a world war.

**Why the crisis ran away.** — Historians have proposed several explanations. Barbara Tuchman, in *The Guns of August* (1962), emphasised the mechanical logic of mobilisation timetables: the statesmen believed that the first to mobilise gained an advantage, so each step forced the next. Fritz Fischer, in *Germany's Aims in the First World War* (1961), argued that Germany deliberately used the crisis to seek European and world power, and bore the chief responsibility. Christopher Clark, in *The Sleepwalkers* (2012), argued that the crisis was a shared failure — a "tragedy, not a crime" — in which Serbian, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, French, and German decisions all contributed, each government acting in ignorance of the others' intentions. The debate is not settled. What is not in dispute is that the alliance commitments turned a local quarrel into a continental war, and that no war plan produced the quick victory it promised.

## The Western Front

**The Marne and the failure of the Schlieffen Plan.** — The German right wing swept through Belgium in August 1914, defeated the French at the frontiers, and by early September was within forty miles of Paris. Then the advance outran its supply lines, the German right was weakened by troops sent east to meet the Russian invasion, and the French commander Joseph Joffre, with the British Expeditionary Force, counter-attacked. In the First Battle of the Marne (5–12 September) the German armies fell back behind the Aisne. The "miracle of the Marne" — legend says Paris taxis carried reservists to the front — ended the hope of a quick victory. Both sides then tried to turn the other's flank in the "race to the sea," and by the end of 1914 a line of trenches ran from the North Sea to the Swiss frontier, moving only a few miles in either direction over the next four years.

**Trench warfare.** — The trench system was a response to firepower. Barbed wire, machine guns, and rapid-firing artillery made open movement suicidal; both sides dug in, and the line became a belt of trenches, dugouts, communication trenches, and shell-swept "no man's land" sometimes only fifty yards wide. Soldiers learned a routine of night digging, dawn "stand-to," rum and letters, and rotation between the front line, support line, and reserve. The strain produced "shell shock," the name then given to the tremors and paralysis of men worn down by bombardment. The trench was a zone, not a line, and the war was fought for the observation posts and high ground that dominated it.

**Verdun.** — In February 1916 the German chief of staff Erich von Falkenhayn chose the fortress complex at Verdun, a place of historic meaning to France, and attacked it not to capture it but to force the French to defend it with every man, "bleeding France white." The battle lasted from 21 February to December. The German capture of Fort Douaumont was a shock; the French commander Philippe Pétain organised the defence with the slogan *on ne passe pas* — "they shall not pass." By the end some 700,000 men on both sides had been killed or wounded, and the front had scarcely moved. Verdun became the emblem of a war of attrition in which the object was not ground but the enemy's army.

**The Somme.** — On 1 July 1916 the British and French attacked along the Somme. A week of bombardment was supposed to have cut the German wire and destroyed the defences; it had not. On the first day the British army suffered 57,470 casualties, of whom 19,240 were killed — the heaviest single day in its history. The battle dragged on until November, using tanks for the first time on 15 September 1916, and ended with about a million casualties and a gain of perhaps six miles. The Somme became, for Britain, the symbol of futile sacrifice.

**Passchendaele.** — The Third Battle of Ypres, from July to November 1917, was fought to break out of the Ypres salient toward the Belgian coast. Heavy shelling destroyed the drainage of the low-lying polders, and rain turned the battlefield into a sea of mud in which men and horses drowned. The British and Dominion forces finally took the ridge at Passchendaele in November, at a cost of some 500,000 casualties, for a few miles of ground. It is often taken as the point at which the "wearing-out" strategy had become an end in itself.

## The new technology

**The machine gun.** — Hiram Maxim's water-cooled gun, adopted by the British army as the Vickers, could fire about 500 rounds a minute; a few could stop a battalion. In 1914 some generals thought it a specialist weapon; by 1916 the machine gun and the artillery dominated the front. The German MG 08 and the British Vickers, dug in behind wire, made the frontal attack almost impossible.

**Gas.** — On 22 April 1915, at the Second Battle of Ypres, German forces released chlorine gas from cylinders and the wind carried it into French and Algerian trenches; thousands were gassed, and the gap in the line was not exploited. Both sides then developed gas shells and better masks. Phosgene and, from 1917, mustard gas, which burned the skin and lungs and lingered in the ground, became common. The chemist Fritz Haber, who developed the process for ammonia and later worked on poison gas, shows how the war mobilised science. Gas killed far fewer than artillery, but it terrorised and disabled, and the gas mask became a symbol of the front.

**The tank.** — The British developed the tank in secret, calling it a "water carrier," and first used the Mark I at the Somme on 15 September 1916. It was slow, mechanically unreliable, and few in number, but it could cross wire and trenches and was immune to machine-gun fire. At Cambrai in November 1917 massed tanks broke the German line without a preliminary bombardment, though the gain was not held. The tank did not win the war, but it showed a way past the stalemate that would be taken up in 1918.

**Aircraft.** — The aeroplane began as a reconnaissance machine, spotting artillery and observing enemy movement. By 1915 fighters such as the Fokker Eindecker, with a machine gun firing through the propeller, fought for control of the air. Germany sent Zeppelins over London from 1915 and Gotha bombers from 1917, killing civilians and tying down defence. Air power was still small in scale, but it made the war three-dimensional.

**Artillery.** — The greatest killer was artillery, not the machine gun or gas. Massed field guns and howitzers fired high-explosive and shrapnel shells; a barrage could churn a hillside into a moonscape and bury men alive. The "shell shortage" of 1915 forced Britain to build a Ministry of Munitions under Lloyd George and to mobilise industry. The war was, above all, a contest of industrial production: the side that could make more guns and shells would eventually prevail.

## The war at sea and the blockade

**The distant blockade.** — Britain's strategy rested on sea power: the Royal Navy did not need a great battle, only to close the exits from the North Sea and stop contraband reaching Germany. The "distant blockade," declared in 1914 and tightened over the following years, cut off food, fertiliser, and raw materials. Neutral ships were stopped and searched, which strained relations with the United States, but the blockade slowly starved German industry and cities. By 1917 the German diet had fallen back on turnips and ersatz bread in the "turnip winter" of 1916–17.

**The U-boat.** — Germany's answer was the submarine. Because a surfaced U-boat could be sunk by an armed merchantman, and because it carried too few torpedoes to give warning, Germany periodically waged unrestricted submarine warfare — sinking merchant ships without warning. On 7 May 1915 a U-boat sank the liner *Lusitania* off Ireland; 1,198 people died, including 128 Americans. The outcry forced Germany to promise, in the "Sussex pledge" of 1916, to stop sinking passenger ships without warning. The restraint held only while Germany judged that it could not win by other means.

**Jutland.** — The one great fleet action was the Battle of Jutland on 31 May–1 June 1916. The British Grand Fleet and the German High Seas Fleet fought in the North Sea; the British lost more ships and men, but the German fleet withdrew to port and never again challenged the blockade. Strategically it was a British victory, and the High Seas Fleet spent the rest of the war in harbour — until its sailors mutinied in 1918.

**Unrestricted warfare and American entry.** — In January 1917 the German leadership decided that unrestricted submarine warfare could starve Britain into surrender before the Americans could arrive in force. The gamble failed. On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed sinking without warning; on 3 February the United States severed diplomatic relations. On 1 March the American press published the Zimmermann telegram, a decoded message from the German foreign secretary offering Mexico the "lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona" if it would join Germany. On 2 April Wilson asked Congress to declare war; it did so on 6 April. American manpower, money, and shipping would tip the balance in 1918.

## The Eastern Front and the Russian collapse

**Tannenberg and the great retreat.** — The Eastern Front was more fluid than the west. In August 1914 two Russian armies invaded East Prussia, and the German commanders Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff destroyed one of them at Tannenberg (26–30 August), taking some 90,000 prisoners; the other was driven back at the Masurian Lakes. In 1915 a combined German and Austro-Hungarian offensive at Gorlice-Tarnów broke the Russian line, and the Russian army retreated hundreds of miles, giving up Poland, Lithuania, and much of the Baltic coast. The losses were enormous, and the Tsarist state never recovered its authority.

**The Brusilov Offensive.** — In June 1916 the Russian general Aleksei Brusilov attacked the Austro-Hungarians in Galicia with a short, intense bombardment and small assault groups, and broke their line. It was one of the great offensives of the war and drew German troops away from Verdun, but it cost Russia perhaps a million casualties and exhausted the army that had won it.

**Revolution.** — In March 1917 (February in the old calendar) bread riots in Petrograd turned into a general rising, the garrison joined the crowd, and Tsar Nicholas II abdicated. A Provisional Government under Prince Lvov and then Alexander Kerensky tried to continue the war, but the army was disintegrating and the peasants were seizing land. In November 1917 (October in the old calendar) Vladimir Lenin's Bolsheviks seized power and promised peace. An armistice came in December, and in March 1918 the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk gave Germany vast territory in the west of the former empire. Russia's exit freed German divisions for the Western Front, but it also released a revolutionary challenge that would shape the peace.

## The home fronts

**Total war.** — The war required the whole economy. Britain introduced conscription in January 1916; France and Germany already had it. Governments directed factories, fixed prices, and rationed food. In Britain the Defence of the Realm Act of 1914 gave the government sweeping powers over daily life, and the Ministry of Munitions under Lloyd George brought private firms under state direction. Women entered munitions works, transport, and offices in large numbers, and the war changed the argument for the vote; in 1918 the Representation of the People Act enfranchised women over thirty. The empire supplied troops on every front — Indian, Canadian, Australian, New Zealand, and South African — and colonial labourers did the unglamorous work behind the lines.

**Propaganda and the atrocity story.** — All governments censored news and produced propaganda. The German invasion of Belgium in 1914 brought real atrocities — the killing of civilians and the burning of towns such as Louvain — and these were used to mobilise opinion. The British "Bryce Report" of 1915 publicised German atrocities, some genuine and some exaggerated. Propaganda made the enemy monstrous and a negotiated peace harder to accept.

**The Armenian genocide.** — In 1915 the Ottoman government, fighting Russia in the Caucasus and fearing Armenian disloyalty, ordered the deportation of the Armenian population from eastern Anatolia. Armenians were driven on forced marches toward the deserts of Syria and Mesopotamia; many were massacred, and many died of hunger and thirst. The interior minister Talaat Pasha and other leaders of the Committee of Union and Progress directed the policy; estimates of the dead range from about 600,000 to more than a million. The word "genocide" was not coined until 1944, but it names the mass killing of a people because of their identity. How far the term applies, and how central the policy was, is debated by historians; what is not disputed is the scale of the suffering.

## The last year

**The spring offensive.** — With Russia out, Ludendorff launched a series of attacks in France in March 1918, aiming to split the British and French before American troops arrived in strength. Operation Michael broke the British line and advanced up to forty miles, the greatest German gain since 1914, but it outran its supplies, and the Allies appointed a single commander, Ferdinand Foch, to coordinate the defence. By July the German attacks had spent themselves.

**The hundred days.** — On 8 August 1918 the British, Canadian, and Australian forces attacked at Amiens with massed tanks and artillery; Ludendorff called it "the black day of the German Army." From then until November the Allies advanced steadily. The German army was short of reserves and food, its allies were collapsing — Bulgaria signed an armistice on 29 September, the Ottomans on 30 October, Austria-Hungary on 3 November — and the home front was in revolt. On 3 November sailors at Kiel mutinied rather than sail for a last battle; the revolt spread, and on 9 November the kaiser abdicated and a republic was proclaimed.

**The armistice.** — At 5 a.m. on 11 November 1918, in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, German and Allied representatives signed an armistice that took effect at 11 a.m. The fighting stopped. The terms left Germany disarmed and the blockade in place until a peace treaty was signed.

**The cost.** — The war killed about nine million soldiers and wounded some twenty-one million; civilian deaths from hunger and disease, including the influenza pandemic of 1918–19, were greater still. Four empires — the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman — fell or were dismantled, and new states were created from their ruins. The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, imposed territorial losses, disarmament, and reparations on Germany, and its Article 231 assigned responsibility for the war; it also created the League of Nations, which the United States refused to join. The peace was not generous enough to reconcile Germany nor harsh enough to cripple it, and the myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back" took root. The settlement did not make another war impossible; it helped to produce one.

## Chronology

- **28 June 1914** — Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie are assassinated at Sarajevo.
- **23 July 1914** — Austria-Hungary delivers its ultimatum to Serbia.
- **28 July 1914** — Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
- **1–4 August 1914** — Germany declares war on Russia and France; invades Belgium; Britain declares war.
- **23 August 1914** — Japan declares war on Germany.
- **5–12 September 1914** — First Battle of the Marne; the German advance is stopped.
- **22 April 1915** — Germany releases chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres.
- **7 May 1915** — A U-boat sinks the *Lusitania*; 1,198 die.
- **25 April 1915–9 January 1916** — The Gallipoli campaign fails.
- **21 February–18 December 1916** — The Battle of Verdun.
- **31 May–1 June 1916** — The Battle of Jutland.
- **1 July–18 November 1916** — The Battle of the Somme; 57,470 British casualties on the first day.
- **March and November 1917** — Revolution in Russia; the Tsar falls, then the Bolsheviks seize power.
- **2 April 1917** — Wilson asks Congress to declare war on Germany; Congress does so on 6 April.
- **31 July–10 November 1917** — Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele).
- **3 March 1918** — Treaty of Brest-Litovsk takes Russia out of the war.
- **21 March 1918** — Germany begins its spring offensive.
- **8 August 1918** — Battle of Amiens, the "black day" of the German army.
- **3–9 November 1918** — Mutiny at Kiel; the kaiser abdicates; a republic is proclaimed.
- **11 November 1918** — The armistice takes effect at 11 a.m.
- **28 June 1919** — The Treaty of Versailles is signed.

## Questions to carry

- Which mattered more in the July Crisis: the alliance system, the war plans, or the decisions of individual leaders?
- Why did the Western Front settle into a stalemate that neither side could break for four years?
- What did the war do to the authority of the old empires, and why did three of them not survive it?
- Can the killing of the Armenians be described as genocide, and what does the evidence allow us to say?
- Did the Treaty of Versailles make another war more likely, or is that judgment made with hindsight?
