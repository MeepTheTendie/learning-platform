# Reformation and religious wars

**Period:** c. 1517–1648 CE · **Region:** Germany · Switzerland · England · France

## The religion of late medieval Europe

**One church, many kingdoms.** — In 1500 western Europe shared one religious institution. The bishop of Rome governed the Latin Church through archbishops, bishops, canon law, and courts of its own, while kings ruled kingdoms; the two authorities overlapped everywhere — in the appointment of bishops, the taxation of clergy, the trial of clerks, and the regulation of marriage. **Jurisdiction** names exactly this: the right to exercise authority, to make and enforce law, and to judge. Church courts probed wills, marriages, contracts, and morals, and could fine or excommunicate. The great quarrel of the later Middle Ages was whether the pope's jurisdiction stood above the king's, beside it, or within it.

**The sacramental system.** — Ordinary religion centred on seven sacraments and above all the Mass, in which the priest re-presented Christ's sacrifice and the bread and wine became his body and blood. Baptism admitted a child to the community; penance restored the sinner after baptism. Penance had three parts: contrition, or true sorrow; confession to a priest; and satisfaction, the works imposed to make amends. That structure produced the practice that ignited the Reformation.

**Indulgences and purgatory.** — **An indulgence was the remission of the temporal penalty of sin** — not the guilt, and not eternal punishment, but the penance still owed after absolution. The church taught that most sinners died still owing such penalties and completed them in purgatory, a place of purifying fire whose duration could be measured in years. From the eleventh century popes claimed a treasury of merit — the surplus merits of Christ and the saints — from which they remitted those penalties, at first for crusaders, later for those who gave money to a holy cause. By 1500 the sale of indulgences was a large business, and its preachers promised more than the theology allowed. An anxious layman could easily conclude that a coin in the box bought a soul's release.

## Critics and the new press

**Humanism and the printing press.** — The Renaissance recovery of ancient languages reached theology through the humanists. Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam published a corrected Greek New Testament in 1516 and, in *The Praise of Folly* (1511), mocked the ignorance and worldliness of monks and theologians. Humanist method put the original text of Scripture, rather than the received Latin translation, at the centre of argument. Around 1450 Johannes Gutenberg, at Mainz, combined movable metal type, oil-based ink, and a screw press, and by 1500 presses were operating in more than two hundred European towns. Cheap print made argument public: a sermon preached in one city could be read in a dozen within a month. The press did not cause the Reformation, but the movement spread as it did because of it. Robert Scribner has shown how much of it was carried not by learned Latin but by woodcuts, broadsheets, and short German pamphlets.

## Luther and the indulgence controversy

**The monk at Wittenberg.** — Martin Luther, born at Eisleben in 1483, entered an Augustinian friary in 1505 and became a teacher of biblical theology at Wittenberg; by his own account he was a scrupulous and frightened man, unsure that his confessions were complete.

**The indulgence of 1515.** — Pope Leo X needed money to finish St Peter's Basilica. In 1515 he proclaimed a plenary indulgence for the archbishoprics of Magdeburg and Mainz, whose incumbent, Albrecht of Brandenburg, had borrowed heavily from the Fugger bank to pay Rome for his offices. Half the proceeds were to repay the Fuggers and half to build the basilica. The preaching fell to the Dominican friar Johann Tetzel, whose salesmanship became legendary: he was said to promise that a purchased indulgence freed a soul from purgatory the instant the coin struck the chest.

**The Ninety-Five Theses (1517).** — On 31 October 1517 Luther proposed a set of propositions for an academic disputation at Wittenberg on the power of indulgences. Written in Latin for theologians, it was an invitation to debate, not a manifesto; by his own later account Luther still assumed the pope would welcome a correction of abuses. The theses argue that repentance is a lifelong inward change; that the pope can remit only penalties he has himself imposed; that he cannot reach into purgatory by the power of the keys but only by intercession; and that the true treasure of the church is the Gospel, not pardons. "They preach man," Luther wrote of the vendors, "who say that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out of purgatory." Nothing yet denied the sacrament of penance or the pope's office. What made the theses explosive was print: within two months they had been printed in Wittenberg, Nürnberg, and Basel, and translated into German.

**Justification by faith.** — The deeper issue emerged as the controversy widened. **Justification is the act by which God declares a sinner righteous**; the question was how that declaration was secured. Luther came to hold that it was given freely, for Christ's sake, and received by faith alone, not earned by human cooperation, merits, or works. He called this the article on which the church stands or falls. His opponents held that faith formed by love — faith made active by works of charity infused into the soul — was what God accepted, so that works were not optional. If justification was a free gift, the apparatus of penances, satisfactions, indulgences, and monastic vows lost its necessity, and with it much of the power and income of the institutional church.

**The break with Rome.** — The dispute escalated. At the Leipzig disputation of 1519 Luther's opponent Johann Eck forced him to admit that some of Hus's condemned propositions were Christian, which put Luther outside the church's own judgment. In 1520 he published three treatises attacking the pope's authority, the sacramental system, and monastic vows. A papal bull, *Exsurge Domine*, threatened excommunication; Luther burned it in December 1520. In April 1521 he was summoned before the Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms, where he refused to recant unless shown error from Scripture. He was declared an outlaw, but the Saxon elector Frederick the Wise sheltered him, and the Edict of Worms was never enforced in the lands that protected him. While hidden in the Wartburg, Luther translated the New Testament into German; it appeared in 1522, and his complete Bible of 1534 shaped the language itself.

## The German Reformation and the common man

**Cities, princes, and the Peasants' War (1524–25).** — The Reformation spread through two channels: in the free cities of the south and the Rhineland, town councils, preachers, and guilds argued the new doctrine in public and often installed it by civic decision; in the princely territories, rulers saw in the break with Rome an opportunity to control the church within their borders — its lands, its courts, its appointments, and its taxes. The alliance of theology with territorial interest explains why reform succeeded where it captured a government. The most dangerous moment came from below. In 1524–25 peasants across the German south and centre rose against their lords, and in the *Twelve Articles* combined old grievances about serfdom, tithes, and common land with an appeal to the Gospel. The preacher Thomas Müntzer gave the revolt a radical religious voice. Luther was horrified by the violence and wrote a savage tract urging the princes to strike the rebels down. The revolt was crushed, perhaps a hundred thousand died, and the Reformation lost its most radical social edge. Peter Blickle has read the rising as "the revolution of the common man"; others see a more mixed bundle of local grievances. Either way, the massacre of 1525 bound the movement more tightly to the princes.

**Confession and league.** — The reforming territories organised for defence. In 1530 they presented the Augsburg Confession to Charles V, a moderate statement of faith drawn up by Philip Melanchthon that insisted the reformers taught nothing contrary to Scripture or the ancient church. When Charles rejected it and war threatened, the Protestant princes and cities formed the Schmalkaldic League. The emperor defeated the League in 1547, but the settlement was reversed within a few years, and in 1555 the Peace of Augsburg gave each territorial ruler the right to determine the religion of his lands. The principle, later compressed into the tag *cuius regio, eius religio*, recognised only the old church and the Augsburg confession; it left no room for the Reformed or the Anabaptists, and it set a time bomb for the next century.

**The Anabaptists.** — The most persecuted radicals rejected infant baptism and baptised adult believers instead. Scattered across Switzerland, south Germany, and the Low Countries, they were hunted by Catholics and Protestants alike; a militant wing seized Münster in 1534–35 and was destroyed after a brutal siege. The memory of Münster was used for generations to discredit every radical, yet the pacifist Anabaptists — Mennonites and later Hutterites — survived.

## Zwingli, Calvin, and the Swiss Reformation

**Zwingli in Zurich.** — Reform in the Swiss Confederation began in Zurich, where the priest Huldrych Zwingli, appointed to the Great Minster in 1519, preached through the New Testament in the vernacular and won the city council to the cause. Zwingli's theology was close to Luther's on justification but differed sharply on the Lord's Supper: where Luther held that Christ's body and blood were really present in the bread and wine, Zwingli read "This is my body" as a figure of speech and treated the Supper as a memorial and a public confession of faith. At the colloquy of Marburg in 1529 the two men failed to agree, and the split between Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism became permanent. Zwingli died in battle at Kappel in 1531.

**Calvin in Geneva.** — The leadership of the Reformed tradition passed to John Calvin, a French lawyer and humanist who fled persecution and settled in Geneva. His *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, first published in Latin in 1536 and enlarged through many editions, became the systematic statement of Reformed theology. Calvin taught that God had from eternity elected some to salvation and passed over others — predestination — and that the church should be governed by pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons rather than bishops. In Geneva he built, with the city council, a disciplinary regime enforced by the consistory, a court of pastors and lay elders that summoned the townspeople to account for their doctrine and morals. Geneva became a school for exiles, who carried Reformed ideas into France, the Netherlands, Scotland, England, and as far as Hungary and Poland. The execution of the anti-Trinitarian Michael Servetus in 1553 shows the limits of toleration on every side: Calvin approved it.

## The English Reformation

**The king's great matter.** — England's break with Rome began not with doctrine but with a marriage. Henry VIII, married since 1509 to Catherine of Aragon, needed a male heir and had fallen for Anne Boleyn. In 1527 he asked Pope Clement VII to annul the marriage; the pope, whose forces had just been humiliated by Catherine's nephew Charles V, refused. Henry's solution was to deny that the pope had ever had jurisdiction in England. The Reformation Parliament (1529–36) cut off appeals to Rome, and the Act of Supremacy of 1534 declared the king supreme head of the Church of England. Thomas Cromwell then dissolved the monasteries (1536–40), seizing their lands and wealth for the crown. Doctrine changed cautiously; what changed decisively was jurisdiction.

**Doctrine under Edward VI and Mary.** — Protestant doctrine arrived properly only in the reign of Henry's young son Edward VI (1547–53), under Archbishop Thomas Cranmer. Cranmer's *Book of Common Prayer* of 1549, revised in 1552, replaced the Latin services with an English liturgy, and articles and homilies taught justification by faith. Edward died in 1553 and his Catholic half-sister Mary I restored the Roman obedience and burned some three hundred Protestants, including Cranmer, at the stake. The burnings created a durable Protestant martyrology, above all John Foxe's *Actes and Monuments*, which taught English readers for generations that true religion suffered under a persecuting church.

**Elizabeth and the via media.** — Mary's death in 1558 brought her Protestant half-sister Elizabeth to the throne, and Elizabeth settled the church by law: the Act of Supremacy of 1559 restored the royal headship, and the Act of Uniformity imposed a revised *Book of Common Prayer* on every parish. The settlement was deliberately broad — the **via media**, the middle way — keeping bishops and liturgy while teaching Protestant doctrine. It satisfied neither the Catholics who wanted Rome nor the Puritans who wanted a thorough reform, and the tension between them shaped English politics for a century. Historians disagree about what the settlement tells us. A. G. Dickens argued that England was already becoming Protestant and the state ratified a popular movement; Christopher Haigh argued that the Reformation was slow, contested, and imposed from above on a people still attached to traditional religion. The debate turns on how the surviving evidence — wills, churchwardens' accounts, recusancy fines — is read.

## Catholic reform, the Jesuits, and Trent

**The Society of Jesus.** — The most important new order was founded by Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque soldier wounded in battle whose *Spiritual Exercises* set out a programme of prayer, examination of conscience, and discernment of God's will. The Society of Jesus received papal approval in 1540 and grew rapidly. Jesuits took vows of special obedience to the pope, were sent wherever the church needed them, and built a system of schools that shaped Catholic elites for centuries; they were also prominent as missionaries in Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

**Trent, the Index, and confessionalisation.** — The Council of Trent met in three sessions over eighteen years and answered the Protestant challenge by fixing Catholic doctrine: Scripture and tradition together as the rule of faith; justification as a process involving both grace and human cooperation; seven sacraments; the real presence of Christ in the Mass; purgatory, indulgences, and the veneration of saints and images all affirmed. It also required bishops to reside in their sees, forbade the sale of church offices, and established seminaries to train parish clergy. The reformed papacy built machinery of enforcement: the Roman Inquisition, reorganised in 1542, tried heresy, and the Index of Prohibited Books, first issued in 1559, listed works Catholics might not read. Both sides, Protestant and Catholic, were constructing institutions to define, teach, and police belief — a process historians call confessionalisation.

## The French wars of religion

**A kingdom divided and the wars begin (1562).** — France was the largest and most Catholic kingdom in western Europe, but Calvinist churches spread rapidly among the nobility, the towns, and the literate, until perhaps a tenth of the population was Protestant. The Huguenots, as they were called, were led by noble houses — the Bourbons and the Châtillons — while the Catholic party was led by the Guises, who had ties to Spain and to the papal court. Mack Holt and others stress that these were not only wars of belief: noble clientage, faction, and the control of the king's person were inseparable from the confessional struggle. The crown was weak: Henry II had died in 1559, and his widow Catherine de' Medici governed for her young sons. An edict of toleration was met in 1562 with the massacre of a Protestant congregation at Wassy, and eight civil wars followed over the next thirty-six years. The worst single atrocity came in 1572. After the marriage of the Protestant Henry of Navarre to the king's sister, Catherine and the Guises ordered the killing of the Huguenot leader Admiral Coligny, and the killing spread through Paris and the provinces. Thousands died. The St Bartholomew's Day massacre destroyed the Huguenots' political leadership and their trust in the crown, and it hardened the war on both sides.

**Resistance theory and the Edict of Nantes.** — The massacre pushed Protestant writers toward a new political argument. If a king could turn murderer, what were subjects to do? In treatises such as François Hotman's *Francogallia* and the anonymous *Vindiciae contra tyrannos*, Huguenot authors argued that inferior magistrates — nobles, town councils, estates — had a duty to resist a tyrant and could even depose him. Quentin Skinner has shown how the Reformation's disputes over who had the right to interpret Scripture produced, on all sides, a durable vocabulary of resistance and, in reaction, a stronger claim by the state to control religion. The wars ended only when the Protestant heir Henry of Navarre became King Henry IV, converted to Catholicism, and issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598. The edict granted the Huguenots liberty of conscience, limited public worship in specified places, access to offices, and a number of fortified towns. It was a settlement imposed by a king, revocable by a later king, and revoked in 1685. The **politiques**, who put civil peace above confessional victory, had won a truce rather than a principle.

## The Dutch revolt

**Philip II and the Low Countries.** — The seventeen provinces of the Netherlands passed to Philip II of Spain in 1555. Philip wanted uniform religion and efficient taxation; the provinces were prosperous, self-governing, and jealous of their privileges. When he reorganised the bishoprics, extended the Inquisition, and taxed trade, the towns and nobles resisted, and in 1566 crowds stripped the churches of images in the Iconoclastic Fury. Philip sent the Duke of Alba, whose Council of Troubles executed more than a thousand people; resistance found a leader in William of Orange, and the rebels seized the port of Brielle in 1572. In 1579 the northern provinces formed the Union of Utrecht, and in 1581 they renounced Philip's rule, while the southern provinces remained under Spanish rule. The Dutch Republic that emerged was a commercial power whose ships and banks made Amsterdam a European financial centre, and in practice a state with a privileged Reformed church and a large Catholic, Mennonite, and Jewish population tolerated to varying degrees.

## The Thirty Years' War

**Bohemia, 1618.** — The war began in the Habsburg lands. When the Bohemian estates, largely Protestant, defied the new king Ferdinand and threw his officials from a window of Prague castle in the Defenestration of 1618, they set off a revolt that the Habsburgs crushed at the battle of White Mountain in 1620. What might have remained a local conflict widened because the Empire's constitution was already brittle and every neighbouring power had something to gain.

**The war widens and its costs.** — The Danish king Christian IV intervened in 1625 and was driven back; the emperor's general Albrecht von Wallenstein built an army that lived off the land. In 1630 Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden landed with a modern army and a Protestant cause; he won great victories and was killed at Lützen in 1632. After 1635 France, under Cardinal Richelieu, entered openly on the Protestant side, though France was Catholic, and the war was now as much a struggle for power as a war of religion. The human cost was enormous: armies foraged and plundered, towns were sacked, and populations in parts of Germany fell by a third or more. Peter H. Wilson has argued that the war is best understood as a constitutional crisis of the Empire and a contest of dynasties in which confession was one resource among many; Geoffrey Parker and others give more weight to religious conviction. The disagreement turns on how the decisions of rulers are to be explained.

## The Peace of Westphalia and what changed

**The settlement.** — The treaties signed at Münster and Osnabrück in 1648 rested on a general amnesty and the restoration of lands and rights, and on a religious settlement whose baseline was the situation of 1 January 1624 — the so-called normative year. The three confessions recognised in the Empire — Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed — were to coexist, and princes who changed their own religion could no longer force their subjects to follow, though subjects who would not conform could be required to emigrate. The estates of the Empire were confirmed in their territorial rights, and the treaty was declared a perpetual fundamental law.

**Sovereignty and its limits.** — The settlement is often said to have created the modern system of sovereign states, each free to determine its own religion and its own affairs. That is an exaggeration: the Empire survived as a body with courts and a diet, and the idea of an international order of equal sovereigns is a later construction. What the treaties did establish was that the confessional map of central Europe would be settled by law and negotiation. The word **jurisdiction** — the right to judge and rule — had been redefined: it now belonged, in the first place, to the territorial prince, and it was exercised over a defined and legally fixed religious landscape.

**Everyday institutions and contingency.** — Princes and cities built territorial churches with their own superintendents and consistories, regulated marriage and morality through church courts, took over schools, hospitals, and poor relief, and recorded baptisms, marriages, and burials in parish registers with new regularity. The result, across confessions, was a more intrusive and better-documented state, and a more disciplined and literate population — the process historians call confessionalisation. Robert Scribner and others have insisted that this was not simply imposed: ordinary people used the new religion, argued about it, and shaped it in their own parishes. None of it was foreordained. Had Charles V won a decisive victory in the 1540s, or had the French crown held together, or had Gustavus Adolphus lived, the map might have been drawn differently. The Reformation survived because it found protectors, because print made it impossible to suppress, and because the institutional church had real grievances that reform could not contain.

## Chronology

- **1517** — Luther proposes the Ninety-Five Theses at Wittenberg; they spread in print within weeks.
- **1519** — The Leipzig disputation; Luther is driven to deny the authority of popes and councils.
- **1520** — Luther publishes his three great reforming treatises and burns the papal bull.
- **1521** — Diet of Worms; Luther refuses to recant and is declared an outlaw.
- **1522** — Luther's German New Testament is published.
- **1524–25** — The German Peasants' War; the revolt is crushed.
- **1529** — The colloquy of Marburg fails to unite Luther and Zwingli.
- **1530** — The Augsburg Confession is presented to Charles V.
- **1531** — Zwingli is killed at Kappel; the Schmalkaldic League is formed.
- **1534** — The Act of Supremacy makes the English king head of the church.
- **1536** — Calvin publishes the first edition of the *Institutes* and settles in Geneva.
- **1540** — The Society of Jesus is approved by the pope.
- **1545–63** — The Council of Trent defines Catholic doctrine and reforms the church.
- **1549** — Cranmer's *Book of Common Prayer* is imposed in England.
- **1555** — The Peace of Augsburg gives each prince the religion of his territory.
- **1559** — Elizabeth I's settlement restores the royal supremacy and a revised prayer book.
- **1562–98** — The French wars of religion.
- **1572** — The St Bartholomew's Day massacre in Paris and the provinces.
- **1579** — The Union of Utrecht; the Dutch Republic takes shape.
- **1598** — The Edict of Nantes grants the Huguenots limited rights.
- **1618** — The Defenestration of Prague begins the Thirty Years' War.
- **1620** — The battle of White Mountain crushes the Bohemian revolt.
- **1630–32** — Gustavus Adolphus intervenes; he is killed at Lützen.
- **1648** — The Peace of Westphalia ends the war in the Empire.

## Questions to carry

- Luther's theses were an academic proposal to debate. What turned them into a movement, and how much of that was doctrine and how much was print, politics, and protection?
- The Ninety-Five Theses and the Augsburg Confession both insist the reformers attacked no one's lawful authority. Where exactly do they draw the line between the church's power and the sword, and who is left to judge when the two disagree?
- An indulgence remitted a penalty, not guilt. Why did a technical distinction about penalties become a crisis about who could forgive sins at all?
- The English Reformation began with a king's marriage and ended with a prayer book. Was England's Reformation primarily a matter of state, or of belief? How would Dickens and Haigh answer, and what evidence would each use?
- The Edict of Nantes and the Peace of Westphalia both settled religious conflict by law. What did each grant, to whom, and at whose discretion?
- The Thirty Years' War began over confession and ended over territory. Does that make religion the cause or only the occasion?
- Which was the greater break with the medieval past: the doctrine of justification by faith, or the transfer of jurisdiction over the church to princes and kings?
