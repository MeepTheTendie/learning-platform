# The Greek city-state

**Period:** c. 800–500 BCE · **Region:** Aegean and the Mediterranean

## The land and the sea

**A country of small plains.** — Greece is a peninsula of mountains. The Pindus range runs down its spine, and its ridges divide the land into pockets: Attica, Boeotia, the Argolid, Laconia, Messenia, Arcadia, Thessaly. Each has its own small plain, its own water, and its own way to the sea. There is almost no navigable river, and arable land is a small fraction of the whole, coming in strips and basins rather than in one great irrigated flat. A single valley could feed a few thousand people and not many more. Overland travel is slow and hard, especially in winter; a day's sail along the coast is easier than a week's walk over a pass. So the Greek communities looked outward to the sea far more than they looked across the mountains to one another.

**A sea with many shores.** — The Aegean is not an open ocean but a basin crowded with islands, and on its eastern side it meets the coast of Anatolia, where Greeks settled in the region they called Ionia. Beyond it the Greek world reached west to Sicily and southern Italy, north through the Hellespont into the Black Sea, south to Cyrene in Libya, and far west to Massalia, modern Marseille. Plato compared the Greeks to frogs sitting around a pond: a single culture scattered around one sea, held together by water and divided by land.

**Why it did not unify.** — Mesopotamia and Egypt each had a great river and a broad plain that one power could seize and tax. Greece had neither. After the Mycenaean palaces burned around 1200 BCE, no state ever reunited the Aegean. To conquer all Greece meant fighting through mountains, ferrying armies between islands, and holding valleys that could each close their own passes. To keep such a conquest was harder still. Geography did not make unity impossible, but it made it expensive, and no archaic power was rich enough to try for long. The political unit that grew in the gap was small, local, and fiercely independent: the *polis*.

## The polis and its citizens

**What a polis was.** — The word *polis* means both a town and a citizen community, and the second sense matters more. A polis was not merely walls and houses. It was a body of citizens who governed themselves under their own laws, worshipped their own gods at their own shrines, and marched under their own commanders. Its physical parts had names: the *asty*, the built-up town; the *chora*, the surrounding farmland that fed it; the *acropolis*, the high fortified point, often the oldest sanctuary; and the *agora*, the open square where the assembly met, goods were sold, and disputes were heard. There were hundreds of poleis, and they varied enormously. Athens, with Attica behind it, was among the largest. Many were tiny, with a few hundred citizens and a single town. Each regarded itself as sovereign, and few were willing to surrender that sovereignty to anyone else.

**Who counted.** — Citizenship was narrow, and it is the first thing to establish before talking about Greek politics. The citizen body consisted of adult men of citizen descent. Women had no vote and no place in the assembly, though they held important roles in religion, controlled property through dowry and inheritance in some cities, and were central to the household and to the transmission of citizenship. Slaves — people owned as property, bought, sold, and inherited — had no legal standing at all; most households of any means kept some, and they worked in fields, workshops, mines, and homes. *Metics* were free resident foreigners: they paid a special tax, served in the army, and often grew rich, but they could not own land or hold office in most poleis, and they could not vote. So when a Greek source says "the Athenians decided," it means a small fraction of the people who lived in Attica. The *demos*, the word for the people, is a slippery term: sometimes it means the whole citizen body, and sometimes it means specifically the common citizens as opposed to the rich and well born.

**Assembly, council, and magistrates.** — A typical polis had an assembly of citizens (*ekklesia*), a smaller council that prepared business (*boule*), and annual magistrates drawn from the leading families. Not every polis was democratic, and most were not. Many were *oligarchies* — governments in which a few wealthy or well-born families held the offices and set the rules, with the assembly reduced to a rubber stamp or excluded altogether. The extreme form of one-man rule the Greeks called *tyranny*: a man who seized power without a lawful claim, whether by a coup, a foreign alliance, or a bodyguard of his own. The word *tyrannos* was borrowed from a non-Greek language, probably Lydian, and it did not originally mean a cruel ruler — it meant a ruler who had no constitutional right to rule. A tyrant might govern well, patronise the arts, and be popular. What made him a tyrant was how he got there. Only later did the word acquire the sense of despotism it carries now.

**The alphabet from Phoenicia.** — Writing returned to Greece in the eighth century after a long gap, and it came from the sea. Phoenician merchants, whose homeland was the coast of modern Lebanon, used a script of about twenty-two signs, each standing for a consonant. Greek speakers took those signs and did something decisive: they reassigned a handful of signs that stood for sounds Greek did not use, and made them stand for vowels. With consonants and vowels both written, almost any Greek sentence could be spelled out. The earliest surviving Greek inscriptions date from about 750 to 700 BCE. This alphabet was not the property of a scribal class, as cuneiform and Egyptian writing had been; it was simple enough to be learned by many people, and Greeks wrote on pottery, stone, metal, and later papyrus. Law codes, poems, dedications, curses, and private letters all become possible on a new scale.

## Homer and Hesiod

**The poems.** — The two great epics, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*, were attributed in antiquity to a poet named Homer, about whom nothing certain is known. They tell of the Trojan War and its aftermath, but they were composed orally, in a formulaic style built for recitation, and they reached something like their present form around 750–700 BCE, centuries after the events they claim to describe. Hesiod, a Boeotian farmer who names himself in his poems, wrote the *Theogony*, an account of the birth of the gods, and the *Works and Days*, a poem of farming, justice, and hard luck. Together these are the oldest Greek literature and the most important window onto the values of the early polis.

**As evidence and as problems.** — Homer and Hesiod are not history. Their heroes belong to a legendary age, and their poems mix customs from different centuries. But they are superb evidence for what their audiences admired and feared. In Homer the highest goods are *timē* (honour, the respect one is owed) and *kleos* (fame that survives death); the poem turns on the rage of Achilles when his honour is taken from him. Guests and hosts are bound by *xenia*, guest-friendship, a network of gift and obligation that linked aristocratic houses across the Greek world. Hesiod gives the other side: the small farmer who must work, the "gift-eating kings" who give crooked judgments, and a world in which the powerful can crush the weak. The historian must treat these poems as evidence of a mental world, not of events.

## The archaic world

**Aristocracies and the first constitutions.** — In the eighth and seventh centuries most poleis were dominated by aristocratic families, the *gene*, who claimed descent from heroes and gods, held the best land, and controlled the major priesthoods. They met in councils, judged disputes, and filled the annual magistracies. Below them stood small farmers, craftsmen, and labourers. The economy was changing. Population grew after the long slump of the Dark Age; trade with the eastern Mediterranean and with the western colonies expanded; and by about 600 BCE the kingdom of Lydia in Anatolia was striking the first coins, a technology that spread quickly through the Greek world. Money made wealth movable and made new men rich, which unsettled the old assumption that birth alone entitled a family to rule.

**The hoplite phalanx.** — Greek warfare changed in the same centuries. By about 700 BCE the decisive arm was no longer the chariot or the single champion but the hoplite: a heavy infantryman with a bronze helmet, breastplate, and greaves, a long thrusting spear, and, above all, a large round shield, the *aspis*, held on the left arm. Hoplites fought packed in a line, each man's shield covering the man to his left, pushing against the enemy as much as stabbing. The formation demanded discipline and trust rather than individual glory. The men who could afford the armour were usually the middling farmers, and the phalanx gave them a new sense of their own importance. It is a plausible link — though historians argue over how tight it is — between the rise of massed infantry and the demand of ordinary citizens for a share in decisions.

**Tyrants.** — The seventh and sixth centuries were the age of the tyrants. Cypselus seized Corinth around 657 BCE and his son Periander ruled after him; Orthagoras and his descendant Cleisthenes ruled Sicyon; Theagenes took Megara; Polycrates ruled Samos with a navy; and at Athens Peisistratus seized power in 561/0. These men were usually aristocrats who turned against their own class. They often had the support of the *demos* and of newly rich families shut out of office, and they weakened the old aristocratic councils by appointing magistrates loyal to themselves. Many were patrons: they built temples, water supplies, and public buildings, founded festivals, and sponsored poets. Others were brutal. The pattern was common enough that tyranny came to seem a stage through which many poleis passed, but it was never a program: each tyrant improvised, and most dynasties fell within two or three generations.

**Colonisation and the sea routes.** — Between about 750 and 550 BCE Greeks founded settlements all around the Mediterranean and Black Sea. The causes were several and are debated: land hunger among farmers who divided inheritance among many sons, famine and drought, the search for metal and trade, and political defeat at home, when the losing faction of a *stasis* — a factional quarrel — left to found a new city rather than submit. The community consulted Delphi, chose a founder called the *oikist*, and carried fire from the mother city's hearth to light the new one. The settlement, the *apoikia*, was independent of its mother city in law, but kept the same gods, calendar, and dialect. Corinthians founded Syracuse in 733; Megara founded Byzantium; Miletus filled the Black Sea coast; Thera founded Cyrene. The result was a Greek world stretched from Spain to the Crimea, bound by language, cult, and trade, and made up of hundreds of separate communities.

## Sparta

**Lycurgus and the problem of the sources.** — The Spartans attributed their whole way of life to a single lawgiver, Lycurgus, who was said to have given them their constitution after consulting the oracle at Delphi. The problem is that we cannot date him, and the ancients could not agree about him either. Herodotus, Xenophon, and Aristotle all mention him, but the fullest account, in Plutarch's *Life of Lycurgus*, was written some eight hundred years after the supposed reform, and Plutarch himself opens by admitting that almost nothing about Lycurgus is agreed. There is no contemporary evidence and no inscription. The safest reading is that the Spartan system grew up over the seventh and sixth centuries, above all in response to the conquest of Messenia, and that "Lycurgus" is the name later generations gave to its imagined author. He belongs to legend; the institutions belong to history.

**The helots and the conquest of Messenia.** — Sparta's neighbours to the west, the Messenians, were conquered in two long wars, the first perhaps around 735–715 BCE and the second around 650–620. Their land was taken and their people reduced to *helots*: enslaved, tied to the soil, and required to hand over a fixed share of what they produced to the Spartan masters who owned them. Helots were not private property in the ordinary sense; they belonged to the community and could not be sold abroad. They greatly outnumbered the Spartans, and the fear of a general rising shaped Spartan life. Between the Spartans and the helots stood the *perioikoi*, "those who live around," free but not citizens, who farmed, mined, and made the goods the Spartans disdained. Once a year the ephors formally declared war on the helots, so that killing one would not pollute the killer; and a secret police of young men, the *krypteia*, is said to have hunted helots in the countryside at night.

**The agoge.** — Spartan male citizens were made by a state system of education, the *agoge*. At about seven a boy left his family and was enrolled in a herd (*agelē*) under an older boy as captain and an adult official as supervisor. The training aimed at obedience, endurance, and military skill. Boys went barefoot, wore one cloak a year, slept on rushes, and were deliberately underfed so that they would learn to steal — and were whipped if caught stealing clumsily. They were expected to answer questions briefly and to the point, which is why we still call such speech laconic. At the altar of Artemis Orthia they were flogged, and Plutarch says he saw youths die under the lash. The purpose was not learning but the making of soldiers who would hold the line and never leave it.

**The gerousia, the ephors, and the kings.** — Sparta had two kings, drawn from two royal houses, the Agiads and the Eurypontids. Their power was real in war and religion but limited at home. The *gerousia*, the council of elders, consisted of twenty-eight men over sixty plus the two kings, and it prepared business for the assembly and could block decisions it disliked. The assembly of all citizens voted, but by acclamation rather than by count, and it could only accept or reject what was put to it. The most powerful officials were the five *ephors*, elected annually from the citizen body, who supervised the kings, managed foreign policy, and oversaw the agoge. Later Greek writers admired this "mixed constitution" as a balance of monarchy, oligarchy, and democracy. It was also a closed system: the citizen body of full Spartans, the *homoioi* or "equals," was a small and shrinking elite living off helot labour.

**The Spartan image.** — Spartans were famous for austerity and endurance, and later writers, especially Athenians, loved to contrast their stern virtue with Athenian luxury. Modern historians call this the "Spartan mirage": a picture built largely by outsiders, which says as much about the observers as about the city. The equality of the "equals" rested on the slavery of the helots, and Spartan women, who managed estates and trained in athletics, enjoyed freedoms unusual elsewhere. What made Sparta strange was how far it went to keep its citizens equal, and whom it enslaved to pay for it.

## Athens

**Before Solon.** — Athens in the early archaic period was ruled by a board of nine annual magistrates, the *archons*, chosen from the leading families, with a council of former archons, the Areopagus, watching over the laws. The kingship had faded into a religious office. Around 632 BCE a young aristocrat named Cylon tried to make himself tyrant by seizing the Acropolis; the plot failed, and his followers were killed, a killing the family of the Alcmaeonids was accused of for generations. About 621 Draco was given the task of writing down the laws. His code was notoriously severe — later Greeks said it was written in blood — and Solon repealed all of it except the rules on homicide. The deeper crisis was economic. Poor farmers who could not repay their loans were reduced to *hektēmoroi*, "sixth-parters," paying a sixth of their produce to a rich creditor, or else were sold into slavery for debt, sometimes abroad. The land was falling into the hands of a few families, and the poor were ready to rise.

**Solon's reforms and his poems.** — In 594/3 BCE, with the city on the edge of civil war, the Athenians appointed Solon, a well-born but middling man, as mediator and archon, and gave him the whole constitution to reshape. His first act was the *seisachtheia*, the "shaking off of burdens": he cancelled debts, forbade loans secured on a person, and brought back Athenians who had been enslaved for debt. He then reorganised the citizen body by property rather than birth. The richest class, the *pentacosiomedimnoi*, were those whose land produced five hundred measures of grain, oil, or wine; then came the *hippeis*, able to keep a horse, at three hundred; then the *zeugitai*, at two hundred; and below them the *thetes*, who had little or nothing. The higher offices were reserved to the top three classes, but the thetes gained the right to sit in the assembly and to serve on the law courts, which Solon opened to all citizens. He created a Council of Four Hundred to prepare business and left the Areopagus as guardian of the laws.

What makes Solon unusual is that we can hear his own voice. He wrote poems about his work, and later writers, above all Aristotle in the *Constitution of Athens*, quote them as evidence. In one he says that he stands between the two sides, "a shield turned to both," refusing to let either side win everything; in another he warns the rich not to be greedy. He also claims, and later tradition agreed, that he could have made himself tyrant and refused. His poems are the earliest surviving political speech by an Athenian, and they are a reminder that our picture of the crisis comes partly from the man who claimed to solve it. Solon's settlement did not end the quarrel; it bought time, and within a generation the factions were fighting again.

**The tyranny of Peisistratus.** — After Solon, Athenian politics divided into three factions, roughly of the plain, the coast, and the hills, and out of the confusion Peisistratus, a relative of Solon, seized the Acropolis with a bodyguard in 561/0 BCE. He was expelled twice and returned, finally ruling from 546 to his death in 528/7. He was a tyrant in the technical sense, but he governed in a way that many Athenians remembered kindly. He lent money to farmers, sent judges travelling into the countryside to hear cases, and founded or expanded festivals, including the Panathenaea and the City Dionysia, at which tragedy was first performed. His sons Hippias and Hipparchus inherited the rule. In 514 Hipparchus was assassinated by Harmodius and Aristogeiton, who became the most famous tyrannicides in Athens, and in 510 the Spartans, called in by the Alcmaeonids and the Delphic oracle, helped drive Hippias out.

**Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7.** — With the tyranny gone, two men contended for power: Isagoras, backed by the Spartans, and Cleisthenes, an Alcmaeonid. When Cleisthenes saw he was losing, Herodotus says, he "took the people into his party," and the move transformed Athens. He replaced the four old tribes, which were based on descent, with ten new tribes based on where a man lived. He made the *deme*, the local village or city ward, the basic unit of citizenship: a man was now registered as an Athenian through his deme, and the demes kept the citizen rolls. He grouped the demes into thirty *trittyes* — thirds — drawn from the city, the coast, and the inland, and mixed them into the ten tribes, so that no tribe was simply a regional faction. Each tribe sent fifty men by lot to a new Council of Five Hundred, which set the assembly's agenda for a year. The tribal army was reorganised on the same lines. The word used for the new order was *isonomia*, equality before the law — not yet the word *demokratia*, but the thing to which it would later be attached.

The reforms did not make Athens a democracy in the modern sense. Women, slaves, and metics remained outside the citizen body, and many offices were still restricted by wealth. But they widened the circle of those who counted, broke the grip of the old kinship tribes on power, and gave ordinary citizens a permanent institutional voice. They were also not inevitable. Cleisthenes acted to win a faction fight, and if Isagoras had won, Athens might have become an oligarchy under Spartan protection. The outcome was contingent, and the Athenians knew it: within a few years they were at war with Sparta and sending envoys to the Persian governor at Sardis to ask for help.

## A shared culture, many states

**What the Greeks had in common.** — For all their separateness, the Greeks knew they were one people. They spoke dialects of a single language; they worshipped the same gods under local names and at shared sanctuaries such as Delphi and Olympia; they told the same stories about heroes and gods; they sent athletes to the same games and used the same alphabet. They called themselves *Hellenes* and everyone else *barbaroi*, people whose speech sounded like "bar-bar." Herodotus says the Athenians defined Greekness by shared blood, language, shrines and sacrifices, and customs. But these ties produced no common government: no Greek empire, no single law, no standing league. The great sanctuaries and games were places where Greeks met as Greeks, not a state.

**The coming of Persia.** — In 546 BCE Cyrus of Persia conquered Croesus of Lydia, and with Lydia the Greek cities of Ionia passed under Persian rule. In 499 they rose in revolt, and in 498 the Athenians sent twenty ships to help them burn Sardis — a small act with large consequences. The revolt failed, but it brought the Persian empire into conflict with the Greek mainland. The tension between a shared Greek culture and a world of separate political communities was not resolved in this period; it was the problem the next one inherited.

## Chronology

- **c. 1200 BCE** — The Mycenaean palaces fall; writing is lost; the Dark Age begins.
- **c. 800 BCE** — Population recovers; the first poleis take shape; the Greek alphabet is adapted from Phoenician.
- **c. 750–700 BCE** — Homer and Hesiod; the earliest surviving alphabetic inscriptions.
- **c. 750–550 BCE** — Greek colonisation spreads across the Mediterranean and Black Sea.
- **c. 735–715 BCE** — The First Messenian War; Sparta begins to reduce the Messenians to helots.
- **c. 657 BCE** — Cypselus seizes power at Corinth, among the first of the tyrants.
- **c. 650–620 BCE** — The Second Messenian War; the Spartan system hardens.
- **c. 632 BCE** — Cylon attempts a tyranny at Athens and fails.
- **621 BCE** — Draco writes down the Athenian laws.
- **594/3 BCE** — Solon is archon; the seisachtheia and his constitutional reforms.
- **561/0 BCE** — Peisistratus first seizes power at Athens.
- **546 BCE** — Cyrus of Persia conquers Lydia; the Ionian Greeks pass under Persian rule.
- **546–528/7 BCE** — Peisistratus rules Athens; the City Dionysia and Panathenaea are expanded.
- **514 BCE** — Hipparchus is assassinated by Harmodius and Aristogeiton.
- **510 BCE** — Hippias is expelled from Athens with Spartan help.
- **508/7 BCE** — Cleisthenes reorganises the Athenian citizen body into ten tribes and demes.
- **499–494 BCE** — The Ionian Revolt against Persia; Athens sends help and then withdraws.

## Questions to carry

- Why did a shared Greek culture coexist with hundreds of separate political communities? Which of the two — the shared culture or the local loyalty — did Greeks themselves feel more strongly, and how would we know?
- Who counted as a citizen in a polis, and what did those excluded from citizenship contribute to the city that excluded them?
- Tyranny, oligarchy, and democracy all appear in this period. What does the Greek vocabulary of rule reveal about what people feared and wanted?
- Homer and Hesiod describe a heroic age, but they were composed in the world of the early polis. What can they be evidence for, and what can they not?
- Sparta and Athens both claimed to be citizen communities. How did they differ in who was included, and what did each require of its citizens?
- Lycurgus and Solon are both remembered as lawgivers. What makes the evidence for one far weaker than the evidence for the other?
- Cleisthenes' reforms are often called the founding of democracy. How much of the outcome was intended, and how much was the by-product of a faction fight?
