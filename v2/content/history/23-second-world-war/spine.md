# The Second World War and the Holocaust

**Period:** c. 1939–1945 · **Region:** Europe, Asia, and the world

**The deadliest war.** — Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939; Japan surrendered on 2 September 1945. In the six years between, fighting spread across Europe, North Africa, the Atlantic, the Pacific, and South-East Asia, and killed an estimated 70 to 85 million people — far more than the First World War, and, for the first time in a major war, probably more civilians than soldiers. The war destroyed the European empires that had begun it, left two new superpowers facing each other, and produced two things without precedent: an industrial campaign to murder an entire people, and a charter for a permanent organization of states. The organizing question is comparative. How was this war fought and experienced differently from the war of 1914–18? The answer lies in the character of the fighting, the place of civilians, the role of ideology, and the reach of the conflict.

## The road to war

**A peace that did not hold.** — The First World War ended with an armistice on 11 November 1918 and a settlement at Versailles in 1919 that imposed on Germany the loss of territory, strict limits on its army, a war-guilt clause, and reparations. The new Weimar Republic carried the blame for a defeat many Germans would not accept. The Great Depression after 1929 made the republic's politics desperate. On 30 January 1933 the president appointed Adolf Hitler, leader of the National Socialist German Workers' Party, as chancellor. Within a year the party had made itself the only legal party, and Hitler's programme was open: the union of German-speaking peoples in one Reich, the conquest of "living space" (Lebensraum) in the east, and the removal of Jews from German life.

**Testing the democracies.** — The road to war was not straight, and it was not inevitable. Hitler moved by stages, testing how far the other powers would go: rearmament announced in 1935, the Rhineland remilitarized in March 1936, Austria annexed in March 1938, the Sudetenland taken from Czechoslovakia at Munich in September 1938, the rest of Czechoslovakia occupied in March 1939. Britain and France followed a policy of appeasement, conceding each demand in the hope of avoiding another war, and then, after Prague, guaranteed Poland's borders. Each concession strengthened Hitler's conviction that they would not fight.

**Two dictators make a bargain.** — On 23 August 1939 the German and Soviet foreign ministers, Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov, signed a nonaggression treaty in Moscow. It bound each side to stay neutral if the other were attacked. Attached to it was a secret protocol that divided eastern Europe into spheres of influence: Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and eastern Poland to the Soviet Union; western Poland and Lithuania to Germany; and Soviet interest in Bessarabia, then part of Romania, acknowledged. The pact stunned the world. Communism and fascism had spent a decade denouncing each other, and the two states were now partners in carving up the states between them. The treaty gave Hitler the security he needed to attack Poland without a two-front war, and gave Stalin time and territory. It also sealed Poland's fate.

**Poland, blitzkrieg, and the phoney war.** — German forces invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. The campaign showed the shape of the war to come: armoured columns and dive-bombers broke through a narrow front and drove deep into the rear, encircling whole armies, while the Luftwaffe attacked roads, railways, and towns. This was "lightning war," blitzkrieg, and it worked. Britain and France declared war on 3 September, but could do little to help Poland. The Soviet Union invaded from the east on 17 September, and the two occupiers divided the country by a boundary treaty on 28 September. In the spring of 1940 the Soviet secret police shot some 22,000 captured Polish officers in the Katyn forest and elsewhere. For months after Poland fell, the western front was quiet — the "phoney war" — while the Soviets attacked Finland in the Winter War of 1939–40 and the Germans prepared their next move.

## The fall of France and the Battle of Britain

**The German offensive in the west.** — On 10 May 1940 German armies attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, and France. The Allied commanders expected a repeat of 1914, with the main blow coming through the flat plains of Belgium. Instead the German armoured spearheads drove through the hilly, wooded Ardennes, which the French had thought impassable for tanks, crossed the Meuse, and raced to the sea. The Allied armies in the north were cut off. Between 26 May and 4 June about 338,000 British, French, and Belgian troops were evacuated from the beaches at Dunkirk by naval and civilian boats, leaving behind most of their equipment. France signed an armistice on 22 June 1940. Germany occupied the north and the Atlantic coast; in the south a collaborationist government under Marshal Philippe Pétain ruled from Vichy. In London, General Charles de Gaulle called on Frenchmen to keep fighting, and a small Free French movement formed around him.

**The Battle of Britain.** — Hitler now expected Britain to make peace. It did not. In July 1940 the Luftwaffe began a campaign to destroy the Royal Air Force and win command of the air as a prelude to invasion. This was the Battle of Britain. It was fought between the German air force and RAF Fighter Command, directed by Hugh Dowding from underground operations rooms and supported by a new chain of radar stations that gave early warning of raids. The German bombers came to grief over the English Channel and southern England, and in September, after the RAF struck at Berlin, Hitler switched to bombing London and other cities — the Blitz. The invasion of Britain, Operation Sea Lion, was postponed and never attempted. On 20 August 1940 Winston Churchill told the House of Commons that "never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to so few." Britain's survival kept a base from which the war could later be carried back to Europe.

## The world war

**Barbarossa.** — On 22 June 1941 Germany and its allies invaded the Soviet Union on a front of some 2,900 kilometres, with about 3.8 million troops. Operation Barbarossa was the largest land invasion in history, and its aim was not only victory but the destruction of the Soviet state and the exploitation of its people and resources. The Germans advanced hundreds of kilometres, encircled and destroyed whole Soviet armies, and reached the outskirts of Moscow by December. But the attack also created the vast eastern front that would consume the German army. The Soviet Union, having signed the pact that made the invasion possible, now joined the alliance against Germany. Britain and the Soviet Union signed an agreement in July 1941; the United States, already supplying Britain under the Lend-Lease Act of March 1941, extended aid to the Soviets.

**Pearl Harbor and the Pacific war.** — In the Pacific, Japan had been fighting a long war in China since 1937 and was planning a strike southward to seize the oil, rubber, and tin of South-East Asia. On 7 December 1941 Japanese aircraft attacked the United States naval base at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii, sinking or damaging much of the American Pacific fleet. The next day the United States declared war on Japan; on 11 December Germany and Italy declared war on the United States. The European and Asian wars now became one. Japan quickly took Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore — where some 80,000 British and Commonwealth troops surrendered on 15 February 1942 — the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies, and Burma, and reached the borders of India. The United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and China were now allies.

## The turning points, 1942–43

**Midway and the Pacific.** — The Japanese advance was checked in the Coral Sea in May 1942 and broken at Midway on 4–7 June 1942. American codebreakers had read enough of the Japanese naval ciphers to know the target and lay an ambush. In a few minutes of dive-bombing, four Japanese aircraft carriers were sunk, with their trained aircrews. Japan never regained the strategic initiative. On Guadalcanal, in the Solomon Islands, American and Japanese forces fought a six-month campaign of attrition from August 1942 until February 1943, and the Allies began the long advance across the Pacific.

**El Alamein and Stalingrad.** — In North Africa, German and Italian forces under Erwin Rommel had pushed toward Egypt. At El Alamein in October and November 1942 the British Eighth Army under Bernard Montgomery, reinforced with American tanks and supplies, defeated them and began to drive them west. In the Soviet Union the German Sixth Army had reached the Volga at Stalingrad in August 1942. The fighting for the ruined city was house-to-house and appalling. In November Soviet forces counter-attacked on the flanks and encircled the Sixth Army; on 2 February 1943 its commander, Friedrich Paulus, surrendered with about 91,000 survivors. Stalingrad was the first great defeat of the German army, and it turned the eastern front. The two turning points came within weeks of each other, and in January 1943 at Casablanca Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt agreed to demand the "unconditional surrender" of the Axis.

**Kursk.** — In July 1943 the Germans attacked the Kursk salient in the largest tank battle of the war. Forewarned and dug in, the Red Army absorbed the blow and then counter-attacked, and from Kursk onward the Soviet armies advanced almost without pause until they reached Berlin. The German army was now in retreat on every front. In the Atlantic, the convoy battles against German submarines also turned in the Allies' favour in 1943 as escort carriers, long-range aircraft, and radar closed the "mid-Atlantic gap."

## The Holocaust

**From persecution to murder.** — The Holocaust was the state-organized murder of Europe's Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. It did not begin as mass killing. It began with law and exclusion. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of citizenship and forbade marriage or sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews. In November 1938, after a German diplomat was shot in Paris, the regime staged a nationwide pogrom — Kristallnacht, the "night of broken glass" — in which synagogues were burned, shops looted, and some 30,000 Jewish men arrested. Emigration, encouraged and then taxed, was the policy until the war closed the borders. After the conquest of Poland in 1939, the Germans forced Jews into crowded ghettos in Warsaw, Łódź, Kraków, and hundreds of other towns, where starvation and disease killed tens of thousands. The invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 brought the next step: mobile killing units, the Einsatzgruppen, followed the army and shot Jews, Roma, Communist officials, and others. At Babi Yar, outside Kiev, they murdered 33,771 Jews on 29–30 September 1941.

**The Wannsee Conference and the "Final Solution."** — On 20 January 1942 fifteen senior officials of the SS, the party, and the state met at a villa on the Wannsee, a lake outside Berlin, at the invitation of Reinhard Heydrich, head of the Reich Security Main Office. The minutes, taken by Adolf Eichmann, set out the "final solution of the Jewish question": the deportation of the Jews of Europe to the east and their murder by labour and other means. The protocol is written in the language of administration, and its euphemisms — "evacuation," "treated accordingly," "natural causes" — are part of the evidence. It counted some eleven million Jews in Europe and its figures for each country show how thoroughly the planners understood the task. The killing was already under way. The death camps of Operation Reinhard — Bełżec, Sobibór, and Treblinka — were built in occupied Poland in 1942 and murdered perhaps 1.5 million people, almost all Jews. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest camp, Jews were selected on arrival: those fit to work were used as slave labour, and the rest were killed in gas chambers with the insecticide Zyklon B, their bodies burned in crematoria. About 1.1 million people were killed at Auschwitz, among them Jews, Roma, and Soviet prisoners of war. By the time the camps were liberated in 1944–45, the Germans had murdered about six million Jews, roughly two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population, along with hundreds of thousands of Roma, the institutionalized mentally and physically disabled, and other victims. As the Red Army advanced, the camps were emptied and prisoners were driven west on foot in "death marches"; many died on the way.

**Bureaucracy, choices, and evidence.** — The historian Raul Hilberg described the Holocaust in *The Destruction of the European Jews* (1961) as a process with stages — definition, expropriation, concentration, deportation, annihilation — run by ordinary ministries and the state railway, not by a single killing agency. The Jewish councils, the Judenräte, were forced by the Germans to administer the ghettos and draw up deportation lists, and their role has been debated ever since: some historians see them as trapped, others as having gained a little room to save some people and lost the rest. Christopher R. Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) studied a unit of middle-aged German reservists, Police Battalion 101, and found that most of them became killers not because they were fanatics but through gradual brutalization, group pressure, and deference to authority; those who refused were not punished. Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996) argued instead that a deep and murderous German antisemitism made them willing, and most historians rejected that single explanation.

**Resistance and rescue.** — Jews resisted in the ghettos and the camps. In April 1943 the Jews of the Warsaw ghetto, with a few weapons smuggled in, rose against the SS and held out for nearly a month. In October 1943 prisoners at Sobibór killed some of the guards and broke out; at Treblinka and Auschwitz there were smaller revolts. Jewish partisans fought in the forests of Belarus and Lithuania. In October 1943 the Danish resistance ferried about 7,200 Jews across the water to neutral Sweden. The villagers of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon in France sheltered Jews, and the Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg issued protective papers in Budapest. Those who helped are remembered at Yad Vashem as the "Righteous Among the Nations." The Allies knew much of what was happening by 1942 but chose not to bomb the railway lines to Auschwitz, a decision that remains contested.

## Home fronts and total war

**Mobilizing everything.** — Germany, Britain, the Soviet Union, Japan, and the United States directed factories, farms, and labour to the war effort. In the Soviet Union entire industries were dismantled and moved east of the Urals ahead of the German advance, and re-assembled to produce tanks and aircraft. In Germany, Albert Speer centralized war production and, from 1942, used millions of forced labourers and prisoners. Women entered factories and auxiliary services in large numbers, and in the Soviet Union and Britain they served in anti-aircraft batteries and on the land. The United States, untouched by invasion, became the "arsenal of democracy," out-producing all the Axis states combined.

**Bombing.** — Both sides bombed cities. The Germans bombed Warsaw, Rotterdam, London, and Coventry; the Allies bombed German and Japanese cities on a far greater scale. British Bomber Command attacked at night, aiming at industrial cities; American bombers attacked by day, aiming at specific plants. In July 1943 raids on Hamburg created a firestorm that killed about 37,000 people. In February 1945 Dresden was destroyed and perhaps 25,000 died. In March 1945 American incendiary raids on Tokyo killed around 100,000 people in a single night. Some historians, such as Richard Overy, judge that it shortened the war and forced resources back into Germany; others argue it achieved little beyond killing civilians and hardening morale, and that "area bombing" was not decisive.

## D-Day and the defeat of Germany

**Normandy.** — On 6 June 1944, D-Day, Allied forces landed on five beaches in Normandy in the largest seaborne invasion ever attempted: about 156,000 troops on the first day, carried by some 5,000 vessels and supported by thousands of aircraft. The German defenders, expecting the attack farther east at Calais, were slow to respond. After weeks of fighting in the hedgerows, the Americans broke out at the end of July, and the Allies liberated Paris on 25 August. An attempt to end the war in 1944 by airborne landings in the Netherlands, Operation Market Garden in September, failed at Arnhem. In December the Germans counter-attacked in the Ardennes in the Battle of the Bulge, their last offensive in the west, and were beaten back by January 1945.

**The eastern front and victory in Europe.** — On the eastern front the Red Army destroyed the German Army Group Centre in Operation Bagration in June and August 1944 and drove into Poland and the Balkans. The Polish underground rose against the Germans in Warsaw in August 1944 and was crushed after two months while the Red Army waited on the far bank. In February 1945 Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin met at Yalta to plan the final campaigns and the shape of post-war Europe. Soviet forces took Berlin in April and May 1945 after a battle that cost hundreds of thousands of lives; Hitler killed himself in his bunker on 30 April. Germany surrendered unconditionally on 8 May 1945.

## The atomic bomb and the Pacific

**Island fighting.** — In the Pacific the Allies advanced from island to island toward Japan, taking Tarawa, the Marianas, and the Philippines. In February and March 1945 they took Iwo Jima after a month of fighting, and from April to June they fought for Okinawa, where Japanese forces used kamikaze suicide aircraft against the fleet and civilians died in huge numbers.

**Hiroshima and Nagasaki.** — The United States had been developing an atomic bomb since 1942 in the secret Manhattan Project, and tested the first device in the New Mexico desert on 16 July 1945. At the Potsdam conference in July, the Allies demanded Japan's unconditional surrender. Japan did not reply. On 6 August 1945 the B-29 bomber Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb on Hiroshima; on 9 August a plutonium bomb was dropped on Nagasaki; the Soviet Union declared war on Japan on 8 August and invaded Manchuria. The two bombs killed an estimated 110,000 to 210,000 people, most of them civilians, by the end of 1945. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August and signed the instrument of surrender on 2 September 1945 aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay. Whether the bombs were necessary to end the war without an invasion, or whether Japan would have surrendered anyway once the Soviet Union entered the war, has been debated since. Gar Alperovitz and others argue that the bombs were aimed partly at the Soviet Union and were not needed; Richard Frank and others argue that the Japanese leadership was not ready to surrender and that an invasion would have cost far more lives.

## Aftermath and the United Nations

**The reckoning.** — The war left Europe and Asia in ruins, with tens of millions of refugees and displaced persons. The Allied powers put surviving Nazi leaders on trial at Nuremberg from November 1945 to October 1946, charged with crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The trials established that individuals, not only states, could be held responsible for such crimes, and they introduced evidence of the Holocaust to the world. The word "genocide" had been coined in 1944 by the jurist Raphael Lemkin.

**The United Nations.** — Even before the fighting ended, the Allies planned a new organization to keep the peace. Delegates from fifty nations met at San Francisco in April 1945 and signed the Charter of the United Nations on 26 June 1945; it entered into force on 24 October 1945. The Charter created a General Assembly of all member states, a Security Council of great powers with the right to veto, and a set of principles: the sovereign equality of states, the peaceful settlement of disputes, and the prohibition of the threat or use of force against another state. In 1948 the General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Within two years the United States and the Soviet Union were rivals in a Cold War, and the European empires that had fought in the war began to break apart as their colonies demanded independence.

## A different kind of war

**Movement against stalemate.** — The First World War on the western front became a war of trenches, in which armies of millions faced each other across a narrow strip of ground for four years. The Second World War was a war of movement: armoured divisions, motorized infantry, and aircraft broke through and encircled, and campaigns were decided in weeks. Yet the difference should not be overstated. Leningrad was besieged for nearly 900 days, and Stalingrad was fought street by street for months.

**Civilians and the state.** — The most important difference is what happened to civilians. In the First World War, soldiers made up the great majority of the dead; in the Second, civilian deaths probably exceeded military ones. Civilians were bombed, starved, deported, and, in the Holocaust, systematically murdered. The state's reach into everyday life was far greater than in 1914–18: conscription of labour, rationing, propaganda, and control of the economy. The German state crossed a line no state had crossed before, using industrial methods to destroy a people.

**Ideology, science, and industry.** — The First World War was fought between empires over territory and power; the Second was also a war of ideology. Nazi Germany fought to build a racial empire and to destroy the Jews; the Soviet Union fought to survive; the Western Allies fought for the survival of their states and, in the Atlantic Charter of 1941, for the right of peoples to choose their own government. The war was also won by industrial capacity: the United States out-produced every other country, the Soviet Union moved its factories east, and Britain and the United States broke the German Enigma and Japanese codes. New weapons — radar, the proximity fuse, the jet aircraft, the V-2 rocket, and the atomic bomb — changed what war could do. The outcome was not settled in advance: in 1940 and 1941 Germany and Japan looked close to victory, and the alliance against them was improvised.
