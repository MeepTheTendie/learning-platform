# Russia and the Soviet Union

**Period:** c. 1905–1939 · **Region:** Russia and the Soviet Union

## The tsarist autocracy

**An empire of peasants.** — In January 1897 the Russian Empire took its first and only general census and counted 125,640,021 subjects. About seventy-seven per cent were peasants, and fewer than one in eight lived in a town. At its head stood Nicholas II, who became tsar in 1894. He ruled as an autocrat in a sense with no exact equivalent in western Europe: he was the source of law, the head of the army, and the protector of the Orthodox Church, and he believed his authority came from God and could not be shared. The Fundamental Laws declared that the emperor held "supreme autocratic power." There was no elected national assembly and no legal political party, and the Okhrana, the secret police, watched the universities and the factories.

**The state and its servants.** — The autocracy governed through a growing civil service and through provincial governors and police answerable to the Ministry of the Interior. The doctrine of official nationality treated Orthodoxy, autocracy, and the Russian people as the pillars of the state and justified Russification after the Polish uprising of 1863; some six million Jews were confined to the Pale of Settlement. The empire was a dynastic realm held together by army, church, and bureaucracy, not a nation-state. When those weakened at once, in 1917, little else remained.

**Industry from above.** — In the 1890s the finance minister Sergei Witte drove state-led industrialisation: tariffs, foreign loans and French capital, the Trans-Siberian Railway from 1891, and heavy investment in coal, iron, and oil. The growth rested on the countryside, which paid through taxes and grain exports, and it produced a concentrated working class of perhaps three million factory workers by 1914, housed in barracks on the city edges. Witte warned that industrialisation would "create a bourgeoisie and a proletariat," and that the autocracy would have to choose which to trust.

**The land and the commune.** — Emancipation in 1861 freed some twenty-three million serfs but left them short of land and paying redemption dues for forty-nine years. Most peasant land was held by the village commune, the obshchina, which redistributed strips among households. By 1900 a growing population pressed on a fixed supply of land. The peasants wanted land, not a constitution first, and many were willing to take it.

## 1905 and the Duma

**Bloody Sunday.** — On 9 January 1905 a priest, Georgy Gapon, led workers and their families toward the Winter Palace in St Petersburg with a petition for an eight-hour day, civil liberties, and a constituent assembly, addressing Nicholas as "father." Troops fired. The government admitted ninety-six dead; later counts ran to several hundred, perhaps a thousand. Gapon told the survivors there was no tsar and no God. Strikes spread, peasants burned manor houses, and the battleship Potemkin mutinied. In October a general strike forced the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and an elected Duma with legislative power.

**The Duma and the Fundamental Laws.** — The promise did not hold. The Fundamental Laws of April 1906 gave the tsar an absolute veto, control of the ministers and army, and the right to legislate between Duma sessions under Article 87. The First Duma was dissolved within months; the Second was dissolved on 3 June 1907 and the franchise rewritten to favour the gentry, leaving the Third and Fourth Dumas manageable. But 1905 left the soviet, a council of workers' deputies that had appeared in St Petersburg and would return in 1917.

**Stolypin's wager.** — Peter Stolypin, prime minister from 1906, tried to build independent peasant proprietors as a conservative counterweight. A decree of 9 November 1906 let peasants leave the commune and consolidate their strips; a land bank helped them buy, and about two million households left by 1916. Stolypin called it a "wager on the strong." It widened the division between richer and poorer peasants — the division the Bolsheviks later exploited in defining the kulak. Stolypin was assassinated in 1911; the war ended his reforms.

## War and the February Revolution

**The First World War.** — Russia entered the war in August 1914 with the largest army in Europe and an economy that could not supply it. Defeats came quickly at Tannenberg in 1914 and in the Great Retreat of 1915, which gave up Poland and much of the Baltic. In August 1915 Nicholas took personal command at the front, tying the monarchy to every later defeat and leaving the government to the empress Alexandra and, through her, Rasputin. By 1916 the army had lost millions, the cities were short of food and fuel, and the railways were failing.

**February 1917.** — On 23 February (8 March, new style), International Women's Day, women textile workers in Petrograd marched for bread; others joined, and the next day strikes spread. On 26 February troops fired on the crowds; on 27 February a garrison regiment mutinied and armed the demonstrators. The Duma, ordered dissolved, set up a Provisional Committee; the workers and soldiers set up a soviet. On 2 March, at Pskov, Nicholas II abdicated, and his brother Michael declined the throne. A dynasty of three centuries ended in a week of bread riots and mutiny.

**Dual power.** — The Provisional Government, drawn from the Duma and headed first by Prince Lvov and then by Alexander Kerensky, held the ministries and the war. The Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies held the streets, the soldiers' loyalty, and the legitimacy of the revolution. At first the Soviet, dominated by Mensheviks and Socialist-Revolutionaries, supported the government on condition that it convene a constituent assembly and pursue a democratic peace. The arrangement could not last, because the government continued the war and postponed land reform, and the soviet's base would accept neither.

## Lenin and the April Theses

**The return.** — On 3 April 1917 (16 April, new style) a sealed train from Switzerland carried Vladimir Ilyich Lenin into Petrograd's Finland Station. Lenin had been in exile since 1907 and had argued that socialists should turn the imperialist war into civil war. He arrived to find a party ready to support the Provisional Government conditionally. That night he told a crowd that the revolution had given them "a republic of the working people" but not the power to go with it.

**The theses.** — The next day Lenin read ten numbered theses to Bolshevik and then joint Bolshevik–Menshevik meetings; Pravda published them on 7 April as "The Tasks of the Proletariat in the Present Revolution," the April Theses. The war, they said, remained imperialist and no "revolutionary defencism" was permissible. Russia was passing from a first stage of revolution, which had given power to the bourgeoisie, to a second that must give it to the proletariat and the poorest peasants. The immediate slogan was "All power to the Soviets," and the party should be renamed Communist.

**A small party.** — In February 1917 the Bolsheviks had perhaps 24,000 members, a small sect beside the Mensheviks and Socialist-Revolutionaries. The theses were not accepted at once: the Pravda editors, Kamenev and Stalin, printed them with a dissenting note. Only as the war continued and the government failed to give land or peace did soviet power move from the margins to the majority. By October the party claimed around 350,000 members and controlled the Petrograd and Moscow soviets. Whether this was a disciplined vanguard leading a mass movement or a small party steering a radicalisation it did not create is the debate between Pipes and Fitzpatrick.

## October

**The July Days and the Kornilov affair.** — In early July 1917 soldiers and workers demonstrated in Petrograd against the war and the government; when the demonstration broke up the government blamed the Bolsheviks, shut Pravda, and arrested Trotsky, and Lenin fled to Finland. In August General Lavr Kornilov marched troops toward Petrograd, apparently to crush the soviet. Kerensky armed the workers and released Bolshevik prisoners; the Red Guards who turned Kornilov back were organised by the Bolsheviks, and the party emerged as the defender of the revolution. In September it won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, and Trotsky became chairman of the Petrograd Soviet.

**The insurrection.** — On 10 October the Central Committee voted ten to two for insurrection, with Kamenev and Zinoviev opposed; they leaked their opposition, which Lenin denounced as blackmail. Trotsky organised the rising through the Military Revolutionary Committee and timed it for the eve of the Second Congress of Soviets, so the congress would face a fait accompli. On the night of 24–25 October (6–7 November, new style) Red Guards and mutinous soldiers took the bridges, telegraph, stations, and bank. The Winter Palace fell on 25–26 October; the ministers were arrested and Kerensky fled. In military terms it was a small operation, because the old state had already lost its army and police.

**The first decrees.** — The Congress of Soviets met on the evening of 25 October; the Mensheviks and many Socialist-Revolutionaries walked out, and the Bolsheviks and Left Socialist-Revolutionaries remained. On 26 October it adopted the Decree on Peace, calling for an immediate armistice and a peace "without annexations or indemnities." On 26 October it adopted the Decree on Land, abolishing private property in land and turning the estates of landlords and the church over to peasant committees, adopting almost word for word the programme of the Left Socialist-Revolutionaries. It then formed a Council of People's Commissars: Lenin as chairman, Trotsky for foreign affairs, Stalin for the nationalities.

**The Constituent Assembly.** — Elections in November 1917 gave the Socialist-Revolutionaries a clear majority and the Bolsheviks about a quarter of the vote. The Assembly met on 5 January 1918 and refused to accept the supremacy of the soviets; the next day the Bolsheviks dissolved it by force.

## Civil War and the Red Terror

**Brest-Litovsk.** — In March 1918 the government signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany, giving up Poland, the Baltic, Finland, and Ukraine — about a third of the empire's population, farmland, and industry. Lenin argued the peace had to be signed to preserve the revolution; the Left Communists and Bukharin wanted a revolutionary war. The losses fed the civil war that followed.

**The war.** — The Civil War lasted from 1918 to 1921. The Whites — Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin and later Wrangel in the south, Yudenich in the northwest — had better officers and foreign supplies but no common programme and no answer to the land question. The Allies intervened on the edges: British and French troops in the north and the Black Sea, Japanese and American in the Far East. The Reds held the centre, the railways, and the two capitals, and Trotsky built the Red Army from conscripts and former officers to some five million men.

**War Communism and the Cheka.** — From 1918 the government imposed War Communism: nationalisation of industry, central control of production, rationing, the abolition of money in many transactions, and grain requisitioning. The Cheka, founded in December 1917 under Feliks Dzerzhinsky, became the instrument of coercion. After an attempt on Lenin's life in August 1918 the government declared the Red Terror; hostages were shot and thousands executed. Lenin's "Hanging Order" of 11 August 1918 demanded that kulaks in Penza be hanged in public. Estimates of the Cheka's victims in the civil-war years range from about 50,000 to 140,000, and historians dispute how much killing was directed from the centre and how much was local panic.

**Kronstadt and the famine.** — By 1921 the regime had won the war but lost the countryside. In March the sailors of the Kronstadt naval base rose against the government, demanding free elections to the soviets and an end to requisitioning; Trotsky and the Red Army crushed the revolt. A famine in 1921–22, worsened by drought and by requisitions that had left no seed, killed some five million people. The regime's answer to the crisis was a retreat in economics, not a loosening of political control.

## The New Economic Policy

**The retreat.** — At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921 Lenin proposed the New Economic Policy: a tax in kind on peasants instead of requisitioning, freedom to sell the surplus in a market, and the return of small industry and trade to private hands, while the state kept the "commanding heights" of heavy industry, banking, transport, and foreign trade. The NEP worked: grain production and the cities recovered, and by 1925 agriculture was near pre-war levels. It also produced traders and middlemen, the nepmen, and the "scissors crisis" of 1923, when peasants refused to sell grain because manufactured goods cost too much. The NEP was a truce, always described within the party as temporary.

**The struggle for the succession.** — Lenin suffered strokes from May 1922 and died on 21 January 1924. In his last writings, sometimes called his Testament, he warned that Stalin had concentrated "unlimited power" as general secretary and suggested his removal; the testament was suppressed. The succession was decided by alliances and betrayals. Stalin joined Zinoviev and Kamenev to defeat Trotsky, removed as commissar for war in 1925 and expelled in 1927; then he turned on Zinoviev and Kamenev; with the slogan "socialism in one country" he defeated the Left Opposition and, in 1928–29, the Right Opposition of Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky. By 1929 he led a one-party state unchallenged.

## Stalin's rise

**The general secretary.** — Stalin had been appointed general secretary in April 1922, a post that looked administrative but gave him control of the party's files and appointments. Through the apparatus he placed supporters in the regional committees that elected delegates to congresses, and that machine carried him through every vote.

**Socialism in one country.** — After 1924 the leadership faced a choice. Trotsky and the Left argued that the revolution could survive only if it spread and that the state should extract a "tribute" from the peasants to fund industry. Bukharin and the Right wanted slower growth, a market, and an alliance with the peasantry: "enrich yourselves," he told them. Stalin took the centre, first with the Right against Trotsky, then with the Left's programme against the Right. "Socialism in one country" meant the Soviet Union could build socialism without waiting for revolutions elsewhere, a claim that turned the Comintern from an instrument of world revolution into an instrument of Soviet policy.

**The grain crisis.** — In 1927–28 the state set grain procurement prices too low and the peasants sold less. Stalin's response, in January 1928, was the "Urals-Siberian method": officials went to the villages with quotas and took the grain by force, treating richer peasants as kulaks. It worked as procurement and was a rehearsal for collectivisation. Bukharin called it a return to War Communism and a declaration of civil war on the countryside; Stalin called it a temporary emergency. The emergency became policy.

## Collectivization and the famine

**The great break.** — In 1929, the year the regime called "the great break," the state set out to collectivise agriculture and "liquidate the kulaks as a class." Peasants were driven into collective farms, kolkhozy, and state farms, sovkhozy; those labelled kulaks — a term applied to any peasant who resisted, not only the rich — were deported, their property confiscated, their families sent north and to Siberia. The state claimed half the peasant farms were collectivised by 1930, a speed achieved by quotas, threats, and armed requisition. Stalin's article "Dizzy With Success," published in Pravda on 2 March 1930, blamed local officials for forcing peasants in and for "socialising" houses, cattle, and poultry, and ordered a pause. The pause was brief; by the mid-1930s about ninety per cent of farms were collectivised.

**The famine.** — In 1932–33 a famine killed some five to seven million people, most of them in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus, and the lower Volga. Procurement quotas continued while peasants starved, and a law of 7 August 1932 made stealing a few ears of grain from a collective farm punishable by death or ten years' labour — the "law of the spikelets." A passport system introduced in December 1932 bound peasants to the farms and made it impossible for the starving to leave; Kazakhstan lost perhaps a third of its population. Whether the famine was a deliberate weapon against the Ukrainian nation, as Robert Conquest argues, or the product of ideology, incompetence, and the grain drive remains one of the sharpest disputes in the field.

**The kolkhoz and the countryside.** — Collectivisation destroyed the peasant household as an independent unit and the village as a political community, and it financed the industrialisation that followed: the state took the grain and sold it abroad for currency and machinery. The machine tractor stations, which owned the tractors and hired them to the farms, gave the state another lever over the harvest. The farms were inefficient and the land was worked without incentive; Soviet agriculture never recovered the productivity of the pre-collectivisation countryside.

## The Five-Year Plans

**Industrialisation by plan.** — From 1928 the state directed investment by five-year plans. The First (1928–32) concentrated on heavy industry, coal, iron, steel, electricity, and machine building; the Second (1933–37) broadened into consumer goods and transport. The state claimed the first plan was completed in four years and that output more than doubled. The figures were contested then and have been argued since: Stephen Wheatcroft and R. W. Davies have shown that Soviet statistics were inflated and inconsistently defined, while Mark Harrison argues the growth was nonetheless real. What is not disputed is the command to build heavy industry regardless of cost.

**Magnitogorsk.** — The emblem of the drive was Magnitogorsk, a steel city built from nothing in the Urals. Thousands of peasants and young communists, many without industrial skill, built blast furnaces in the open, and the city grew in a few years into one of the world's largest steel centres. It also shows the pattern of the campaign: enormous output, appalling housing, forced labour, and a workforce recruited by propaganda and held by the internal passport. Stephen Kotkin's study of Magnitogorsk argues that the workers learned to "speak Bolshevik," building a new industrial civilization with its own hierarchies and loyalties. The Gulag supplied labour for other projects, including the White Sea–Baltic Canal (1931–33), where tens of thousands died.

**The costs.** — By 1939 the Soviet Union produced more steel, coal, and electricity than any country except the United States, and its industrial base would prove decisive when Germany invaded in 1941. The price was paid by workers and peasants. Real wages fell through the first plan, food was rationed, and millions lived in barracks and communal flats. The urban population doubled between 1926 and 1939, to about 56 million. The state called this a heroic struggle against backwardness; the workers experienced hunger, long hours, and the threat of denunciation.

## The Great Purge

**Kirov.** — On 1 December 1934 Sergei Kirov, the party boss of Leningrad, was shot in his office. The killing remains unexplained; Stalin used it to launch a wave of arrests. The security police, the NKVD, expanded rapidly, and in 1936–38, under Nikolai Yezhov, it carried out the Great Purge. The purge had two faces: show trials of the old Bolshevik leaders, and mass operations against ordinary citizens.

**The show trials.** — In August 1936 the first great show trial convicted Zinoviev, Kamenev, and fourteen others of plotting with Trotsky to murder Stalin; all were shot. A second trial in January 1937 convicted Karl Radek and others; a third in 1938 convicted Bukharin, Rykov, and Genrikh Yagoda, the former head of the NKVD. The defendants confessed in open court to sabotage, espionage, poisoning, and conspiracy with foreign powers, and were executed. In June 1937 the military purge removed Marshal Tukhachevsky and much of the senior officer corps, a blow felt in 1941. The trials were staged as lessons in loyalty; their purpose was to destroy any alternative to Stalin's authority and to explain the plans' failures as sabotage.

**The mass operations.** — The larger terror was not staged. In July 1937 the Politburo approved NKVD Operational Order No. 00447, which set regional quotas for the arrest and execution of "anti-Soviet elements" — former kulaks, priests, members of the old parties, and "socially dangerous" people. According to the NKVD's own records, released after 1991, 681,692 people were executed in 1937–38, and hundreds of thousands more were sent to the Gulag, which held some 1.3 million prisoners by 1939. The 1936 "Stalin Constitution," adopted in the same period, promised universal suffrage, secret ballots, and a bill of rights; it described a state that did not exist. J. Arch Getty argues the terror was driven by local officials and chaos; Robert Conquest and Oleg Khlevniuk argue for central direction and Stalin's responsibility.

## The Comintern

**The founding.** — In March 1919, with revolution in Germany and Hungary still a hope, the Bolsheviks founded the Communist International, or Comintern, in Moscow, with Zinoviev as its first chairman. At its Second Congress in 1920 it adopted twenty-one conditions for admission, requiring member parties to accept Moscow's leadership, purge reformists, and model themselves on the Bolsheviks. The conditions split the European left: social-democratic parties refused them, and the new communist parties were small, disciplined, and dependent on Soviet money and instructions. The Comintern was an instrument of Soviet foreign policy as much as a forum for revolution, and after 1924 that became its primary function.

**From class against class to the Popular Front.** — Under Stalin the line changed with Soviet needs. In 1928 the Comintern adopted "class against class," treating social democrats as "social fascists"; this split the German left and helped the Nazis to power in 1933. In 1935, with fascism ascendant, the Seventh Congress adopted the Popular Front, calling on communists to ally with socialists and liberals against fascism; its architect was Georgi Dimitrov. The new line produced the International Brigades and Soviet aid in the Spanish Civil War of 1936–39. For member parties the lesson of the decade was that revolution abroad would be subordinated to the security of the state at home.

## Chronology

- **1894–1903** — Nicholas II becomes tsar (1894); the census counts 125,640,021 subjects (1897); the Social-Democratic party splits into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks (1903).
- **9 January 1905** — Bloody Sunday: troops fire on Gapon's procession in St Petersburg.
- **October 1905** — A general strike forces the October Manifesto; the first St Petersburg Soviet is formed.
- **1906–11** — The Fundamental Laws preserve the tsar's veto; the Second Duma is dissolved on 3 June 1907; Stolypin's agrarian reforms and his assassination in 1911.
- **1914–15** — Russia enters the First World War; defeats at Tannenberg and in the Great Retreat; Nicholas II takes personal command.
- **23 February–2 March 1917** — The February Revolution; Nicholas II abdicates at Pskov.
- **7 April 1917** — Lenin's April Theses are published in Pravda.
- **July–August 1917** — The July Days and the Kornilov affair.
- **24–25 October 1917** — The October Revolution; the Decrees on Peace and Land.
- **5–6 January 1918** — The Constituent Assembly meets and is dissolved by force.
- **March–July 1918** — The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk; the Constitution of the RSFSR is adopted in July.
- **1918–21** — Civil War, War Communism, and the Red Terror.
- **March 1921** — The Kronstadt revolt is crushed; the New Economic Policy is adopted; famine kills some five million.
- **April–December 1922** — Stalin becomes general secretary; the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics is founded.
- **21 January 1924** — Lenin dies; the succession struggle begins.
- **1927–29** — The grain crisis; the Left Opposition is defeated; the First Five-Year Plan begins; mass collectivisation and dekulakisation.
- **1932–33** — Famine in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus, and the Volga.
- **1934–38** — Kirov is assassinated (1934); the "Stalin Constitution," the show trials, and the Great Purge (1936–38); 681,692 recorded executions.
- **1939** — The Soviet Union is the world's second industrial producer.

## Questions to carry

- The February Revolution took almost everyone by surprise. What had to be true of the tsarist state for a week of bread riots to end a dynasty?
- Lenin's party was small in April 1917 and dominant in October. Was that a disciplined vanguard leading a mass movement, or a small party steering a radicalisation it did not create?
- The first decrees of October ratified land seizures and promised peace. How much of the revolution was the Bolsheviks' programme, and how much was the country forcing the party's hand?
- Collectivisation and the Five-Year Plans were carried out in the name of the workers and peasants and were paid for mainly by them. What does that gap show about the Soviet state?
- Pipes, Fitzpatrick, Kotkin, Conquest, and Getty disagree about how much was planned and how much was chaos. Which sources can settle that, and which cannot?
