# Persia, Athens, and Alexander

**Period:** c. 499–323 BCE · **Region:** Aegean · Persia · Eastern Mediterranean

## The Greek fringe of a Persian world

**A coast of cities.** — In 546 BCE the Persian king Cyrus the Great defeated Croesus of Lydia and took Sardis. With that, the Greek cities of the western coast of Anatolia — Miletus, Ephesus, Phocaea, Smyrna, and the island cities of Chios and Samos — passed from Lydian to Persian rule. They were wealthy, literate, and quarrelsome. Miletus had founded colonies around the Black Sea and in Egypt; Samos had a navy and a great temple to Hera. The cities shared a dialect and festivals but had never formed a single state.

**How Persia ruled.** — The Persian empire stretched from the Aegean to the Indus. Its king ruled through satraps, provincial governors who collected tribute and raised troops; the Greek coast formed a satrapy with its capital at Sardis. The Persians generally left the cities their own laws, cults, and assemblies and ruled through local strongmen. The Greeks called such a ruler a tyrant (tyrannos) — a man who had seized power without constitutional right, not necessarily a cruel one. His position depended on the king's favour, and the price was tribute in silver, ships, and obedience. Persian rule was not especially harsh by ancient standards, but it was rule by outsiders, and it was resented by men who thought their cities free.

**The wider Greek world.** — West of the Aegean lay the Greek mainland, divided among hundreds of small states. The two largest were Sparta, in the southern Peloponnese, whose hoplites — heavily armoured infantry who fought shoulder to shoulder in a formation called the phalanx — were the best in Greece; and Athens, in Attica, which in 508 BCE had, under Cleisthenes, reorganized its citizen body into new tribes and councils. Athens called its system democracy (demokratia, "rule by the people"), though the people meant adult male citizens only; women, slaves, and resident foreigners had no vote.

## The Ionian revolt, 499–493

**Aristagoras and the failed expedition.** — In 499 BCE the tyrant of Miletus, Aristagoras, was in trouble. He had promised the satrap Artaphernes that he could conquer Naxos, in the Cyclades, and had been given a Persian fleet. The expedition failed: the commanders quarrelled, the siege dragged on for four months, and the fleet withdrew. Facing ruin, Aristagoras did the opposite of what a loyal vassal would do: he gave up his tyranny and urged the other Ionian cities to revolt. He then sailed to the mainland to find help.

**Sparta refuses, Athens agrees.** — At Sparta, Aristagoras showed King Cleomenes a bronze map of the world and promised easy conquest; Cleomenes, learning that the journey to the Persian capital took three months, told him to leave. At Athens he had better luck, arguing that the Milesians were Athenian colonists and the Persians rich and easy to beat. The Athenians voted twenty ships; Eretria sent five. Herodotus marked the decision: "These ships proved to be the beginning of evils for the Hellenes and the Barbarians."

**Sardis burns.** — In 498 the allies marched inland, took Sardis without a fight, and then watched it catch fire. The houses were roofed with reeds, and the flames spread until the whole town burned, destroying the temple of the goddess Cybele. The Persians later cited this as their reason for burning Greek temples in return. The Greeks withdrew, were beaten at Ephesus, and the Athenians sailed home. Herodotus says that when Darius learned who the Athenians were, he shot an arrow into the air with a prayer for vengeance and ordered a servant to say to him three times at every dinner: "Master, remember the Athenians."

**The revolt crushed.** — Without Athenian help the Ionians fought on for four years. In 494 the Persian fleet defeated the Ionian fleet at Lade, off Miletus; Miletus was taken and destroyed, its men killed and its women and children enslaved. In 493 the last resistance ended. The Persians reorganized the cities, fixing tribute and replacing tyrants in many places with more accountable governments. The revolt had failed, but it had drawn the mainland into a war with Persia, and it had taught the Persians that the Greeks across the Aegean were a problem to be settled.

## Marathon, 490

**Darius plans.** — In 490 Darius sent a naval expedition under Datis and Artaphernes. Its stated aim was to punish Athens and Eretria for the burning of Sardis and to restore the exiled Athenian tyrant Hippias, who went along as a guide. The fleet island-hopped, demanding earth and water, the tokens of submission. It destroyed Carystus, besieged Eretria, and took it when two citizens opened the gates; the temple of Apollo was burned.

**The battle.** — The Persians landed at Marathon, in Attica, about forty kilometres from Athens. The Athenians marched out under ten generals, one of them Miltiades, and were joined by a contingent from Plataea. They had perhaps nine or ten thousand hoplites; the Persians, with cavalry and archers, were more numerous, though the numbers are disputed. The Athenian line was thinned in the centre and strengthened on the wings. When the sacrifices proved favourable, they charged at a run, a manoeuvre Herodotus says no Greeks had attempted before. The Persian centre broke the Greek centre, but the Greek wings routed their opponents, turned inward, and crushed the Persian centre. The Persians fled to their ships; Herodotus gives the dead as 6,400 Persians and 192 Athenians.

**The aftermath.** — The Persian fleet sailed around Cape Sunion to attack Athens, hoping to arrive before the army returned. The Athenians marched back in a single day and reached the city first; the fleet turned away. Darius died in 486 preparing another expedition; his son Xerxes inherited the quarrel. Marathon was a small battle with a large moral effect: it showed that Persian infantry could be beaten by a Greek phalanx, and it gave Athens a claim to have saved Greece. The famous run from Marathon to Athens with the news is not in Herodotus; he mentions Pheidippides only as the runner sent to Sparta before the battle.

## The road to Salamis, 489–480

**Themistocles and the fleet.** — Athens in the 480s had perhaps thirty thousand adult male citizens and a larger population of women, slaves, and foreigners. Into its politics came Themistocles, a politician of extraordinary foresight and few scruples. In 483, when the state silver mines at Laurium produced a windfall, he persuaded the assembly to spend it not on a distribution to citizens but on two hundred triremes — fast, manoeuvrable warships with three banks of oars. The ships were built for a war with Aegina, Athens' old enemy, but they became the instrument that saved Greece.

**The Greeks prepare.** — In 481 a congress of about thirty Greek states, dominated by Sparta, met at the Isthmus of Corinth and swore an alliance. The oracle at Delphi told the Athenians to trust in their "wooden walls." Themistocles persuaded the assembly that the wooden walls meant the ships, not the acropolis. In the spring of 480 Xerxes set out with an army and fleet of enormous size, the greatest Persia had mounted. Bridges were thrown across the Hellespont, a canal cut through the peninsula of Mount Athos, and the cities along the route required to feed the army.

## Thermopylae and Salamis, 480

**The pass.** — The Greeks chose to make their first stand at Thermopylae, a narrow coastal pass where the Persian numbers would count for little. A force of perhaps seven thousand under the Spartan king Leonidas — including three hundred Spartans, seven hundred Thespians, and contingents from other states — held the pass for two days. Then a local man, Ephialtes, showed the Persians a mountain path around the Greek position. Leonidas dismissed the bulk of the army and stayed with the Spartans, the Thespians, and the Thebans. In the final fighting he was killed, and the Greeks were surrounded and shot down. Herodotus records the epitaph cut for the Spartans: "Stranger, report this word, we pray, to the Spartans, that lying here in this spot we remain, faithfully keeping their laws."

**The battle at sea.** — Thermopylae was a defeat, but it bought time and gave the Greeks a story of sacrifice. The Greek fleet had fought an indecisive action at Artemisium and withdrawn south. Xerxes marched into Attica, took the empty city, and burned the acropolis. The Greek fleet, some 380 ships, most of them Athenian, lay in the narrow strait between Salamis and the mainland. The Peloponnesians wanted to retreat to the Isthmus; Themistocles wanted to fight in the strait, where the Persian numbers would be cramped. He sent a secret message to Xerxes, through a trusted slave, saying the Greeks were about to flee and urging him to block the exits. Xerxes took the bait. In the narrows the Persian fleet was broken, and Xerxes, watching from a throne on the shore, saw his navy destroyed. He withdrew to Asia, leaving Mardonius with a picked army to hold Greece.

**Plataea and Mycale, 479.** — In 479 the Greeks met Mardonius at Plataea, in Boeotia, under the Spartan regent Pausanias. After a hard fight the Persian army was destroyed and Mardonius killed. On the same day, it was said, the Greek fleet won at Mycale, on the coast of Asia Minor, and the Ionian cities rose again. The invasion was over. Sparta, whose army had won Plataea, was the nominal leader of the Greek cause; Athens, whose fleet had won Salamis, had the ships and the appetite to carry the war across the Aegean.

## From alliance to empire, 478–454

**The Delian League.** — In 478 or 477 the states that wanted to continue the war formed a new alliance, with its meeting place and treasury on the island of Delos. Members swore oaths and dropped lumps of iron into the sea as a pledge to remain allies until the iron floated. Most contributed ships; the larger ones contributed money instead, a tribute called phoros, assessed by the Athenian Aristides at 460 talents a year. The league's aims were to ravage the king's territory, free the Greeks still under Persian rule, and take reparations for the invasion. Athens, with the largest fleet, supplied the commanders. From the beginning it was an alliance in name and an Athenian instrument in practice.

**Cimon and the war.** — Cimon, son of Miltiades, led the league's campaigns in the 470s and 460s. The Greeks took Eion, expelled pirates from Scyros, and won a double victory by land and sea at the Eurymedon, perhaps in 469 or 466. But the league also acted against its own members. When Naxos tried to leave in the late 470s, Athens besieged it. When Thasos revolted in 465 over a gold mine on the Thracian coast, Athens reduced it after more than two years, imposing a fine and tearing down its walls. The pattern was set: a member that wanted out was treated as a rebel, and the fleet built to fight Persia was used to keep the alliance together.

**The treasury moves to Athens.** — In 454 the treasury moved from Delos to Athens, on the pretext of security after an Athenian fleet supporting a revolt in Egypt was destroyed. The move put allied money under the Athenian assembly's control and made the temple of Athena on the acropolis a treasury in the literal sense. From this point the league was, in effect, an empire. Its member cities were subjects, not allies: they paid tribute, submitted disputes to Athenian courts, accepted Athenian garrisons and officials, and in some cases received Athenian settlers.

## The Athenian empire

**Tribute and the allied money.** — The tribute was assessed and collected by Athenian officials, and the assessments were recorded on stone slabs, the Athenian Tribute Lists, fragments of which survive. At its height it came to perhaps 400 to 600 talents a year, more than the revenue of many kingdoms. It paid for the fleet, the garrisons, and the buildings of Athens. In the 440s, after a peace with Persia credited to Callias, the war against the king largely stopped, but the tribute continued to be collected. The league had been founded to fight Persia; it had become the source of Athens' power over Greeks.

**Cleruchies and control.** — Athens tied its allies to it by several means. It sent out cleruchies — settlements of Athenian citizens on land confiscated from allied cities. A cleruch kept his Athenian citizenship but farmed land in an allied city; the effect was to plant a garrison in the subject's territory. Athens also required allies to use Athenian courts, accept Athenian garrisons and governors, and contribute ships or money on demand. When an ally revolted — Samos in 440, for example — it was besieged and forced back into line.

**The Parthenon and the tyrant city.** — After the Persian sack, Pericles led a great building program on the acropolis: the Parthenon, with its ivory-and-gold statue of Athena, was begun in 447 and finished in 432. It was paid for in part out of the allied tribute, and this was not a secret. Pericles' opponents said openly that the Greeks had been enslaved and that Athens adorned itself with the common money as a vain woman decks herself with costly stones. Pericles answered that the money was Athens' by right, since Athens did the fighting. Thucydides has him call Athens "the school of Hellas" and describe the empire as a tyranny that it was dangerous to give up.

**Periclean Athens.** — Under Pericles, elected general almost every year from 443 until his death in 429, Athens was at its height. The democracy was at its most radical: most offices were filled by lot, jurors were paid, and the assembly decided war, taxes, and law. Pericles' partner Aspasia, a Milesian woman, was famous for her intelligence, and the sculptor Pheidias directed the acropolis works. The confidence rested on the empire, and the empire on the fleet.

## The Peloponnesian War, 431–404

**Two leagues and the triggers.** — The Greek world had settled into two armed camps: Athens led the Delian League, now an empire, and Sparta led the Peloponnesian League. In 446 or 445 they had made a Thirty Years' Peace; it lasted less than fifteen years. The quarrels that began the war were local. In 435 a civil war in Epidamnus drew in Corcyra and then Corinth; Athens, not wishing to lose the Corcyran navy, made a defensive alliance with Corcyra. In 432 Potidaea, a Corinthian colony that was also an Athenian ally, revolted with Corinth's encouragement, and Athens besieged it. In the same year Athens passed the Megarian Decree, barring the Megarians from the ports and markets of the empire, a trade embargo that Corinth and Megara saw as an act of war. Sparta's allies voted that Athens had broken the peace; in 431 Sparta invaded Attica, and the war began.

**Thucydides' distinction.** — Thucydides, an Athenian general who wrote the war's history, set out two levels of cause. He would first give the "grounds of complaint and points of difference," so that no one need ask what "immediate cause" plunged the Greeks into so great a war. "The real cause I consider to be the one which was formally most kept out of sight. The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war inevitable." Historians have argued about that word ever since. Some see a structural collision between a rising naval empire and a fearful land power; others, such as Donald Kagan, argue the war was not inevitable, and that specific decisions by Pericles, Sparta, and Corinth turned a tense peace into war.

**The war.** — The first phase, the Archidamian War (431–421), was fought on Pericles' plan: the Athenians abandoned the Attic countryside, moved inside the long walls connecting city and port, and used the fleet to raid the Peloponnese. In 430 a plague killed perhaps a third of the population, including Pericles in 429. In 425 the Athenians took Pylos and captured Spartans on Sphacteria; in 422, at Amphipolis, Cleon and Brasidas were both killed. The Peace of Nicias in 421 did not last. In 415 Athens, urged on by Alcibiades, sent a great expedition against Syracuse; it ended in 413 with the destruction of the fleet and army. Sparta, with Persian money, occupied Decelea and took the war to the sea. Athens won at Cyzicus in 411 and Arginusae in 406, but in 405 Lysander destroyed the Athenian fleet at Aegospotami, and in 404 Athens surrendered and the Thirty Tyrants were installed.

**Contingency.** — Nothing was foreordained. Athens came close to winning in 425 and again in 406; the Sicilian expedition was a gamble that might have succeeded; the Spartan alliance was repeatedly near collapse. What destroyed Athens was the plague, a series of poor decisions, and the defection of resentful subjects. The empire built to fight Persia was lost in a war among Greeks.

## The fourth century and the rise of Macedon

**Sparta, Thebes, and the exhausted cities.** — Sparta's victory was brief. Its harsh treatment of former allies made it hated, and in 395 Athens, Thebes, Corinth, and Argos went to war with it. In 371 the Theban general Epaminondas defeated the Spartans at Leuctra, breaking their military reputation and freeing the helots of Messenia; Thebes dominated Greece for a decade, until Epaminondas was killed at Mantinea in 362. The cities had fought one another to exhaustion, and Persia regained influence over the Greek cities of Asia. It was into this vacuum that a new power to the north stepped.

**Philip II of Macedon.** — Macedon was a kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world, whose kings claimed Greek descent but whose people southerners regarded as half barbarians. Philip II became king in 359 and turned it into a great power. He reformed the army, creating a Macedonian phalanx armed with the sarissa, a long pike, and developing the Companion Cavalry, heavy horsemen who charged in a wedge. He took Amphipolis and the gold mines of Mount Pangaeum, which gave him money, and interfered in the Third Sacred War over the shrine at Delphi; by the Peace of Philocrates in 346 he sat on the Amphictyonic Council that controlled the shrine.

**Chaeronea, 338.** — Athens, urged on by the orator Demosthenes, whose speeches against Philip are called the Philippics, allied with Thebes to stop him. In 338 the armies met at Chaeronea in Boeotia. Philip's phalanx and his son Alexander's cavalry broke the allied line; the Theban Sacred Band was killed to a man. Philip then called the Greek states to a congress at Corinth and formed a league, with himself as leader, to make war on Persia. In 336, as he prepared to cross to Asia, he was assassinated at Aegae. His son Alexander, twenty years old, succeeded him.

## Alexander, 336–323

**Securing the throne.** — Alexander moved at once. He had potential rivals killed, secured the army's loyalty, and in 335 marched south, where Thebes had revolted. He took the city, destroyed it, and sold its people into slavery, a warning that made the rest of Greece submit. The League of Corinth appointed him its general against Persia, and in 334 he crossed the Hellespont with some thirty thousand infantry, five thousand cavalry, and a fleet of about 160 ships.

**The conquest of the Persian empire.** — At the Granicus in 334 he defeated the local satraps, and most of Asia Minor came over to him. In 333, at Issus in Cilicia, he met Darius III in person and defeated him; Darius fled, leaving his mother, wife, and children in Alexander's hands. Alexander treated the royal women with courtesy and pressed south, taking Tyre after a long siege and entering Egypt, where he was welcomed as a liberator. He founded Alexandria and made a pilgrimage to the oracle of Ammon at Siwa, after which he styled himself the son of Zeus-Ammon. In 331, at Gaugamela near Arbela, he met Darius again. The Persians had chosen the ground and had the numbers, but the Macedonian cavalry charge broke their line, Darius fled, and the empire was his. He entered Babylon and Susa, and in 330 burned the palace at Persepolis, a deliberate act, Arrian says, meant as vengeance for the Persian burning of Athens; Arrian himself doubts that it was just.

**To the Indus.** — For three years Alexander campaigned in Bactria and Sogdiana, in what is now Afghanistan and Central Asia, against local leaders such as Spitamenes. He founded cities, married a Bactrian princess, Roxana, and demanded that his companions prostrate themselves before him, a Persian practice the Greeks found humiliating. The strain showed: he executed his general Philotas and then his father Parmenio, killed Cleitus, the man who had saved his life, in a drunken quarrel in 328, and had the philosopher Callisthenes arrested and left to die. In 327 he crossed the Hindu Kush into India and in 326 defeated King Porus at the Hydaspes, but at the river Hyphasis his soldiers refused to march further. He turned back and led part of his army home across the Gedrosian desert, where many died of thirst and heat.

**Death at Babylon.** — By 324 he was back in Persia, arranging the government of his empire and training Persian troops in the Macedonian manner. In 324 his closest friend, Hephaestion, died, and Alexander's grief was extravagant. In June 323, at Babylon, he fell ill after a banquet; the sources describe a fever, and later writers claimed poison, a story Arrian repeats only to reject it. He died ten days later, aged thirty-two. Asked to whom he left his kingdom, he is said to have answered, "To the best"; his generals then fought over the fragments for forty years. Arrian, writing in the second century CE, ends with a portrait of Alexander as a man of boundless energy and insatiable desire for praise; in the judgment of historians such as A. B. Bosworth, his conquests rested on force and self-interest, not on any dream of uniting the peoples he ruled.

## Chronology

- **546 BCE** — Cyrus the Great takes Sardis; the Greek cities of the coast pass under Persian rule.
- **499 BCE** — Aristagoras of Miletus leads the Ionian revolt against Persia.
- **498 BCE** — The Ionians, with Athenian and Eretrian help, burn Sardis.
- **494 BCE** — The Persian fleet defeats the Ionians at Lade; Miletus is destroyed.
- **490 BCE** — Darius sends a naval expedition; the Athenians and Plataeans win at Marathon.
- **486 BCE** — Darius dies; Xerxes becomes king of Persia.
- **483 BCE** — Themistocles persuades Athens to build a fleet with the silver from Laurium.
- **481 BCE** — Greek states form an alliance at the Isthmus of Corinth.
- **480 BCE** — Xerxes invades; the Greeks hold Thermopylae, then win at Salamis.
- **479 BCE** — The Greeks defeat the Persians at Plataea and Mycale.
- **478/477 BCE** — The Delian League is founded, with its treasury on Delos.
- **454 BCE** — The league's treasury is moved to Athens.
- **447–432 BCE** — The Parthenon is built, funded in part from allied tribute.
- **431 BCE** — The Peloponnesian War begins.
- **430–429 BCE** — Plague at Athens; Pericles dies.
- **421 BCE** — The Peace of Nicias.
- **415–413 BCE** — The Athenian expedition against Syracuse is destroyed.
- **404 BCE** — Athens surrenders; the Peloponnesian War ends.
- **371 BCE** — The Thebans defeat Sparta at Leuctra.
- **359 BCE** — Philip II becomes king of Macedon.
- **338 BCE** — Philip defeats Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea.
- **336 BCE** — Philip is assassinated; Alexander becomes king.
- **334 BCE** — Alexander crosses to Asia; the Battle of the Granicus.
- **333 BCE** — Alexander defeats Darius III at Issus.
- **331 BCE** — Alexander wins at Gaugamela; the Persian empire falls to him.
- **330 BCE** — Persepolis is burned; Darius III is murdered.
- **326 BCE** — Alexander defeats Porus at the Hydaspes; the army refuses to cross the Hyphasis.
- **323 BCE** — Alexander dies at Babylon.

## Questions to carry

- Why did the Ionian revolt draw Athens into a war with Persia, and why did Herodotus call those twenty ships the beginning of evils?
- Thucydides distinguishes the immediate triggers of the Peloponnesian War from its "truest cause." Which kind of cause does the evidence best support, and why?
- How did an alliance founded to fight Persia become an empire over Greeks? Which steps were deliberate, and which were accidents?
- Athens called itself a democracy and ruled an empire of subject cities. How did Athenians justify that combination, and how did their subjects answer?
- Alexander burned Persepolis in the name of avenging Athens. Was his campaign a Greek war of revenge, a Macedonian war of conquest, or something else? What does Arrian's own doubt suggest?
