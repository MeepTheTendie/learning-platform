# Revolution, Napoleon, and the settlement of 1815

**Period:** c. 1789–1815 CE · **Region:** France · Europe · the Atlantic

## The monarchy that could not pay

**A fiscal crisis before a political one.** — By 1789 the French crown could no longer borrow cheaply or raise enough revenue to close its deficit. Two wars had built the debt: the Seven Years' War, which cost France its North American empire, and the war for American independence. Perhaps half of ordinary revenue went to interest; the rest had to cover army, navy, court, and bureaucracy. The tax system was the trap: the *taille*, the main direct tax, fell on commoners, while nobility and clergy were largely exempt, and indirect taxes were collected by private financiers who took a profit first. Every remedy touched a privilege some powerful body would defend.

**A society of orders.** — France was a society of orders defined by law, not classes defined by wealth. The clergy, the First Estate, owned perhaps a tenth of the land and paid a voluntary "free gift" instead of taxes. The nobility, the Second Estate, held exemptions from the *taille*, monopolised high office, and drew seigneurial dues. The Third Estate was everyone else — more than 95 per cent: peasants, artisans, shopkeepers, lawyers, merchants. "Privilege" meant a private law, not merely luxury.

**Reform, and resistance to it.** — Four finance ministers tried to reform the system and fell. Turgot proposed to tax the nobility and was dismissed in 1776; Necker published the crown's accounts and was dismissed in 1781; Calonne summoned an Assembly of Notables in 1787, which refused his land tax. The *parlements*, the high law courts that registered royal edicts, then claimed a right to block new taxes, and in 1788 the Parlement of Paris declared that only the nation, assembled in the Estates-General, could consent to a permanent new tax. That turned a financial emergency into a constitutional one. On 8 August 1788 the king called the Estates-General for May 1789. The Estates-General was the old assembly of the three orders, not a parliament; it had last met in 1614 and voted by order, giving clergy and nobility two votes against the Third Estate's one. Whether it would vote by order or by head was left open, and it decided everything.

**Sieyès and the meaning of the Third Estate.** — In January 1789 the abbé Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès published a pamphlet that gave the Third Estate a theory of itself. "What is the Third Estate?" he asked. "Everything. What has it been until now in the political order? Nothing. What does it demand? To be something." He argued that the nobility was not part of the nation but a burden on it, and that commoners did the productive work of society and could therefore constitute a nation alone. Elections followed, and voters drew up *cahiers de doléances*, lists of grievances. The deputies who reached Versailles in May 1789 were mostly lawyers, officials, and professionals — not peasants, and not revolutionaries.

## From the Estates-General to the National Assembly

**The assembly declares itself a nation.** — The Estates-General opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789, the three orders sitting separately. The Third Estate, granted double representation, demanded that the orders verify powers together and vote by head; the clergy and nobility refused. On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly, claiming to represent the nation. Three days later, locked out of its hall, it moved to an indoor tennis court and swore not to separate until France had a constitution. On 23 June the king ordered the orders to sit separately and annulled their resolutions; the deputies stayed in their seats. Within days most of the clergy and many nobles had joined. The monarchy had lost the initiative without a shot being fired.

**The fall of the Bastille.** — The court concentrated troops around Paris and on 11 July dismissed Necker, the minister the public trusted. Paris, hungry after a bad harvest, feared attack. On 14 July crowds searching for arms turned on the Bastille, an old fortress and prison; its governor surrendered after a day of fighting and was killed. The event mattered less for what it was than for what it showed: the king had no reliable force in Paris, while the crowd and a new municipal militia — the National Guard under Lafayette — could protect the Assembly. Louis XVI drew back, visited Paris, and accepted the tricolour cockade. A revolution had begun that no one had planned.

**The Great Fear and the night of 4 August.** — That summer, rumours spread in the countryside that brigands in noble pay were coming to burn the harvest. Peasants armed themselves and in many places attacked manor houses and burned the records of seigneurial dues. On the night of 4 August 1789 frightened deputies renounced feudal dues, tithes, venality of office, and the fiscal privileges of provinces and clergy. The decrees were later qualified, but the order of privilege had been formally dismantled. On 26 August the Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights"; "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation"; a society without guaranteed law and separated powers "has no constitution at all."

**The limits of the Declaration.** — The Declaration spoke of man in the abstract, and practice was narrower. The Assembly separated "active" citizens, who paid enough tax to vote, from "passive" citizens, who could not; women had no political rights at all. In the colonies, free people of colour demanded the rights the Declaration proclaimed, and the Assembly equivocated for years. In 1791 Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen, turning the Assembly's language against it: "Woman, wake up; the tocsin of reason is being heard throughout the whole universe; discover your rights." The universal claims had been made; who could hold them was undecided.

## The constitutional monarchy and its collapse

**Rebuilding the state.** — The Assembly built as well as destroyed. It replaced the old provinces with eighty-three departments of roughly equal size, each with elected officials, so that no province could become a base of resistance. It created uniform courts and a single national market. To pay the debt it confiscated church lands and issued *assignats*, paper notes secured on their sale; too many were printed and their value collapsed. Its most divisive act was the Civil Constitution of the Clergy of July 1790, which reorganised the church, made bishops and priests elected salaried officials, and required an oath of loyalty to the constitution. About half the clergy refused. Refractory priests became leaders of popular counter-revolution, and religion divided the country more deeply than any tax had.

**Varennes and the coming of war.** — On the night of 20–21 June 1791 Louis XVI fled Paris with his family and was caught at Varennes. The fiction that he accepted the revolution could not survive. In July a crowd demanding his removal was fired on at the Champ de Mars, and the Assembly briefly suspended the press. In September it produced the Constitution of 1791: a constitutional monarchy with one elected legislature and a restricted franchise. The new Legislative Assembly met in October, and in April 1792, under pressure from the left and from the king's ministers, declared war on Austria. The war radicalised politics, made every defeat look like treason, and brought the king's loyalty to a head. In July the Prussian commander issued the Brunswick Manifesto, threatening Paris if the royal family were harmed; it enraged the city instead of cowing it. On 10 August crowds stormed the Tuileries and the king was suspended. In early September, as the Prussians advanced, crowds killed more than a thousand prisoners in the September Massacres. On 20 September the French checked the invasion at Valmy, and the next day the new National Convention abolished the monarchy and declared a republic. In January 1793 the Convention executed Louis XVI by a narrow majority.

## The republic, the Terror, and the counter-revolution

**The emergency of 1793.** — By spring 1793 the republic was at war with almost all of Europe: Britain, Spain, the Dutch Republic, and the German and Italian states had joined Austria and Prussia. In the west, a revolt in the Vendée began in March when peasants resisting conscription and defending their priests took up arms; it became a savage civil war. Federalist revolts against Paris broke out after the Girondin deputies were expelled in June. The *assignat* collapsed, food was scarce, and the urban poor, the *sans-culottes*, demanded price controls and terror against hoarders. The Convention created emergency institutions: a Committee of Public Safety (April 1793), a Revolutionary Tribunal, and "representatives on mission" with dictatorial powers. In August the *levée en masse* conscripted able-bodied men; in September the Law of Suspects authorised the arrest of anyone suspected of disloyalty, and a general maximum fixed prices.

**The machinery of the Terror.** — *Terror* here names a period and system of emergency government, roughly September 1793 to July 1794, in which the republic used arrest, revolutionary courts, and the guillotine against its internal enemies — not a random attack on civilians. The guillotine was in origin a humanitarian device, meant to make execution quick and equal for all ranks; it was now used on a mass scale. The Committee of Public Safety, dominated after July 1793 by Maximilien Robespierre, governed through the Convention but increasingly in place of it. Jacobin clubs, watch committees, and representatives on mission carried the Terror into every town. Dechristianisation closed churches, and at Nantes the representative Carrier had prisoners drowned in the Loire.

**Robespierre and the Great Terror.** — Robespierre was a provincial lawyer, incorruptible and dogmatic, and by 1794 the most powerful man in France. He destroyed the factions to his left and right, sending the radical Hébertists and then the moderate Dantonists to the guillotine in spring 1794. In June the law of 22 Prairial stripped defendants of most procedural protections, and executions rose steeply — the "Great Terror." But the Terror frightened the men who had made it, and when Robespierre threatened further purges the Convention turned on him. On 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) he and his allies were arrested and the next day guillotined.

**Was the Terror inevitable?** — It is easy to read 1793 backwards and see the guillotine as the Revolution's destination, but the Terror arose from a specific emergency of invasion, civil war, collapsing currency, and fear of betrayal. Some historians trace it to the Revolution's own logic — an idea of a single general will that could not tolerate dissent; others call it improvised. The disagreement is the central argument about the Revolution.

## The Directory and the ascent of Bonaparte

**Thermidorian reaction.** — After Thermidor the Convention dismantled the emergency government, and in the south and west a "White Terror" killed Jacobins. The constitution of the Year III (1795) created a bicameral legislature — the Council of Five Hundred and the Council of Ancients — and an executive of five Directors. The franchise was restricted to property owners. The Directory, as the regime was called, was weak from the start: it lacked a stable majority, faced royalist plots on the right and egalitarian plots on the left, and survived by annulling elections and staging coups. In 1796 it crushed the Conspiracy of Equals, a proto-communist plot led by Gracchus Babeuf; in 1797 and 1798 it purged first the royalists and then the left. A republic that lived by coups could not long survive them.

**A republic of generals.** — The Directory's one success was war, and its generals had become political. The most successful was Napoleon Bonaparte, a Corsican artillery officer who made his name at Toulon in 1793 and saved the government from a royalist rising in 1795. In 1796–97 he defeated Austria in a brilliant Italian campaign; in 1798 he invaded Egypt, where Nelson destroyed his fleet at the Nile, but he returned to France in 1799 leaving the army behind, and his reputation survived.

**Brumaire.** — On 18–19 Brumaire (9–10 November 1799) Bonaparte, with Sieyès and his brother Lucien, engineered a coup. The Councils were persuaded to transfer power to three provisional consuls, and Bonaparte quickly dominated them. The constitution of the Year VIII gave France a First Consul with far more power than the Directors had held. The Revolution's decade of parliamentary government ended not with a battle but with a parliamentary manoeuvre backed by troops.

## The Consulate and the reordering of France

**The Code civil.** — Bonaparte's most durable achievement was the Civil Code, promulgated in March 1804 and later known as the Code Napoléon. It unified French law, guaranteed equality before the law, protected property absolutely, and made contracts binding. It was also conservative: it placed the wife under her husband's authority, gave the father control of family property and children, restricted divorce, and treated women as legally subordinate. Civil equality was preserved; the challenge to the authority of husbands and fathers was not. Exported by conquest, the Code shaped the law of much of the world.

**Administration, finance, religion, and education.** — The Consulate rebuilt the state. The law of 28 Pluviôse (1800) created the *prefects*, the chief administrative officers of the departments, appointed by and answerable to the central government. A prefect was not elected but was the instrument through which Paris controlled the provinces; the office survives in France. The Bank of France (1800) gave the state a central bank, and the silver franc germinal (1803) stabilised the currency. In 1801 Bonaparte signed a Concordat with Pope Pius VII, ending the breach of the Civil Constitution: the church regained a public role, but the state appointed bishops and paid the clergy, and the church accepted the sale of its lands. Education was reorganised around state secondary schools, the *lycées*, and the Legion of Honour (1802) rewarded service to the state. The result was a centralised administration that outlasted the man who built it — and a question the period debated: had the Consulate consolidated the Revolution or betrayed it?

## Empire and the wars

**From Consul to Emperor.** — In 1804 Bonaparte had himself proclaimed emperor and in December was crowned in Notre-Dame, placing the crown on his own head. In 1805 Britain formed the Third Coalition with Austria, Russia, and Sweden; on 2 December he destroyed the Austrian and Russian armies at Austerlitz, and the Treaty of Pressburg stripped Austria of territory. In 1806 he reorganised Germany into the Confederation of the Rhine, ended the Holy Roman Empire, and defeated Prussia at Jena; in July 1807 he imposed the Treaties of Tilsit on Russia and Prussia. From Madrid to the Niemen, Napoleon controlled or dominated the continent.

**The Continental System and the Peninsular War.** — Britain remained unbeatable at sea, especially after Trafalgar in 1805. Napoleon tried economic warfare: the Berlin Decrees of 1806 and later measures forbade the continent to trade with Britain — the Continental System — and required allies to close their ports. The policy hurt Britain, but it hurt Europe too and forced more conquests to enforce it. In 1807 he invaded Portugal, Britain's oldest ally; in 1808 he deposed the Spanish Bourbons and put his brother Joseph on the throne. The Spanish rose in revolt, and the Peninsular War became a long, brutal conflict in which guerrillas and Wellington's British army bled the French for years. Napoleon called it his "Spanish ulcer"; it also helped give birth to a new force in European politics, nationalism.

**Russia, 1812.** — In June 1812 Napoleon invaded Russia with more than half a million men. The Russians retreated, trading space for time, and fought a terrible defensive battle at Borodino in September. Napoleon entered Moscow, but the city burned and the tsar refused to negotiate. In October the retreat began and became a catastrophe; only a small fraction of the army returned. The myth of invincibility was broken, and the subject nations of Europe began to rise.

## Collapse and the settlement of 1815

**The fall of the empire.** — In 1813 Russia, Prussia, Austria, and Britain formed a new coalition. In October the allies defeated Napoleon at Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations," and pushed him back across the Rhine. In April 1814 he abdicated, and the Bourbons returned in the person of Louis XVIII, who granted a Charter preserving many revolutionary and Napoleonic gains: equality before the law, the Code, and a bicameral parliament. Napoleon was exiled to Elba.

**The Hundred Days and Waterloo.** — In March 1815 Napoleon escaped Elba and landed in France; the soldiers sent to arrest him joined him, and he entered Paris while Louis XVIII fled. The Hundred Days ended at Waterloo on 18 June 1815, where Wellington and Blücher's Prussians defeated him. He was sent to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821.

**The Congress of Vienna.** — The peace was made at Vienna, where from September 1814 to June 1815 the great powers — Metternich for Austria, Castlereagh for Britain, Tsar Alexander I for Russia, Hardenberg for Prussia, and Talleyrand for defeated France — redrew the map of Europe. They worked from legitimacy, the restoration of dynasties; compensation, the reward of the powers; and the balance of power, so that no state could dominate. They did not seek revenge: France kept its 1792 borders and was not dismembered, because a weakened France would leave Austria and Russia too strong. Buffer states were created around France, Prussia was enlarged in the Rhineland, and Austria took control of northern Italy. In 1815 the powers signed the Quadruple Alliance to enforce the settlement, and Russia, Austria, and Prussia formed the Holy Alliance, a conservative pledge to uphold Christian monarchy. The settlement did not end war, but it kept the great powers from a general European conflict for a century, until 1914. Its deeper significance is that it settled matters among states rather than restoring the old society: the Code, civil equality, and centralised administration survived across much of Europe. The revolution had been defeated in arms; many of its institutions had won.

## The Haitian Revolution

**The richest colony in the world.** — Saint-Domingue, the western third of Hispaniola, was in the 1780s the most profitable colony on earth. Its sugar and coffee plantations, worked by some 500,000 enslaved Africans, produced a large share of Europe's supply. Above them stood about 30,000 free people of colour, many propertied, and about 30,000 whites. The colonial Code Noir defined the enslaved as property, and free people of colour faced growing discrimination.

**The rising of 1791.** — In August 1791 the enslaved people of the northern plain rose in a vast, organised revolt; within weeks plantations were burning. In 1793, with the colony invaded by Britain and Spain, the commissioner Sonthonax abolished slavery in the north to win the insurgents to the republic, and the National Convention extended abolition to the whole French empire in February 1794. A formerly enslaved leader, Toussaint Louverture, joined the republicans, drove out the British and Spanish, and by 1801 governed the whole island. In July 1801 he promulgated a constitution declaring: "There cannot exist slaves on this territory, servitude is therein forever abolished. All men are born, live and die free and French." It also made Toussaint governor for life and bound the cultivators to the plantations. The revolution had freed the enslaved, kept the plantation economy, and concentrated power in one man.

**Independence.** — Napoleon, who had restored slavery in the French Caribbean, sent an army under his brother-in-law General Leclerc in 1802. Toussaint was captured by deceit and died in a French prison in 1803, but the French army was destroyed by yellow fever and by a renewed war of liberation led by Jean-Jacques Dessalines. On 1 January 1804 the victors declared the independence of Haiti, the second independent state in the Americas and the first founded by a successful revolution of enslaved people. Haiti was isolated by the slaveholding powers, and in 1825 France demanded a crushing indemnity for recognition.

## How historians argue about it

**Furet and the political dynamic.** — In *Interpreting the French Revolution* (1978), François Furet argued against the older view, associated with Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul, that the Revolution was a bourgeois revolution driven by class conflict. He read it as a political and ideological event: once the monarchy's authority collapsed, a new conception of legitimacy — the indivisible general will — made compromise impossible, and the Terror was built into the Revolution's own politics rather than forced by war. His reading is much contested; critics reply that war and counter-revolution were not incidental.

**James and the Black Jacobins.** — In *The Black Jacobins* (1938), C. L. R. James placed the Haitian Revolution at the centre of the age, arguing that the enslaved people of Saint-Domingue were not passive victims but actors who seized the revolutionary language of rights and made it their own, and that Toussaint Louverture was a leader of the first rank. Later historians have debated his emphasis on Toussaint over the mass movement, and the link between events in the colony and politics in France.

## Questions to carry

- The crown's problem in 1788 was a debt it could not service and a tax system it could not reform. At what point did a financial crisis become a revolution?
- The Declaration says "men are born and remain free and equal in rights." How did its authors define who counted as a citizen, and how did Olympe de Gouges and the free people of colour of Saint-Domingue use the same words against them?
- Was the Terror the product of war and counter-revolution, or of the Revolution's own idea of a single general will?
- The Code Napoléon preserved equality before the law and made women subordinate to their husbands. Which of those outcomes should count as the "real" revolution?
- Napoleon was defeated in 1815, but his Code, his prefects, and his centralised state survived. What does that tell us about the difference between defeating a man and undoing an institution?
- The Congress of Vienna restored monarchs but accepted many revolutionary changes. Was the settlement a restoration, a compromise, or something new?
- The Haitian Revolution abolished slavery and founded a state. Why did the European powers treat it so differently from the revolutions in France and America?
