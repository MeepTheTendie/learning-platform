# Early India and China

**Period:** c. 2600–200 BCE · **Region:** Indus · Ganges · Yellow River

## The Indus cities

**The river and the cities.** — The Indus runs 3,200 kilometres from Tibet and Kashmir to the Arabian Sea, fed by snowmelt and a summer monsoon then stronger than today's. Its cities — Harappa on the Ravi, Mohenjo-daro on the Indus, Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi, Lothal — lay near rivers or seasonal channels. The civilization covered about 1.25 million square kilometres, more than Egypt and Mesopotamia together, and more than a thousand settlements are known. Its people farmed wheat and barley, grew the world's first cotton, and herded cattle.

**Brick, grid, and drains.** — Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were laid out on a grid, with courtyard houses, a raised walled citadel, and a lower town. Houses had private wells and, often, a bathroom whose chute emptied into a covered drain under the street; the drains are the wonder of the site. Bricks were almost uniform in a 1:2:4 ratio, roughly 7 by 14 by 28 centimetres, and chert weights followed a binary and decimal system. Standardizing bricks and weights across a million square kilometres required authority, whatever form it took.

**The Great Bath, seals, and script.** — In the citadel at Mohenjo-daro stood the Great Bath, a pool about 12 by 7 metres and 2.4 metres deep with steps, a bitumen floor, and a drain. Its purpose is unknown, and the nearby "Great Granary" is equally disputed. The citadel has no agreed temple, royal tomb, or palace, and the cities have yielded no reliefs of kings and very few weapons; some read this as a society without a strong central state, others as proof of an authority unlike Mesopotamia's. Some four thousand objects also carry writing, mostly small steatite seals cut with an animal and a short line of signs. The signs number about four hundred, run mostly right to left, and the longest text is only about twenty signs; short texts, no bilingual key, and an unknown language have defeated every decipherment. The seals stamped clay tags and bales, suggesting trade; a few reached Ur, whose texts call the land Meluhha, and Indus beads, cotton, and ivory reached the Persian Gulf.

**The decline.** — After about 1900 BCE the cities emptied: drains fell out of use, trade faded, and people moved into smaller villages to the east and south. The Ghaggar-Hakra dried as its Himalayan feeders shifted, the monsoon weakened after about 2200 BCE, the Indus may have flooded or changed course, and trade with Mesopotamia collapsed. Most archaeologists now treat the end as a combination of climate and river change, not conquest; the old "Aryan invasion" theory has been largely abandoned, since there are no burned cities and no destruction layer. The people dispersed, and their descendants, mixed with newcomers, became part of the population of the Ganges plain.

## The Vedic age

**The Rigveda.** — India's oldest text is a collection of hymns: 1,028 of them in ten books, about 10,600 verses, composed by priests for the sacrificial fire and transmitted orally with great precision before being written down. The oldest books are dated to about 1500–1200 BCE. The hymns belong to a world of cattle, horses, and chariots, of chiefs and priests — a wandering, warlike people who called themselves arya, "noble" or "kinsman," speaking an Indo-European language related to Old Iranian, Greek, and Latin.

**A language and a migration.** — The kinship of Sanskrit with the languages of Europe was recognized in 1786 by Sir William Jones. Most linguists hold that Proto-Indo-European was spoken on the steppe north of the Black and Caspian Seas, and that one branch moved south-east into Iran and India. Archaeologists point to the Bactria-Margiana oasis and the Sintashta and Andronovo cultures, with their spoke-wheeled chariots, as the likely source of the Indo-Aryan speakers who entered the subcontinent between roughly 2000 and 1500 BCE. Genetic studies since 2019 have found steppe ancestry arriving in India in the second millennium BCE and mixing with the Indus population. The debate is unsettled and politically charged: some Indian writers reject any migration, while others accept the evidence but dispute its scale and timing. "Aryan" here is a linguistic and cultural label, not a race; its use as a racial category in Europe was a later and false construction.

**Varna and the body of Purusha.** — Vedic society was not yet the caste system of later India, but a ranked order appears in the hymns. The Purusha Sukta, Rigveda 10.90, tells how the gods sacrificed a primal man, Purusha, and made the world from his body. From his mouth came the Brahmin, the priest; from his arms the Rajanya, the warrior; from his thighs the Vaishya, the herder; from his feet the Shudra, the servant. The hymn is probably a late addition, but its image is powerful: society is one body, its parts unequal. Outside the four varnas stood the dasas and dasis, slaves or captives. In time the varna order hardened, and the separate system of jati, thousands of birth groups, grew up alongside it.

**Sacrifice, the move east, and the Upanishads.** — At the centre of Vedic religion stood the sacrifice, the yajna, offered on a fire altar by priests who knew the formulas and metres. Agni the fire carried the offering to the gods; Soma, a plant pressed into a sacred drink, gave strength and vision; Indra fought drought; Varuna watched over oaths. Correctly performed, the sacrifice sustained the cosmic order, rita, and its priests grew wealthy; a great king might perform the ashvamedha, the horse sacrifice. By about 1000 BCE the Vedic people had moved into the Ganges-Yamuna plain, clearing forests with iron tools; rice became a staple and herders gave way to kingdoms and towns. Alongside the ritual texts appeared the Upanishads, "sittings near" a teacher, composed between about 800 and 400 BCE. They ask not what should be sacrificed but what the self is. Their answer is that the deepest self, the atman, is identical with the ultimate reality, the brahman, and that to know this is release from the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth — samsara — driven by the consequences of one's actions, karma. The Katha Upanishad puts it in an image: "Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins."

## The Buddha and the Maurya empire

**Cities of the Ganges.** — In the sixth century BCE the Ganges plain saw a second wave of urbanization. Fortified towns rose along the rivers, and the map filled with the mahajanapadas, the "great states," sixteen of which are named in Buddhist texts. The strongest lay in the east: Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, and above all Magadha, in what is now Bihar, whose capital Pataliputra would become the seat of an empire. Some states were monarchies; some, such as the Vajji confederacy at Vaishali, were republics governed by a council of clansmen.

**The Buddha.** — Siddhartha Gautama was born, probably in the middle of the fifth century BCE, into the Shakya clan at Lumbini, near the border of what is now Nepal; the traditional dates are c. 563–483 BCE, though many historians prefer c. 480–400 BCE. At twenty-nine, troubled by old age, sickness, and death, he left his palace, studied with two teachers, practised extreme asceticism for six years, and then, sitting under a tree at Bodh Gaya, reached enlightenment. His first sermon, at Sarnath near Varanasi, taught the Four Noble Truths: that life is marked by suffering, that suffering has a cause, that it can end, and that there is a path to its ending — the Noble Eightfold Path. He taught a Middle Way between indulgence and self-mortification, denied that the self is permanent, and said the goal, nirvana, is the end of craving and rebirth. His followers formed the sangha, the community of monks and nuns, and recited his teachings for centuries before they were written down. He did not claim to be a god, and his teaching spread across Asia.

**The Jains.** — A near-contemporary, Vardhamana Mahavira, traditionally dated 599–527 BCE, was the twenty-fourth teacher of a movement that became Jainism. He taught an extreme non-violence, ahimsa, and an ascetic discipline aimed at freeing the soul from past action. Like the Buddhists, the Jains rejected the authority of the Vedas and the killing of animals in sacrifice, and they too attracted merchants and town-dwellers. Both movements challenged the ritual order of the brahmins, but neither set out to abolish social hierarchy.

**Alexander and Chandragupta.** — In 326 BCE Alexander the Great crossed the Indus, defeated king Porus at the Hydaspes, and marched east until his exhausted army refused to go further at the Beas. Chandragupta Maurya, helped by a brahmin adviser remembered as Kautilya or Chanakya, overthrew the Nanda dynasty of Magadha and by about 321 BCE ruled the Ganges plain. He drove the Greeks from the Indus valley and, when Seleucus, one of Alexander's successors, tried to recover them, defeated him and made a treaty about 305 BCE, taking the lands west of the Indus and giving Seleucus five hundred elephants. He ruled from Pataliputra a territory reaching from the Bay of Bengal to the Arabian Sea. The Greek ambassador Megasthenes wrote an account of his court, the Indica, which survives only in fragments; Kautilya is credited with the Arthashastra, a manual of statecraft whose date is debated.

**Ashoka and dhamma.** — Chandragupta's grandson Ashoka came to the throne about 268 BCE. In his eighth year, about 261 BCE, he conquered Kalinga in a war whose slaughter he later said filled him with remorse. From then on he presented his rule as a mission of dhamma — duty, law, virtue, or the teaching of the Buddha — and had his message carved on rocks and polished pillars across the empire, in local languages and scripts. The edicts ask people to honour parents, respect teachers, be truthful, refrain from killing living creatures, and show tolerance toward one another's sects. Ashoka banned animal sacrifice in the capital, gave up the royal hunt for pilgrimage, and sent missions to Sri Lanka. The edicts are the best source for his reign and the king's own words: they tell us what he wanted his subjects to believe, not what they believed. He died about 232 BCE, the empire weakened, and about 185 BCE a general named Pushyamitra Shunga killed the last Maurya king and founded a new dynasty. The lion capital Ashoka set up at Sarnath is today the emblem of the Republic of India.

## The Shang and the oracle bones

**The Yellow River and the Shang.** — The Yellow River, the Huang He, rises on the Tibetan plateau and cuts through the loess plateau of north China, where wind-blown soil lies ready to be carried away. The river picks up yellow silt until it is one of the muddiest in the world and drops it on the North China plain, building up its bed until the water runs above the land; when it breaks its banks it changes course by hundreds of kilometres, and the Chinese call it "China's Sorrow." The first Chinese dynasty with contemporary evidence is the Shang, which ruled from about 1600 to 1046 BCE. Its last capital, at Anyang in Henan, was occupied from about 1300 BCE and has yielded the royal cemetery with its huge pit graves, palaces and temples, bronze workshops, and thousands of inscribed bones. The Shang kings ruled a network of allied lords rather than a centralized state. Their bronze-casting was the finest in the ancient world, and kings and lords were buried with chariots, bronze weapons, and human victims. The tomb of Fu Hao, a royal consort, found intact in 1976, held some two thousand objects and sixteen human skeletons.

**Oracle bones.** — The most revealing Shang documents are not books but bones. To ask a question of the ancestors or gods, a diviner took a turtle plastron or an ox shoulder blade, hollowed the back, and applied a hot rod until cracks appeared on the front; the pattern was read as an answer, and on many bones the question was then written, sometimes with the outcome: "Will it rain?" "Will the king's wife give birth safely?" "Shall we attack the Qiang?" These inscriptions are the earliest Chinese writing, ancestors of the characters still in use. Some 150,000 fragments are known, carrying about 4,500 distinct characters, of which scholars have deciphered roughly 1,500. The bones were recognized as ancient in 1899, when a scholar named Wang Yirong noticed inscriptions on "dragon bones" sold as medicine; their source, Anyang, was then identified and excavated. Because they record the questions the king thought worth asking, they open a window on the court — but a narrow one, for they are elite ritual records, not ordinary life.

**The king and the ancestors.** — Shang religion centred on the ancestors and on a high god, Di, who could send rain, harvests, and disaster. The king was the chief diviner, the one who could approach the ancestors and Di for the community, and the sacrifices he offered bound the living to the dead. The Shang sacrificed animals and humans on a large scale, and kept a careful calendar with a ten-day week and a sixty-day cycle. Government, religion, and the family were not separate spheres; the ancestors were the court behind the court.

## The Zhou and the Mandate of Heaven

**The conquest and the Mandate.** — The Shang were overthrown by the Zhou, who lived in the Wei river valley to the west. In about 1046 BCE, at Muye, the Zhou king Wu defeated the last Shang king, Di Xin, and took the capital. The Zhou were smaller and less sophisticated than the Shang, and they faced the conqueror's problem: how to justify overthrowing a long-established dynasty. Their answer was the Mandate of Heaven. Heaven, tian, grants the right to rule to the virtuous and takes it from the corrupt; the Shang had lost the Mandate, tianming, because their last kings were cruel, and the Zhou had won it. The doctrine set a standard for the future: a bad ruler could be said to have forfeited the Mandate, and rebellion justified as Heaven's withdrawal of favour. It appears in the Book of Documents and in the Analects, where the founder of the Shang says: "If, in my person, I commit offences, they are not to be attributed to you, the people of the myriad regions. If you in the myriad regions commit offences, these offences must rest on my person." The ruler is responsible for the people; the people are not responsible for the ruler.

**The Duke of Zhou and the move east.** — King Wu died soon after the conquest, and his young son became king. The boy's uncle, the Duke of Zhou, took charge as regent, put down a rebellion, and then handed power back when the king came of age; he became the model of the loyal minister, and Confucius looked back to him as the ideal. The Zhou enfeoffed relatives and allies as lords, a system later called fengjian, sometimes translated "feudalism," and the rituals — li — governing mourning, sacrifice, marriage, and the court became the framework of society. In 771 BCE the capital was sacked and the king killed; his successor moved the court east to Luoyang, where it survived five more centuries with little real power. The period after the move is the Eastern Zhou; its first part, 771 to 476 BCE, is the Spring and Autumn period. It was in this fragmented world, not a secure empire, that the great Chinese thinkers lived and taught.

## The Warring States and the thinkers

**The seven states.** — The second half of the Eastern Zhou, from 475 to 221 BCE, is the Warring States period. The many lordships consolidated into seven great states — Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Zhao, Wei, and Han — which fought for survival. Armies grew from a few thousand chariot-borne nobles to hundreds of thousands of infantry; iron replaced bronze; the crossbow and the cavalry horse, adopted from the steppe, changed war; and the state that could mobilize the most men and grain survived.

**Confucius.** — The most influential teacher of the age was born in 551 BCE in the small state of Lu, in what is now Shandong, and died in 479 BCE. Kong Qiu, known in the West as Confucius, came from a poor but respectable family, held minor offices, and spent much of his life as a teacher. He claimed no new doctrine; he said he was transmitting the wisdom of the ancients, and he looked back to the early Zhou as a golden age. He gathered disciples, by tradition some three thousand, and taught the qualities of the junzi, the "gentleman" — not a man of noble birth but of moral cultivation. The central virtue was ren, benevolence; it was expressed through li, ritual and propriety; and it began at home, with xiao, filial piety. He held that a ruler should govern by moral example rather than force: "If the people be led by laws, and uniformity sought to be given them by punishments, they will try to avoid the punishment, but have no sense of shame. If they be led by virtue, and uniformity sought to be given them by the rules of propriety, they will have the sense of shame, and moreover will become good." He taught the "rectification of names" — a father should act as a father, a ruler as a ruler — and summarized his ethic negatively: "not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself."

**The Analects, Mencius, and Xunzi.** — Confucius wrote nothing. After his death his disciples collected his sayings into the Analects, twenty short chapters compiled over several generations. It is not a system but a portrait, which makes it vivid and also uncertain: we cannot be sure which sayings go back to Confucius. The tradition he founded, the Ru or "scholars," is known in the West as Confucianism. Two later thinkers defined its wings. Mencius, about 372–289 BCE, argued that human nature is good and that a ruler who does not care for the people loses the Mandate and may be removed: "The people are the most important element in a nation, the spirits of the land and grain are the next, the sovereign is the lightest." Xunzi, about 310–235 BCE, held that human nature is bad and can be made good only by ritual, education, and law.

**Laozi and the Daodejing.** — The other great tradition of the age is Daoism. Its founding figure is said to be Laozi, an older contemporary of Confucius, but the historical Laozi may never have existed, and the book attributed to him, the Daodejing, is a composite work of about 5,000 characters in eighty-one short chapters, put together probably in the fourth or third century BCE. It teaches the Dao, "the Way," the nameless source of all things, and wuwei, "non-action," the art of acting without forcing and governing without interfering: "Govern a large state as you would cook a small fish," runs one saying. Daoism distrusts the busy reformer, prizes simplicity, and imagines a small community content without ambition. It offered a very different picture of a good ruler from the one Confucius drew, and the two traditions argued for centuries.

**The Hundred Schools.** — Mozi, about 470–391 BCE, taught "universal love" and opposed offensive war. The Legalists, whose greatest theorist was Han Feizi, argued that government should rest on clear laws and rewards and punishments, not the ruler's moral example, and wrote for rulers who wanted power. There were also the Logicians and the Yin-Yang school. The Chinese call this ferment the "Hundred Schools of Thought," and it arose, as such movements often do, from a long political breakdown.

## The Qin unification

**Shang Yang's Qin.** — The state that ended the warring states was Qin, in the west. About 356 and 350 BCE a minister named Shang Yang carried out reforms that turned Qin into a disciplined military machine. He reorganized the population into groups responsible for one another, rewarded soldiers with ranks and land for heads taken in battle, broke up the old aristocratic families, encouraged farming, and applied the law to noble and commoner alike. The reforms were harsh and effective, and Qin grew stronger than its rivals.

**The first emperor.** — Between 230 and 221 BCE the king of Qin, Zheng, conquered the other six states one by one. In 221 BCE he took the title Qin Shi Huangdi, the "First August Emperor," and set out to make the empire one thing: he standardized writing, weights, measures, and coinage; standardized the width of cart axles; divided the empire into commanderies and counties governed by appointed officials rather than hereditary lords; and connected the northern walls of earlier states into the long fortification later called the Great Wall. He built roads and canals and toured his realm, setting up stone inscriptions to announce his achievements. His tomb, near modern Xi'an, is guarded by the terracotta army — thousands of life-sized soldiers, horses, and chariots in battle order, discovered in 1974.

**The cost and the collapse.** — The first emperor also suppressed criticism. Tradition says he burned books other than those on practical subjects and executed hundreds of scholars; the story comes mainly from the Han dynasty that followed, and its details are debated, but the Qin state was harsh. The emperor died in 210 BCE, and his empire fell apart within four years. In 206 BCE the capital was taken, and by 202 BCE a commoner named Liu Bang had founded the Han dynasty. The Qin lasted fifteen years, but the centralized empire they created outlasted them. The endpoint of this account, 221 BCE, is the moment when the many states of the Yellow River and the Yangtze were welded into one.

## Chronology

- **c. 2600–1900 BCE** — The Mature Harappan cities of the Indus flourish; seals, standard weights and bricks, the Great Bath, and the undeciphered script.
- **c. 2200–1900 BCE** — The Ghaggar-Hakra dries and the Indus cities decline and empty.
- **c. 2000–1500 BCE** — Speakers of Indo-Aryan enter the subcontinent from the north-west.
- **c. 1600–1046 BCE** — The Shang dynasty rules the Yellow River plain.
- **c. 1500–1200 BCE** — The oldest hymns of the Rigveda are composed in the Punjab.
- **c. 1300 BCE** — Oracle-bone divination at Anyang; the earliest Chinese writing.
- **c. 1046 BCE** — The Zhou defeat the Shang at Muye and claim the Mandate of Heaven.
- **c. 1000–600 BCE** — The Vedic people settle the Ganges plain; the early Upanishads are composed.
- **771 BCE** — The Zhou court moves east to Luoyang, beginning the Eastern Zhou.
- **c. 600 BCE** — A second wave of cities and states grows in the Ganges plain.
- **c. 563–483 BCE** — Traditional dates for the life of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha.
- **551–479 BCE** — Confucius lives in the state of Lu.
- **c. 475–221 BCE** — The Warring States period; the "Hundred Schools of Thought."
- **326 BCE** — Alexander the Great crosses the Indus and turns back at the Beas.
- **c. 321 BCE** — Chandragupta Maurya founds the Maurya empire in Magadha.
- **c. 305 BCE** — Chandragupta defeats Seleucus west of the Indus.
- **c. 261 BCE** — Ashoka conquers Kalinga and adopts the policy of dhamma.
- **c. 257 BCE** — Ashoka's edicts are carved on rock and pillars across the empire.
- **c. 232 BCE** — Ashoka dies; the Maurya empire begins to break up.
- **221 BCE** — Qin Shi Huangdi unifies the warring states and founds the Chinese empire.
- **c. 185 BCE** — The last Maurya king is killed; the Shunga dynasty replaces the Mauryas.
- **206–202 BCE** — The Qin fall; Liu Bang founds the Han dynasty.

## Questions to carry

- The Indus cities had drains, standard bricks, and a script, but no clear kings or temples. What kind of authority builds and maintains a city without leaving a royal image?
- The Rigveda's Hymn 90 makes the four social orders parts of one body. What does that image assume, and how would the world look to someone born at the feet?
- Ashoka's edicts and Confucius's sayings both describe how a ruler should behave. Where do they agree, and what does each leave out?
- The Zhou claimed that Heaven grants rule to the virtuous. Who decides when a ruler has lost the Mandate, and what does the answer mean for rebellion?
- India and China developed writing, cities, and empires on separate rivers at the same time. What is gained, and what is lost, by telling their histories together?
