# Governing Rome's empire

**Period:** c. 27 BCE–476 CE · **Region:** Mediterranean · Rhine · Danube

## The settlement of 27 BCE

**A republic that had failed.** — Julius Caesar was killed in 44 BCE by men who said they were saving the republic. Instead came thirteen more years of civil war. Octavian, Caesar's adopted son, defeated Brutus and Cassius, then Mark Antony, and after the naval battle at Actium in 31 BCE was the only power left in the Roman world. Romans had no word for a legitimate monarch and hated the word *rex*, king; a man who openly took a crown would be killed as Caesar had been. So Octavian did not take one. He invented a way of holding power that looked like the old republic and was not.

**The settlement.** — On 13 January 27 BCE Octavian laid down the extraordinary powers he had held since the wars and returned the state to the senate and people. The senate, unwilling to try governing without him, gave them back in a new form. It granted him the name Augustus; a great *provincia* — Spain, Gaul, and Syria — to command for ten years; and kept him consul. In 23 BCE he gave up the consulship and received instead two powers for life: the tribunician power, which let him summon the senate and veto its acts, and a greater proconsular *imperium*, command over the armies. The settlement was a bundle of old offices, not a new throne.

**What the princeps was.** — Augustus called himself *princeps*, "first man" or "first citizen," and the regime is called the principate. In the *Res Gestae*, the account of his deeds cut on bronze pillars outside his mausoleum, he wrote that he had "transferred the commonwealth from my own dominion to the authority of the senate and Roman people," and that "after that time I excelled all others in dignity, but of power I held no more than those also held who were my colleagues in any magistracy." Both clauses are true and both conceal the army: he held no more power than a colleague, but he held the provinces where the legions stood.

**The legions.** — After Actium, Augustus kept twenty-eight legions, about 150,000 men, with a similar number of auxiliaries raised from peoples without citizenship. Legions were professional, not a citizens' levy: men served sixteen years, later twenty-five, drew a wage, and expected a lump sum on discharge. In 6 CE Augustus created the military treasury, the *aerarium militare*, funded by a five per cent inheritance tax, a one per cent auction tax, and a large gift of his own. The army's loyalty was bought with regular pay, and the emperor, who controlled the pay, became its patron.

**The praetorians.** — Nine cohorts of picked troops, the praetorian guard, guarded the emperor in Italy. Their commander, the praetorian prefect, was an equestrian rather than a senator, and so could be trusted with arms near the capital. The prefect Sejanus controlled Rome in the late 20s CE; in 41 CE the guard killed Caligula and found Claudius hiding behind a curtain and made him emperor; in 193 CE it auctioned the throne. The guard was the price of protecting a monarchy that would not admit it was one.

**The provinces and the census.** — A *provincia* first meant the task assigned to a magistrate; under the empire it came to mean a subject territory. Augustus divided the provinces between those the senate governed through proconsuls and those he governed through legates, his deputies, with equestrian *procurators* handling the money. Egypt he kept apart, ruled by an equestrian prefect and closed to senators, because it fed Rome. The empire was assessed for tax by a census, and Augustus took three. The *Res Gestae* records the citizens counted: about four million in 28 BCE, four million two hundred thousand in 8 BCE, and four million nine hundred thousand in 14 CE. These count adult male citizens only, but they show a state that counted the people it taxed.

## The high empire

**The Pax Romana.** — The two centuries from Augustus to Marcus Aurelius are often called the Pax Romana, the Roman peace. Rome fought wars of conquest in Britain, Dacia, and Mesopotamia, and crushed revolts with great violence, above all in Judea. But within the Mediterranean the great rival powers were gone, piracy was suppressed, and a merchant could move goods from Syria to Spain under one legal system and one currency. Edward Gibbon, writing in the 1770s, called the second century the period in which "the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous."

**The frontiers.** — The army's real work was the frontier. Legions stood along the Rhine and the Danube, on the Euphrates facing Persia, and in Britain, with smaller garrisons inside. Hadrian, who toured the provinces and built the wall across northern Britain in 122 CE, fixed a policy of defence in depth rather than endless expansion. Forts, roads, and watchtowers along the *limes*, the frontier line, made the army a permanent presence. In many frontier districts the legion was the most effective government a person would ever meet.

**Roads, ports, and the grain fleet.** — Rome governed through movement. About 80,000 kilometres of paved road and a far larger network of tracks tied the provinces to the capital, and the *cursus publicus*, the imperial post, carried officials and dispatches. The sea carried more. Rome's grain dole fed perhaps 200,000 people, and the grain came from Egypt and North Africa through the harbour at Ostia, where Trajan built a great hexagonal basin, the Portus. Oil, wine, and fish sauce travelled in stamped clay amphorae, so a potsherd can show where a jar was filled and where it was emptied.

**The cities.** — The empire was a network of cities before it was a state. Conquered towns became *municipia* with their own magistrates, or *coloniae* settled by veterans, and their leading men, the decurions, ran local affairs, collected taxes, and paid for buildings, games, and baths from their own fortunes. This competition for local honour, called euergetism, raised the theatres and aqueducts that still stand from Spain to Syria. Ostia, the port of Rome, and Pompeii, buried by Vesuvius in 79 CE, preserve ordinary streets, shops, and bars. In the provinces, cities like Lepcis Magna in Africa and Ephesus in Asia were rebuilt in Roman form while keeping their own gods and languages.

**Taxation and the colonus.** — The empire ran on taxes: a land tax and a poll tax paid in money or kind, customs dues, a five per cent tax on inheritances, and a tax on freeing slaves. Collection was once farmed to private companies, the *publicani*, hated for their greed; under the empire, provincial *procurators* and city councils took over, but the burden remained. Land was worked by free farmers, by slaves, and by tenants. A *colonus* was a tenant farmer who rented land and paid rent, often a share of the crop. In the later empire, laws bound many *coloni* to the land and restricted their movement, a condition historians call the colonate. Whether this was a new serfdom or the hardening of an old tenancy is debated.

**Succession and its dangers.** — The principate never solved the succession. Augustus had no son and adopted Tiberius; the Julio-Claudian line ended in 68 CE with Nero, and the next year four men in four places fought for the throne. The Flavians and then the Antonines tried adoption, choosing a capable heir rather than a son, and the second century was unusually stable. But in 180 CE Marcus Aurelius left the empire to his son Commodus, and in 193 the army again chose the emperor.

## Law, citizenship, and belonging

**The jurists and the law.** — Rome's most durable export was law. The *ius civile* was the law of citizens; the *ius gentium*, the law of peoples, grew from rules used in dealing with foreigners; the *ius honorarium* was the body of remedies built up by the praetor. From Augustus's reign professional jurists wrote opinions, and under the empire their responses carried the emperor's authority. Gaius wrote an elementary textbook, the *Institutes*, about 160 CE; Papinian, Ulpian, and Paul were the great names of the Severan age. Their distinctions between persons, things, and actions outlived the empire and were gathered into the codes of Theodosius and Justinian.

**Tiers of belonging.** — Citizenship was not one thing but a ladder. A Roman citizen had the vote, the right to marry and to make a will under Roman law, and protection from degrading punishment. Below citizens stood the Latins, with some rights, and the *peregrini*, free provincials who lived under their own law. At the bottom were the *dediticii*, peoples who had surrendered and had almost no rights. The ladder was also a machine: a man who served twenty-five years in an auxiliary regiment received citizenship for himself and his children, and his bronze discharge diploma recorded the grant. In this way the army turned soldiers of conquered peoples into Romans one generation at a time.

**Caracalla, 212.** — In 212 CE the emperor Caracalla issued the Antonine Constitution, granting Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. The usual explanation comes from the historian Cassius Dio, a senator who disliked Caracalla: he did it to increase the number of people who paid the inheritance tax and other taxes that fell on citizens. The edict was less a gift than a fiscal measure, but its effects were enormous. Millions became Roman citizens at once, subject to Roman law and in principle to the same courts. After 212 the legal line between conqueror and conquered was largely gone.

**A machine for assimilation?** — Did Rome turn the peoples it ruled into Romans? The old answer was Romanization: provincials learned Latin, built temples to Roman gods, and took Roman names. But the same record shows what survived. Gaulish, Punic, and Greek were still spoken; local gods were worshipped under Roman names or beside them; and the eastern half, which kept Greek for government, remained culturally Greek rather than Latin. Historians now describe a two-way traffic, in which Rome absorbed as much as it imposed. To be "Roman" was itself changed by the peoples who became Roman.

## Religion, the imperial cult, and Christianity

**Gods, sacrifice, and the state.** — Roman religion had no creed and no sacred book. It worked by sacrifice. To refuse the sacrifice was not to hold a different opinion but to withdraw loyalty, which is why religion and politics were never separate. The emperor was *pontifex maximus*, chief priest, and after death the senate might declare him a god, a *divus*. In the provinces, assemblies called *koina* built temples to Rome and the emperor and held festivals in his honour.

**Judea.** — The Jews of Judea were an exception the empire never quite managed. Their God was one and would not share, and their refusal to sacrifice to other gods marked them as odd and, to some, seditious. In 66 CE a revolt broke out; in 70 CE Roman armies under Titus took Jerusalem and destroyed the temple, the centre of Jewish worship. The emperor imposed a special tax, the *fiscus Iudaicus*, on Jews throughout the empire. A second great revolt under Bar Kokhba was crushed in 132–135 CE, after which Hadrian refounded Jerusalem as a Roman colony, Aelia Capitolina, and barred Jews from the city. Judaism survived, but its centre shifted from the temple to the synagogue and the law.

**Jesus and Paul.** — Christianity began as a movement within Judaism. Its central figure, Jesus of Nazareth, preached in Galilee and Judea and was crucified about 30 CE by the Roman prefect of Judea, Pontius Pilate. The movement might have stayed a Jewish sect had not Paul of Tarsus, a Jew and a Roman citizen, carried it to non-Jews across the Greek-speaking cities of the east. The earliest Christian writings are Paul's letters, from the 50s CE; the gospels came a generation or more later. All are sources written by believers and must be read as such.

**Persecution, sporadic and general.** — For two centuries the new religion was illegal in principle and mostly ignored in practice. Nero blamed Christians for the fire at Rome in 64 CE and killed many. About 112 CE Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, wrote to Trajan to ask how to try them; Trajan replied that they should not be sought out, but if accused and convicted should be punished, and that anonymous informers should be ignored. Persecution became general only when emperors ordered everyone to sacrifice and prove it with a certificate: Decius in 250 CE, Valerian in 257, and Diocletian, whose Great Persecution began in 303, the most systematic of all. Christians who died were called martyrs, and their deaths were remembered as victories.

**Toleration and establishment.** — The persecution failed, and the empire changed sides. In 311 CE the dying emperor Galerius issued an edict of toleration; in 313 CE Constantine and Licinius granted Christians freedom of worship, an arrangement later called the Edict of Milan. Constantine favoured the church, gave it property and privileges, and summoned its bishops to the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE. His nephew Julian, emperor from 361 to 363, tried to restore the old gods and failed. The end came under Theodosius I, who in 380 CE made Nicene Christianity the religion of the empire and in 391–392 forbade public pagan sacrifice. Within four centuries a persecuted sect had become the state church.

## The third-century crisis and the remaking of the empire

**The crisis, 235–284.** — In 235 CE soldiers murdered the emperor Severus Alexander and began half a century of chaos. Twenty or more men claimed the throne; almost all died by violence. Plague swept the empire in the 250s. The silver coinage was debased until it was mostly bronze with a wash of silver, and prices rose. Persia, under a new and aggressive dynasty, invaded from the east and in 260 CE captured the emperor Valerian. Goths and other peoples crossed the Danube and raided Greece and Asia. For a time the empire split into three: a Gallic empire in the west and the kingdom of Palmyra in the east. Aurelian, emperor from 270 to 275, reconquered both and built the walls of Rome that still stand.

**Diocletian's remedies.** — In 284 CE Diocletian, a soldier from Dalmatia, took power and rebuilt the state. He divided the empire for government into four, the tetrarchy: two senior emperors, the Augusti, and two juniors, the Caesars, each with a capital and a field army. He split the provinces into about a hundred smaller units grouped into twelve dioceses, so governors had less power to rebel. He enlarged the army and reformed taxes to be assessed on land and people and paid largely in kind. To keep farmers on the land and receipts steady, he bound many *coloni* to their fields. In 303 CE he ordered the Great Persecution. In 305 CE he did what no emperor had done: he retired.

**Constantine.** — Diocletian's system did not outlast him. By 312 CE Constantine, son of a tetrarch, was fighting for the west; before the battle at the Milvian Bridge he is said to have seen a sign in the sky and fought under the Christian God. He ruled alone from 324 CE. He moved the capital east, founding Constantinople on the site of Byzantium in 330 CE. He minted a stable gold coin, the *solidus*, which held its value for centuries, and gave the church money, land, and legal privileges. Baptized only on his deathbed in 337 CE, he left a Christian empire.

**Why the East held.** — The eastern empire was richer than the west, and that shaped everything. Its cities were larger and more numerous, its agriculture more productive, its tax base deeper. Its frontier facing Persia was shorter and could be held with fewer troops, while the west had to defend the long Rhine and Danube line against many peoples. When the empire was divided for good in 395 CE, the eastern half had the money and the manpower. This is the structural reason the eastern empire survived the shocks that destroyed the western imperial office.

## The fourth century and the end in the west

**Julian and the last pagan revival.** — Constantine's family destroyed itself, and in 361 CE the throne passed to his nephew Julian, a serious man who had secretly abandoned Christianity. Julian tried to rebuild the old cults, restore the temples, and copy the church's charitable institutions. He reigned twenty months and was killed on campaign in Persia in 363 CE. His attempt shows how open the outcome still was: had he reigned as long as Diocletian, the religious history of the empire might have run differently.

**Adrianople, 378.** — In 376 CE a people called the Goths, pressed from behind by the Huns, asked to cross the Danube into the empire. Roman officials let them in, then treated them so badly that they revolted. In 378 CE the eastern emperor Valens met them near Adrianople and was killed with much of his army, the worst Roman defeat since Hannibal. The historian Ammianus Marcellinus, who had fought in the eastern wars, described the Huns as "savage beyond all parallel," a people who scarred their children's faces, lived on horseback, and had no king. His account tells us as much about Roman fear as about the Huns.

**Theodosius and the division.** — Theodosius I made peace with the Goths in 382 CE, settling them in the empire as allies, *foederati*, who fought under their own leaders. He made Nicene Christianity the state religion and banned pagan sacrifice. When he died in 395 CE he left the empire to his two sons, Arcadius in the east and Honorius in the west, and the division became permanent. The west had the weaker army and the poorer treasury just when its frontiers were most exposed.

**Alaric and 410.** — The Gothic leader Alaric, who had once served in the Roman army, marched on Rome and in 410 CE sacked the city, which had not fallen to an enemy for eight hundred years. From it came Augustine's *City of God*, which argued that the fall of an earthly city was not the fall of the Christian faith. Yet 410 was not the end. The western empire still had an army, a court, and provinces. What it lacked was the tax base to pay for them, and it was losing that base piece by piece as provinces passed out of its control.

**Attila and the Huns.** — The Huns reached their height under Attila, who ruled from 434 to 453 CE and extracted vast sums from the eastern empire. In 451 CE a Roman general, Aetius, with Visigothic and other allies, stopped Attila at the Catalaunian Plains in Gaul. Attila died two years later, and his empire of subject peoples dissolved. The Huns had terrified the empire and then vanished; their pressure pushed other peoples across the frontiers, and those peoples stayed.

**476.** — In 476 CE a barbarian officer in Roman service named Odoacer deposed the boy emperor Romulus Augustulus and sent the imperial regalia to the eastern emperor in Constantinople, saying that one emperor was enough. In 480 CE the last claimant, Julius Nepos, died in Dalmatia, and the western imperial office was at an end. But the senate still met, Roman law still ran, the bishops still preached, and the eastern empire still ruled from Constantinople. What ended in 476 was an office, not a civilization.

## What held the empire together, and what survived

**The mechanics of cohesion.** — The empire was held together by a loop of tax, army, and elite. The provinces paid taxes; the taxes paid the army; the army protected the provinces and enforced the taxes; and the local elites who collected the taxes were rewarded with citizenship, Roman law, and a share in imperial honours. Roads and sea routes carried the soldiers, officials, and goods that made the loop work. Shared citizenship, near-universal after 212, gave free inhabitants one legal identity. The cities gave the empire a thousand local governments, and religion — first the civic cults, then Christianity — gave it a common language of loyalty. Break any part and the whole grew weaker.

**Gibbon's answer.** — Edward Gibbon published the first volume of *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* in 1776. He found the causes of the fall in the loss of old civic virtue and, most famously, in the rise of Christianity, which he thought turned Romans from public life toward the next world. His account of Christianity has been contested ever since, and few now accept that a minority religion can bear the weight he put on it.

**The modern debate.** — The debate has not settled. Peter Heather argues in *The Fall of the Roman Empire* (2005) that the west fell from a combination of external shocks — above all the Huns, who drove other peoples across the frontier — and the internal weakness of a state that had lost the tax base to pay its army. Bryan Ward-Perkins, in *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* (2005), argues that the fall was a real catastrophe for ordinary people: pottery, coinage, trade, and literacy collapsed, and life became poorer and more dangerous. Peter Brown, in *The World of Late Antiquity* (1971), reads the same centuries as a time of religious and cultural creativity, not decline, and taught historians to speak of "late antiquity" rather than a fall. These are arguments, not facts, and the same evidence can serve more than one.

**What outlived the western office.** — The western imperial office ended, but most of what Rome built did not. Roman law survived in the codes: the Theodosian Code of 438 CE and, above all, the *Corpus Iuris Civilis* that Justinian compiled in 529–534 CE, which became a foundation of European law. The eastern empire, which we call Byzantium, continued until 1453. The Christian church outlived the state that had persecuted it and became the great institution of the Latin west, with the bishop of Rome at its head. Latin survived as the language of the church, the law, and learning. Cities shrank and trade declined, but did not disappear. Which parts of the empire were the office, and which were the world it governed?

## Questions to carry

- Augustus held no more power than a colleague, yet he ruled. Where did his power actually lie?
- The army, the roads, and the tax collector did more governing than any emperor. Which of the three mattered most, and how would we know?
- Trajan told Pliny not to seek out Christians but to punish those brought before him. What does that policy assume about the state's duty?
- Diocletian and Constantine rebuilt the state and split it. Which of their changes did most to decide that the east would outlast the west?
- Gibbon blamed Christianity; Heather blames barbarians and taxes; Brown sees not a fall but a change. Are these answers to the same question?
- In 476 an office ended, but law, cities, and the church continued. What, if anything, had really fallen?
