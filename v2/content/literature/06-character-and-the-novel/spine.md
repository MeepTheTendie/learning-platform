# Character and the novel

**Period:** The novel in English, eighteenth to early twentieth centuries

## A person made of words

**The organizing question.** — How does a novelist make a person out of words? Everything a reader knows about Elizabeth Bennet, Dorothea Brooke, or Clarissa Dalloway arrives as ink on a page, and yet readers grieve at their losses and quarrel over their choices as though they had met. A character has no body, no life between chapters, no memory except the memory the text chooses to report. The novelist cannot copy a person the way a painter copies a face; the novelist must build the impression of one from language — a name, a habit of speech, a few decisive actions, the verdict of a narrator, and an imagined access to another mind. The novel is the form in which that construction became the main business, and the three centuries from Defoe to Joyce are the record of writers discovering how much can be done with it.

**The problem stated plainly.** — A real person is known through a body, a voice, a history, and the guesses of others. A fictional person is known through none of these things directly, only through the words a writer puts down. The reader never sees the whole of a character's life; the text selects, and the selection is the art. One novelist may give a page to a single gesture and skip ten years in a sentence. Another may spend five hundred pages inside one day. The character that emerges is not a transcription of a person but an effect produced by these choices, and the reader supplies the rest by inference. The novelist's craft lies in making the inference feel like knowledge.

**The flat character and the round.** — E. M. Forster, in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), drew the distinction that still organises most talk about fictional persons. A flat character, he wrote, is constructed round a single idea or quality: the miser, the pedant, the woman whose whole being is devotion to her husband. Such a character can be summed up in a sentence and does not develop; the reader recognises the type at once and is never surprised. A round character is one who can surprise the reader in a way the reader finds convincing — who is capable of change, contradiction, and inward conflict, and whose actions cannot be predicted from a label. Forster took his examples of flatness from Dickens and named Mrs. Micawber, whose refrain "I never will desert Mr. Micawber" is the whole of her; he took his examples of roundness from Austen, Eliot, Dostoevsky, and Proust. The distinction is not a ranking. Forster insisted that flat characters are often the most memorable and that a novel can be great without a single round one. The terms are a tool, not a law, and later criticism has treated flatness as a matter of degree and function rather than a property a character either has or lacks.

**Why the question is contested.** — Two positions have divided readers. One treats characters as imitations of people, so that the test of a character is psychological truth and the reader's response is the proper subject of criticism. The other treats characters as devices in a verbal machine, so that talk of their inner lives is a category mistake. L. C. Knights made the second case in an essay of 1933 whose title — "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?" — mocked the habit of discussing fictional persons as though they had biographies. The argument has never been settled, and it returns whenever a reader feels that a character is real. It is worth holding both positions at once: the character is made of words, and the reader's experience of the character as a person is the point of the making.

## Before the novel: character as type

**Theophrastus.** — The oldest surviving book of character sketches is by Theophrastus, Aristotle's successor at the Lyceum, who wrote around 319 BCE. His *Characters* presents thirty short portraits — the flatterer, the boaster, the stingy man, the talkative man, the man of petty ambition — each defined by a single vice and illustrated by a list of habitual actions. The flatterer agrees with everything and laughs at his companion's worst jokes; the boaster tells strangers of the wealth he does not have. There is no development and no inner life. The type is the whole point, and the reader is meant to recognise the type in the street.

**Humours and the stage.** — Medieval and Renaissance drama kept the same logic. The morality plays made virtues and vices into walking abstractions, so that a figure called Good Deeds or Fellowship appeared on stage as an idea rather than a person. Ben Jonson's *Every Man in His Humour* (1598) drew on the older medical theory of the four bodily humours — blood, phlegm, choler, and melancholy — and gave each comic figure a temperament ruled by one of them. A man whose humour was unbalanced became a fixed type: the jealous husband, the braggart soldier, the miser. The audience knew what a character was before the character walked on.

**Why this matters.** — The early novel inherited this furniture and then broke with it. The novel kept the memorable single trait — Dickens never lost it — but added two things the older forms did not have: the particular individual in a particular time and place, and access to a mind that changes. That addition is the subject of the rest of this unit.

## The rise of the novel

**Defoe.** — Daniel Defoe published *Robinson Crusoe* in 1719 and *Moll Flanders* in 1722, and both are told in the first person by a narrator looking back on a life. Crusoe keeps a journal, inventories his stores, builds, plants, and survives; Moll recounts a life of theft, marriages, and reversals. In Defoe the self is made by action and by accounting. The reader knows Crusoe by what he does and what he owns far more than by what he feels, and the long accumulation of ordinary detail — the exact tools, the exact measurements, the exact sequence of days — is what gives the story its weight. Defoe's inwardness is thin by later standards, but his particularity is new: these are not types but named individuals whose lives unfold in time.

**Richardson.** — Samuel Richardson changed the method by changing the form. *Pamela* (1740) and *Clarissa* (1747–48) are novels in letters, written by the characters themselves, often "to the moment," so that the reader watches a mind react before it has had time to arrange its account. Pamela's letters to her parents record her fear and her virtue as she resists her master; Clarissa's long letters, and the letters of her friend Anna Howe, record the pressure that destroys her. The epistolary form gives what no earlier fiction had given at this length: continuous access to a character's inner life, minute by minute. Henry Fielding mocked the method in *Shamela* (1741), a parody of *Pamela*, and then wrote *Joseph Andrews* (1742), whose preface calls it "a comic epic poem in prose," and *Tom Jones* (1749).

**Fielding.** — Fielding's narrator is the opposite of Richardson's letter-writer: a learned, ironic showman who stands outside the action, knows everything, and tells the reader what to think. He names his characters by their qualities — Allworthy the good magistrate, Thwackum and Square the two tutors, one all rod and one all theory — and reveals them through comic action and self-deception. Where Richardson goes inward, Fielding goes outward and social, and his characters are known by what they do in the world. The two novelists define the poles between which the English novel has moved ever since: the mind within and the world without.

**Ian Watt's argument.** — Ian Watt, in *The Rise of the Novel* (1957), asked why this form appeared in England in the eighteenth century, and answered that a new philosophy and a new society had produced a new kind of person: an individual whose own experience was the source of meaning. Watt linked the novel to Locke's account of personal identity and to Descartes's solitary thinker, to the growth of a middle-class reading public with time and money for books, and to what he called "formal realism" — the convention of presenting the particulars of ordinary life in time and place as though they were true. Robinson Crusoe is his type: an individual alone, whose story is the record of his own experience. Watt's book is the most influential account of the novel's origins, and it is also contested. Later critics have pointed to prose fiction written before 1700, to the central role of women writers and readers, and to traditions outside England; others have argued that the "rise" is a story told backwards from a small canon of three men. The debate is itself evidence that the novel's origins are an argument, not a fact.

## Building a character: action, speech, and free indirect discourse

**Action.** — Aristotle argued in the *Poetics* that character is disclosed by action, and the novel inherited the idea. A character is what he or she does under pressure. Fitzwilliam Darcy is revealed by his behaviour at the Meryton ball, where he refuses to dance and calls Elizabeth tolerable, and then by his letter after the first proposal, which shows a man the reader had misjudged. Dorothea Brooke is revealed by her choice to marry Casaubon, a choice the reader sees as a mistake before she does. In each case the novelist does not tell the reader who the person is; the person is inferred from choices, and the reader's judgment is part of the experience.

**Speech.** — Dialogue and idiolect fix a character as firmly as any action. Dickens's people are known by their voices: Uriah Heep's whining "'umble," Mr. Micawber's confidence that "something will turn up," the Cockney of Sam Weller in *The Pickwick Papers* (1836–37). Speech carries class, region, education, and habit, and a single verbal tic can do the work of a paragraph of description. Austen distributes judgment through dialogue: Mr. Bennet's dry irony, Mrs. Bennet's breathless chatter about Netherfield and four thousand a year, and the narrator's own closing sentence, which tells the reader that Mr. Bennet is "so odd a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice" that his wife of three-and-twenty years has not understood his character, while hers is easy to read. The narrator can say in one sentence what a page of dialogue has shown, and the two together make the person.

**Free indirect discourse.** — The technique that most distinguishes the novel's handling of character is free indirect discourse: the narrator's third person blended with a character's idiom and thought, without quotation marks and without a phrase such as "she thought." The character's words surface in the narrator's voice, and the reader hears both at once. Jane Austen is the great early master. The first sentence of *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife" — sounds like an aphorism spoken by the narrator, but the following sentence reveals that this "truth" is what the surrounding families believe, and the irony lands on them. When Mrs. Bennet's excitement enters the text as "A single man of large fortune; four or five thousand a year. What a fine thing for our girls!" the sentence is grammatically the narrator's, but the eagerness and the vocabulary are hers. The reader receives her mind from the outside, with the narrator's judgment folded in.

**Naming.** — Names are devices too. Fielding's Allworthy and Thwackum, Dickens's Gradgrind and Bounderby and Scrooge, Bunyan's Christian: a name can be a verdict delivered before the character speaks. A neutral name leaves the reader free to judge; a loaded name forecloses judgment and turns the character toward allegory. The choice is a decision about how much the reader is trusted.

## The bildungsroman

**Formation.** — The German term *Bildungsroman*, "novel of formation," was coined in the 1820s by the critic Karl Morgenstern for Goethe's *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* (1795–96). The form tells the story of a young person's growth: a departure from home, an encounter with the world, a series of mistakes, a testing by love and by work, and an arrival at a settled relation to society. Character is not fixed at the start; the whole novel is the process of its forming. In English the great examples are Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* (1847), Dickens's *David Copperfield* (1850) and *Great Expectations* (1861), and George Eliot's *The Mill on the Floss* (1860). Each follows a child into adulthood and asks what the world will make of the person and the person of the world.

**The form as an argument.** — Jerome Buckley, in *Season of Youth* (1974), described the typical English pattern: a provincial childhood, a loss or a crisis, a departure for the city, a love affair, a disillusion, and a final accommodation. Franco Moretti, in *The Way of the World* (1987), read the form as a negotiation between youth and the social order, a way for a society to imagine how a new generation might be fitted to it, and he argued that the form declined when that negotiation lost its confidence. Feminist critics have shown that the female version differs. Susan J. Rosowski, in "The Novel of Awakening" (1979), argued that the heroine's story is often not a formation but an awakening to limits, since the society the hero is invited to join is closed to her. Not every such novel ends in reconciliation. Some are anti-bildungsromans in which the protagonist is broken rather than formed, and the failure is the point. The form, in other words, is not a neutral container; it carries a claim about whether a person can be made at all.

## The Victorian omniscient narrator

**Dickens.** — Charles Dickens built characters by external mark and repeated phrase, and his narrator is a performer. In *Bleak House* (1852–53) he split the narration between a first-person account by Esther Summerson and a present-tense omniscient voice of brooding authority, the voice that opens the book in the mud and the fog and the Chancery suit that will outlast everyone in it. In *David Copperfield* (1850) the first person looks back, and the mature narrator judges the boy he was, so that memory itself becomes a way of making character. Dickens's people are often flat in Forster's sense, and Forster thought them the proof that flatness can be a kind of genius.

**Eliot.** — George Eliot's narrator is a moral intelligence. *Middlemarch* (1871–72) moves among a whole provincial town, entering the minds of Dorothea, Lydgate, Rosamond, and Casaubon, and commenting from above. The narrator tells the reader that "character too is a process and an unfolding," and the novel enacts the claim: Tertius Lydgate, the gifted young doctor, is undone by small compromises and a bad marriage, and each step is traced. The image of the pier-glass, in which a housemaid's scratches appear as concentric circles around a candle, is a parable of egoism: every person sees events arranged around the self. Character, for Eliot, is not a fixed essence but a history, and it is inseparable from the society that shapes it. The omniscient narrator is what lets her show both at once — the inward life and the web of causes that surrounds it.

**The cost of omniscience.** — The Victorian narrator knows everything and says so, and this authority can feel like instruction. Henry James, in "The Art of Fiction" (1884) and in the prefaces he wrote between 1907 and 1909, argued for the "scenic" method, in which the decisive moments are rendered as scenes rather than summarised, and for a "central consciousness" through which the story is filtered. The reaction against telling is the bridge to the modern novel.

## The modern turn inward

**Woolf and the luminous halo.** — Virginia Woolf, in the essay "Modern Fiction" (1919; collected in *The Common Reader*, 1925), complained that the novelists of the preceding generation gave the reader a great deal of furniture and property and very little of the mind. Life, she wrote, is not a series of gig-lamps symmetrically arranged; it is a luminous halo, a semi-transparent envelope surrounding us from the beginning of consciousness to the end. The novelist's task, she argued, is to convey that halo. *Mrs. Dalloway* (1925) puts the argument into practice. Its action is a single day in June in London, and its method is a free indirect discourse that passes in and out of its characters' minds without a break, so that a memory of a girlhood at Bourton and the sound of Big Ben striking can occupy the same movement of prose. The inwardness is not an escape from the world: the shell-shocked veteran Septimus Warren Smith, whose mind the narration also enters, is a casualty of the same society Clarissa is preparing to celebrate at her party.

**Joyce and the stream of consciousness.** — James Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) carries the method further. The term "stream of consciousness" comes from the psychologist William James, who in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) described thought as a continuous stream rather than a chain of separate ideas. Joyce renders that stream in interior monologue: the unspoken words of Leopold Bloom and Stephen Dedalus, and, in the final episode, the unpunctuated soliloquy of Molly Bloom, which runs for tens of pages and ends with her repeated "Yes." The reader is placed inside a mind rather than told about it. The method is the extreme point of the novel's long movement toward the interior, and it raises the question of this unit in its sharpest form: if character is made of words, what is the difference between a mind and the prose that imitates it?

**The technique and its critics.** — Dorrit Cohn, in *Transparent Minds* (1978), distinguished three modes of presenting consciousness: psycho-narration, in which the narrator reports a character's thoughts; quoted monologue, in which the character speaks them; and narrated monologue, the free indirect mode in which the narrator's grammar carries the character's voice. The vocabulary is a reminder that interiority in fiction is a set of conventions, not a transparent window. Ann Banfield, in *Unspeakable Sentences* (1982), argued that free indirect discourse is a distinct linguistic form with its own grammar. The point is not that Woolf or Joyce failed to capture the mind; it is that the mind in a novel is always already made of language, chosen and arranged.

## What the novelist does

**The answer.** — A character is the sum of the devices a novelist uses. A name, which may be neutral or a verdict. A body and a face, described selectively. A manner of speech, with its class and its tics. A set of actions under pressure. The judgments of a narrator, whether omniscient, ironic, or withheld. The arrangement of scenes, so that decisive acts are shown and intervening years are summarised. And, above all, access to a mind, which the novel can grant and life cannot. The reader completes the construction, inferring a person from the evidence, and the illusion depends on consistency and on the reader's willingness to treat marks as a life. Change one device — replace the name, withhold the thought, let the narrator sneer — and the person changes.

**The unfinished debate.** — Whether characters are best understood as imagined people or as functions of a text remains open. Forster's flat and round, Watt's individualism, and Cohn's modes of consciousness are all arguments about the same thing: how words become a person. The question is worth carrying because it changes what the reader does. To read for character as truth is to ask whether a person is convincing; to read for character as construction is to ask how the effect was made. The strongest reading does both, and notices when the two answers pull apart.

- **1722** — Defoe's *Moll Flanders*.
- **1740** — Richardson's *Pamela*, the epistolary novel of the inner life.

- **1747–48** — Richardson's *Clarissa*.
- **1749** — Fielding's *Tom Jones*, with its ironic omniscient narrator.

- **1852–53** — Dickens's *Bleak House*.
- **1871–72** — Eliot's *Middlemarch*.

## Questions to carry

- Why does a reader treat a pattern of words as a person, and on what does that response depend?
- Forster's flat and round is a metaphor. What does it explain, and what does it leave out?
- Defoe's characters are known from the outside, Richardson's from letters, Joyce's from the unspoken stream. What does each method let a novelist do that the others cannot?
- The bildungsroman makes character a process. Can a novel show a person changing without the change feeling either arbitrary or inevitable?
- If the mind in a novel is always made of language, can any novel claim to show consciousness as it really is?
