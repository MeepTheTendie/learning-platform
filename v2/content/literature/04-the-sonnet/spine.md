# The sonnet

A sonnet is a poem of fourteen lines. That is almost all that the word itself guarantees. The line-length, the rhyme scheme, the arrangement of the parts, and even the metre have changed from language to language and century to century, and poets have disagreed about nearly every one of them. What has not changed is the number: fourteen, and with it the pressure the number exerts. A sonnet is short enough to be read in a single breath and long enough to hold a complete movement of thought. The form gives a poet roughly as much room as an argument needs to start, complicate itself, and turn. The question this unit follows is therefore not what a sonnet is but what its shortness does: how does a fourteen-line box force a poet to turn an argument?

**The fourteen-line box.** — The sonnet was invented in the thirteenth century at the court of the Hohenstaufen emperor Frederick II in Sicily, among poets writing in the vernacular Italian of the day. The usual credit goes to Giacomo da Lentini, a notary at that court, in the 1230s, though the attribution rests on later manuscript collections rather than on any contemporary statement. The early sonnet was a stanza: fourteen lines of eleven syllables each, the line that Italian prosody calls the *endecasillabo*, rhymed in a pattern and set to music or spoken aloud. Its name, *sonetto*, means a little sound or a little song — a diminutive of *suono*, sound — and the earliest examples were written to be accompanied. The form spread north from Sicily through Tuscany, and by the end of the thirteenth century it was the common property of Italian poets. Dante used it in the *Vita nuova*, embedding sonnets in a prose narrative about his love for Beatrice, and it was Dante's friend and admirer Petrarch who made the form the vehicle of a whole life's book.

**The Italian pattern and the turn.** — In its classic Italian shape the sonnet divides into two unequal halves. The first eight lines, the *octave*, rhyme *abba abba*: two quatrains whose rhymes interlock, so that the first line's sound does not return until the fourth and the fourth until the fifth. The remaining six lines, the *sestet*, may rhyme in several ways — *cdecde* is the commonest, and *cdcdcd* appears often — but the sestet does not repeat the octave's rhymes. The break between the two halves is the *volta*, the turn: a change of direction, of tone, or of argument that the rhyme scheme itself announces, because the ear has been waiting for a sound that never comes and is given new ones instead. The classic place for the turn is the first line of the sestet, the ninth line of the poem. The first eight lines pose something — a comparison, a problem, a state of feeling — and the last six answer, qualify, or reverse it. This two-part structure is the oldest and most durable idea in the form's history. The critic Michael R. G. Spiller, in *The Development of the Sonnet: An Introduction* (1992), treats that two-part argumentative shape, rather than any fixed rhyme scheme, as the sonnet's defining inheritance, and reads the form's later history as a long series of experiments in how far the shape can be stretched.

**The line and the metre.** — The sonnet's fourteen lines are not the same length in every language. The Italian line is the *endecasillabo*, a line of eleven syllables with a stress near the end, and it is a flexible instrument: the position of the main stresses can shift from line to line, which is one reason Italian sonnets can sound conversational. The English line is usually iambic pentameter — five stresses, ten syllables, the metre that Shakespeare and Milton used for blank verse as well — and its steadier beat gives the English sonnet a different music from the Italian. French sonnets were written in the twelve-syllable alexandrine; Spanish and Portuguese sonnets in eleven-syllable lines like the Italian. Because the form fixes the number of lines but not the length or the beat of them, it travelled easily and changed as it went. The metre is part of what a translator of a sonnet has to decide: to keep the rhyme, or the line-length, or the argument, and which of the three to sacrifice when they cannot all be kept.

**Petrarch and the book of fragments.** — Francesco Petrarca — Petrarch — was born in 1304 and died in 1374. On 6 April 1327, by his own account, he first saw a woman he called Laura in a church at Avignon, and for the rest of his life he wrote poems about her. He collected them into a single book, the *Rerum vulgarium fragmenta* — "fragments in the vernacular," often called the *Rime sparse* or the *Canzoniere*. It holds 366 poems, of which 317 are sonnets; the rest are *canzoni*, *sestine*, *ballate*, and madrigals. The book is arranged as a story: the poems of Laura alive give way to the poems of Laura dead, and the last poems turn from earthly love toward repentance and the Virgin. It is the first great sonnet sequence in a European language, and its influence on what followed is difficult to overstate. Petrarch's octave is usually a single long sentence or two, and his sestet often turns on a conceit — a comparison worked out in detail — that reframes everything the octave has said. His sonnet 1, which stands as the book's preface, asks the reader who has known love to pardon the poet's "scattered verse," and ends by calling the world's pleasure "a short, short dream." The poem's movement from plea in the octave to self-knowledge in the sestet is the pattern that English poets would spend two centuries imitating.

**Wyatt and Surrey bring the form into English.** — The sonnet reached England in the 1530s, carried by two courtiers who served Henry VIII. Sir Thomas Wyatt (1503–1542) had travelled in Italy and Spain and translated and imitated Petrarch directly. His "Whoso list to hunt" follows Petrarch's *Canzoniere* 190, in which a white hind wears a collar inscribed with a warning; Wyatt turns the allegory into a poem about the court and about a woman who belongs to a more powerful man — "Noli me tangere, for Caesar's I am." Wyatt's rhyme schemes are irregular by later standards; he sometimes ends with a couplet, sometimes not, and his lines carry the stress of English speech rather than the smoothness of Italian. Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey (1516/17–1547), went further. Translating Petrarch's *Canzoniere* 140, he arranged the poem as three quatrains and a final couplet — *abab cdcd efef gg* — and that arrangement became the English sonnet. Surrey was executed in 1547, at twenty-nine or thirty, but his poems and Wyatt's were printed ten years later by the stationer Richard Tottel in *Songes and Sonettes* (1557), known ever after as *Tottel's Miscellany*. It was the first printed anthology of English poetry, and it made the two men the founders of the English sonnet. The form they naturalised was not Petrarch's octave-and-sestet but a looser three-part structure, and the change mattered: it moved the poem's decisive turn from the ninth line to the final couplet.

**The English pattern.** — The English or Shakespearean sonnet rhymes *abab cdcd efef gg*: three quatrains, each with its own pair of alternating rhymes, closed by a couplet. In this shape the argument tends to develop in three stages, one per quatrain, and then to be summed up or reversed in the couplet. The couplet is the English sonnet's most distinctive feature and its most dangerous one: because it is set off by rhyme and usually by a pause, it can land with the force of an epigram, or it can sound like a moral tacked on at the end. Whether the couplet completes the poem or betrays it is one of the oldest arguments in English criticism of the form. The English pattern did not drive out the Italian. Sir Philip Sidney (1554–1586) wrote *Astrophil and Stella* largely in the Italian shape, with an octave and a sestet; Edmund Spenser (1552–1599) invented a third pattern for his *Amoretti*, linking the quatrains with rhymes that carry across the stanza break — *abab bcbc cdcd ee* — so that the poem seems to wind forward rather than to stop at each quatrain. The sixteenth century in England ended with at least three live sonnet patterns, not one.

**The Elizabethan sequence.** — The sonnet's great English fashion was as a sequence: a book of sonnets that tells a story of courtship, longing, jealousy, and loss. Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella*, written about 1582 and printed in 1591, was the first, with 108 sonnets and eleven songs, and it set the model that the 1590s followed. Its opening sonnet is itself about the difficulty of writing: the poet hunts for fine words, fails, and is told by his Muse to "look in thy heart, and write." Spenser's *Amoretti*, printed in 1595, records a courtship that ends in marriage, and its seventy-fifth sonnet answers the beloved's claim that writing her name in sand cannot make her live by insisting that verse will. Samuel Daniel's *Delia* (1592) and Michael Drayton's *Idea* (1594 and later) belong to the same decade. The sequences were not private diaries; they were shaped literary works, built out of Petrarchan conventions — the cruel mistress, the lover's fire and ice, the catalogue of the beloved's beauties — that poets competed to vary and outdo. The modern critic Thomas P. Roche Jr., in *Petrarch and the English Sonnet Sequences* (1989), argued that these books were designed as unified wholes rather than as strings of occasional poems, and that their order and framing carry meaning that the individual sonnets do not.

**Shakespeare's three quatrains and couplet.** — The 154 sonnets printed in 1609 under Shakespeare's name are the most famous in English and the most argued over. They fall into two groups: sonnets 1–126 address a young man, urging him to marry and to preserve his beauty; sonnets 127–152 address a woman, the so-called Dark Lady. Whether the poems are autobiographical, whether they are in the order Shakespeare intended, and whether the 1609 quarto was authorised are all unsettled questions. What is not in doubt is the verbal pressure the poems put on the English form. Sonnet 18 — "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" — spends twelve lines conceding that everything fair declines and then, in the couplet, claims that the beloved will live as long as the poem does: "So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee." Sonnet 116 makes love an "ever-fixed mark" that does not alter, and stakes the poem's own truth on the claim in its couplet. Sonnet 130 opens by denying every conventional comparison — "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun" — and turns in the couplet to say that the beloved is as rare "As any she belied with false compare," which is both a compliment and an attack on the whole Petrarchan catalogue the poem has just refused. The critic Stephen Booth, in *An Essay on Shakespeare's Sonnets* (1969) and in his edition of the sonnets (1977), argued that much of the poems' power lies in the way they make a reader feel the form as a set of expectations — the expected end of a quatrain, the expected completion of a rhyme — and then satisfy or frustrate those expectations, so that the meaning is produced in the reader's experience of the shape. Helen Vendler, in *The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets* (1997), reads each poem as a self-contained "miniature drama" and draws attention to what she calls "couplet ties": words and images that appear in the couplet and also earlier in the poem, binding the last two lines to the body of the sonnet rather than leaving them as a detached summary. Booth and Vendler disagree about much — Booth emphasises the instability of the poems' language, Vendler their designed coherence — but both treat the fourteen-line arrangement as the engine of the meaning, not as a container for it.

**Donne and the metaphysical turn.** — John Donne (1572–1631) wrote his *Holy Sonnets* around 1609–1611, while he was still hesitating between public office and the priesthood he finally entered in 1615; they were printed after his death, in *Poems* (1633). Donne keeps the Italian two-part structure, but he fills it with the direct address and the strained paradox that his age called "metaphysical." "Death be not proud" speaks to Death as a person and argues with it, using the logic of a lawyer: if rest and sleep, which are Death's pictures, give pleasure, then Death itself must give more; Death is not a king but a "slave to Fate, Chance, kings, and desperate men"; and finally Death will itself die. The volta in Donne is often a turn in the argument's logic rather than a change of scene, and the last line is a paradox that closes the poem like a door. Donne also wrote sonnets and near-sonnets of farewell and of religious fear, and the critic Helen Gardner, in her edition *John Donne: The Divine Poems* (1952, revised 1978), argued that the *Holy Sonnets* are carefully ordered meditations rather than occasional outbursts, though later editors have questioned how far any single order is authorial.

**Milton and the sonnet of conscience.** — John Milton (1608–1674) wrote fewer than two dozen sonnets, mostly in the 1640s and 1650s, and he wrote them in the Italian form, with an octave and a sestet. He used the sonnet for subjects that English poets had rarely brought to it: his own blindness, the death of a friend's wife, the massacre of Protestants in Piedmont, the duties of a public man. "When I consider how my light is spent," probably written about 1652, after Milton had lost his sight, asks whether God expects work from a man who cannot see, and answers itself through the voice of Patience: "They also serve who only stand and wait." The turn falls at the sestet, and the sestet is itself a reply — the poem stages an argument and then lets another voice resolve it. Milton's example mattered to the Romantics, who saw in him a poet who had put the sonnet to public and moral use. In 1802 Wordsworth wrote, "Milton! thou shouldst be living at this hour: / England hath need of thee," and the sonnet to Milton became a small genre of its own.

**The Romantic sonnet.** — The sonnet revived at the end of the eighteenth century after a period of neglect, and William Wordsworth (1770–1850) wrote more of them than any English poet before or since — some 523. He used the Miltonic Italian form, and he used it for politics, landscape, and the ordinary world. "Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802" describes London from a coach at dawn, when the city is "silent, bare" and the river glides "at his own sweet will." The octave presents the sight, and the sestet claims that the calm is deeper than any the poet has felt in the country — a reversal of the usual Romantic preference for nature over the city. John Keats (1795–1821) wrote "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer" in October 1816, after a night of reading George Chapman's translation of Homer with his friend Charles Cowden Clarke, and published it in his *Poems* of 1817. The octave describes the realms of gold the poet has travelled and the one wide expanse he had only heard of; the sestet breaks into two similes, an astronomer watching a new planet swim into view and Cortez silent on a peak in Darien. (Keats names Cortez; the first European to see the Pacific from the isthmus was Vasco Núñez de Balboa, and the error has been discussed ever since.) The poem's turn is the moment of discovery, and the form lets Keats delay it until the eighth line has closed. The sonnet was also the form in which Wordsworth and Keats conducted some of their thinking about poetry itself; the critic William J. Kennedy, in *Authorizing Petrarch* (1994), has argued that the Renaissance English poets who took up the form were consciously "authorising" themselves through Petrarch, and that later poets read the tradition through those English intermediaries as much as through the Italian.

**The Victorian sonnet.** — The nineteenth century kept the sonnet and made it, once more, the instrument of a sequence. Elizabeth Barrett Browning (1806–1861) wrote the forty-four *Sonnets from the Portuguese* during her courtship by Robert Browning in 1845–1846; they were published in 1850, and their title disguises them as translations to protect their privacy. Sonnet 43 — "How do I love thee? Let me count the ways" — makes the counting of ways the poem's action, and the sestet turns from the present to the future: "and, if God choose, / I shall but love thee better after death." The sequence is a courtship narrative with a happy ending, and it gave the form a Victorian popularity it had not had since the 1590s. George Meredith's *Modern Love* (1862) used fifty sixteen-line sonnets to tell the opposite story, the breakdown of a marriage, and its stretched form — the sixteen-line "sonnet" — was itself an argument about how far the box could be enlarged. Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Christina Rossetti also wrote sonnets and sonnet sequences; Christina Rossetti's "Monna Innominata" (1881) answers the Petrarchan tradition from a woman's point of view.

**Hopkins and sprung rhythm.** — Gerard Manley Hopkins (1844–1889) was a Jesuit priest who published almost nothing in his lifetime; his *Poems* appeared in 1918, edited by his friend Robert Bridges, nearly thirty years after his death. Hopkins invented what he called "sprung rhythm," a metre in which the poet counts stressed syllables rather than total syllables, and allows any number of unstressed syllables between the stresses. The effect is a line that seems to be spoken with sudden weight and hurry at once, and it changes what a sonnet line can do. "God's Grandeur," written in 1877, is a sonnet in sprung rhythm: the octave sets the world's charge of divine grandeur against the trodden, smeared, shoeless world of men, and the sestet turns on the word "And" to claim that nature is "never spent," because the Holy Ghost broods over the bent world. Hopkins also compressed the form: his "curtal sonnets," such as "Pied Beauty," run to ten and a half lines, a scaled-down sonnet that keeps the two-part turn. His poetry was read as a new thing in 1918, and the critic and poet who introduced it presented him as a modern before modernism; later criticism, including studies of his prosody, has argued about how far sprung rhythm is a system and how far it is a set of notations for reading aloud.

**What the box does.** — The sonnet's history is a history of poets answering the same constraint in different ways. The Italian form gives the turn a fixed place at the ninth line and makes the sestet a reply. The English form moves the turn to the final couplet and makes the poem a three-step approach to a summary. Spenser's linked quatrains blur the steps; Milton restores the Italian halves and fills them with conscience; Donne argues inside the halves; Hopkins keeps the halves but changes the line. The fourteen lines do not dictate the argument, but they do ration it. A poet who wants to say something in a sonnet must decide what belongs in the first part and what the turn will do to it, and that decision is the form. The organizing question — how does a fourteen-line box force a poet to turn an argument? — has no single answer, because the box has been built differently in different hands. What the examples in this unit show is that the turn is not an ornament added to the poem; it is the reason the poem is fourteen lines long.

## Chronology

- **c. 1230s** — Giacomo da Lentini and the poets of Frederick II's Sicilian court devise the fourteen-line sonnet.
- **c. 1290s** — Dante uses the sonnet in the *Vita nuova*, embedding it in prose.
- **1327** — Petrarch first sees Laura, by his own account, on 6 April.
- **c. 1327–1368** — Petrarch writes and gathers the *Rime sparse* (*Canzoniere*), 366 poems, 317 of them sonnets.
- **1348** — Laura dies; the sequence turns from love to mourning and repentance.
- **1530s–1540s** — Wyatt and Surrey translate and imitate Petrarch in English.
- **1547** — Surrey is executed; he leaves the *abab cdcd efef gg* pattern.
- **1557** — *Tottel's Miscellany* prints Wyatt and Surrey, the first English poetry anthology.
- **1582** — Sidney writes *Astrophil and Stella*; it is printed in 1591.
- **1595** — Spenser's *Amoretti* is printed.
- **1609** — Shakespeare's *Sonnets* (154 poems) are printed.
- **c. 1609–1611** — Donne writes the *Holy Sonnets*; they are printed in 1633.
- **c. 1652** — Milton writes "When I consider how my light is spent"; printed 1673.
- **1802** — Wordsworth writes "Composed upon Westminster Bridge," printed 1807.
- **1816** — Keats writes "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer," printed 1817.
- **1850** — Elizabeth Barrett Browning's *Sonnets from the Portuguese* are printed.
- **1862** — Meredith's *Modern Love*, fifty sixteen-line sonnets, is printed.
- **1877** — Hopkins writes "God's Grandeur."
- **1918** — Hopkins's *Poems* is printed, edited by Robert Bridges.

## Questions to carry

- Where does the turn fall in the Italian sonnet, and what happens to it in the English form?
- What does a poet gain, and what does a poet give up, by ending a sonnet with a couplet?
- Sidney, Spenser, and Shakespeare each built a different kind of sequence. What does a sequence add that a single sonnet cannot?
- Donne and Milton both keep the Italian octave and sestet. How do they use it differently?
- Hopkins counts stresses instead of syllables. Does a sonnet stop being a sonnet when its metre changes?
