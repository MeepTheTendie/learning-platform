# Drama and Shakespeare

**Period:** English drama, sixteenth to seventeenth centuries

**A script is not a play.** — Read *Macbeth* alone and the witches are three speeches on a page. Stand in a playhouse yard in 1606, daylight overhead and three thousand people packed around a bare platform, and the same words become bodies, voices, and a crowd's held breath. A play is written for performance, and performance adds what the page cannot carry: an actor's face, the pause before a line, the fact that an audience is present and can see the actor and be seen by him. The organizing question of this unit is how a play means something different when it is spoken on a stage. The difference is not only volume or colour. A stage makes meaning by means the page lacks — by casting, by the order in which the audience learns things, by the shared event of a crowd watching the same action at once. Three documents follow: scenes from *Macbeth* and *Hamlet*, and Aristotle's *Poetics*, the fourth-century BCE treatise that supplied European drama with its vocabulary for tragedy. The question is what the playhouse did to those words.

## The playhouse

**The Globe.** — The most famous of the London playhouses stood on the south bank of the Thames at Bankside, in Southwark, outside the city walls. It was built in 1599 by the Lord Chamberlain's Men, the company in which Shakespeare was a shareholder, using timber carried across the river from an older playhouse, the Theatre, in Shoreditch. The Globe was an open-air amphitheatre, probably polygonal with some twenty sides and perhaps thirty metres across, holding between two and three thousand people — the figures are estimates and are disputed. The yard was open to the sky, and the stage was a platform that thrust out into it, so the audience stood on three sides of the actors. A spectator paid a penny to stand in the yard, more to sit in a gallery. There was no painted scenery and no curtain in the modern sense; a play established its place by words and costume, not by a set. Above the stage a roof painted with the sun and stars, the "heavens," sheltered the actors; below it a trap door led under the stage. At the back, doors in the tiring-house wall gave the actors their entrances.

**Daylight, and the shape of the audience.** — Performances began in the afternoon, around two o'clock, and lasted about two hours; the Chorus in *Romeo and Juliet* calls the play "the two hours' traffic of our stage." Because the Globe was open to the day, the audience and the actors could see one another, and the playwright could count on that. The crowd in the yard — the "groundlings," as Hamlet calls them — was a cross-section of London: apprentices and labourers, servants and sailors, merchants and law students. The audience was not silent or passive; it talked, ate, hissed, applauded, and threw things when displeased. A play was written for a specific, visible, and volatile gathering, and the playwright knew its tastes: ghosts and battles, clowns and murders, rhetorical speeches and quick jokes.

**The indoor playhouse and the company.** — Not every playhouse was open to the sky. In 1608 Shakespeare's company, by then the King's Men under James I, acquired the Blackfriars, an indoor hall theatre on the north bank. It was smaller, roofed, lit by candles, and charged higher prices, so its audience was richer and more select; its acoustics favoured music and a quieter, more intimate style. The company was a business. Its leading men were the "sharers," who owned the costumes and the playbooks and split the profits; among them were the actor Richard Burbage, the company's great tragic lead, and Shakespeare himself, who acted as well as wrote. Beneath the sharers were hired men who played small parts, and boys apprenticed to the company who played the women's roles, since women were not allowed on the public stage in England until the Restoration of 1660. A playwright wrote for particular actors, a particular stage, and a particular crowd, and the text we read is the residue of that collaboration.

## Verse, prose, and the speaking voice

**Blank verse as the medium.** — The plays are mostly written in blank verse, unrhymed iambic pentameter, the line that Christopher Marlowe made the instrument of the public stage in the late 1580s. Ben Jonson, in his poem prefixed to the First Folio, called it "Marlowe's mighty line." Blank verse was the default for noble characters, serious action, and heightened feeling; it was close enough to speech to seem natural and formal enough to be heard as verse. Shakespeare bends it constantly — running the sense past the line break, splitting a line between two speakers, adding an extra unstressed syllable at the end, letting a thought collapse into a short exclamation. In the dagger soliloquy the meter turns nervous as the imagined weapon becomes "a dagger of the mind"; in *Hamlet* the pauses in "To be, or not to be" fall where the thought hesitates.

**Why the prose.** — Not everything is in verse, and the switch is a signal. Shakespeare gives prose to clowns and servants, to letters read aloud, to comic and low-life scenes, to madness, and to informal or hurried talk. In *Macbeth*, the Porter who answers the knocking after Duncan's murder speaks prose, and his drunken jokes about an "equivocator" drop the audience into the ordinary world before the verse of discovery resumes; the same play ends Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking in broken prose as her control dissolves. In *Hamlet*, the prince speaks verse when he is alone and prose when he puts on his "antic disposition," so the medium itself tells the audience when he is performing madness and when he is not. A reader who does not notice the change misses a stage direction written into the language.

**The soliloquy.** — The soliloquy is the convention by which a character alone on stage speaks his thoughts aloud. It is not realistic — nobody talks to an empty room in perfect pentameter — and the audience accepts it as a licence, a window into a mind. The device makes interiority audible: Hamlet weighing suicide, Macbeth seeing the dagger, Lady Macbeth reading her husband's letter. The aside is its smaller cousin: a remark spoken to the audience that the other characters are understood not to hear. When Macbeth says "[Aside.] This supernatural soliciting / Cannot be ill; cannot be good," he is speaking to the yard, not to Banquo, and the audience is let into a secret the other characters lack.

## Tragedy, comedy, and the tragic hero

**Tragedy and comedy.** — English drama inherited its two great genres from classical and medieval tradition. Comedy shows people worse than they are and ends in reconciliation, often a marriage; tragedy shows people better than they are and ends in death and waste. The First Folio of 1623 sorted Shakespeare's plays into Comedies, Histories, and Tragedies, and those labels still shape how the plays are read and staged. They are also imperfect. *The Merchant of Venice* is a comedy that turns on a Jew's forced conversion and a pound of flesh; *Troilus and Cressida* fits none of the three; the late plays *The Winter's Tale* and *The Tempest* end in reunion and forgiveness but carry a weight the word comedy does not quite hold. The categories are a convenience of the book trade, not a law of the drama.

**Aristotle on tragedy.** — The *Poetics*, composed in the fourth century BCE and known in Renaissance Europe through Latin and Italian translations, defines tragedy as "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude ... through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions." The plot, Aristotle says, is "the first principle, and, as it were, the soul of a tragedy," and character comes second. The change of fortune should be from good to bad, and it should come about "not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty" — the Greek word is *hamartia*, often translated "error," sometimes misleadingly as "tragic flaw." The ideal hero is neither saint nor villain but a person of high standing, "like Oedipus," whose fall arouses pity because it is undeserved and fear because the audience recognises the same liability in itself.

**The contested machinery.** — Almost every term in that summary is disputed. *Catharsis*, the "purgation" of pity and fear, has been read as a medical purging, a moral clarification, and a purely aesthetic relief, and no reading commands agreement. *Hamartia* may be a moral fault, an intellectual mistake, or simply the ignorance in which Oedipus kills his father; the difference decides whether tragedy is about character or about circumstance. Aristotle also insists, in chapter 14, that pity and fear are better produced by the structure of the plot than by spectacle — "even without the aid of the eye, he who hears the tale told will thrill with horror" — an argument for the primacy of the written action over the staged effect. His ranking of plot above character was later reversed by criticism that read the tragedies as portraits of individuals. The *Poetics* is not a rulebook Shakespeare followed; it is a vocabulary later readers used to describe him.

**The hero and the critics.** — The most influential English account of the tragic hero is A. C. Bradley's *Shakespearean Tragedy* (1904), which treats *Hamlet*, *Othello*, *King Lear*, and *Macbeth* as studies in character and reads each catastrophe as the outworking of the hero's moral nature. Bradley's method was attacked in the twentieth century. L. C. Knights, in an essay whose title mocked the habit — "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?" (1933) — argued that treating characters as real people distracts from the words on the page. Jan Kott, in *Shakespeare Our Contemporary* (1964), read the histories and tragedies as a "Grand Mechanism," a staircase of power on which each ruler repeats the crimes of the last. Stephen Greenblatt, in *Shakespearean Negotiations* (1988), argued that the theatre was a place where the period's social and religious conflicts were staged and negotiated. These are arguments, not findings, and they disagree about where a play's meaning lives: in a character, in a structure, or in the society that produced it.

## Ambition in *Macbeth*

**The source, and what Shakespeare did to it.** — Shakespeare rarely invented a plot. For *Macbeth*, written about 1606, his main source was Raphael Holinshed's *Chronicles of England, Scotland and Ireland* (1577; reprinted 1587). Holinshed tells of a Scottish thane who meets three women "in strange and wild apparel," hears a prophecy that he will be king, and murders Duncan with his wife's help. Shakespeare kept the outline and changed it wherever it mattered. In Holinshed, Banquo is Macbeth's accomplice; on Shakespeare's stage he is innocent, because James I traced his ancestry to Banquo. Holinshed's Duncan is young and weak; Shakespeare makes him old and gracious, so the murder is more monstrous. The dagger, the sleepwalking scene, the Porter, and much of the witches' language are Shakespeare's invention.

**The prophecy and the equivocation.** — The witches open with a paradox — "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" — and greet Macbeth with titles he does not yet hold: Thane of Glamis, Thane of Cawdor, "king hereafter." When the first two come true, Macbeth must decide about the third. Shakespeare gives him asides in which the audience hears the murder forming before he admits it to anyone: "My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical, / Shakes so my single state of man / That function is smother'd in surmise, / And nothing is but what is not." The witches are "imperfect speakers," and their promises are later shown to be equivocations — true in a sense no one expects. The word equivocation was loaded in 1606, when it was tied to the trial of the Jesuit Henry Garnet after the Gunpowder Plot of 1605; a play about a king murdered by a usurper who trusts a prophecy spoke to the anxieties of the court.

**Lady Macbeth and the marriage.** — Lady Macbeth supplies the resolve her husband lacks. Reading his letter, she fears his nature is "too full o' th' milk of human kindness" to take "the nearest way," and calls on the spirits to "unsex me here." When Macbeth hesitates in Act I, scene 7, she steadies him, taunting his manhood and promising to drug Duncan's guards. The marriage is a partnership in ambition and in crime. Her collapse is staged as the loss of that control: in Act V she walks in her sleep, tries to wash imagined blood from her hands, and speaks in prose, and the woman who once called for darkness now keeps a candle by her.

**"Vaulting ambition."** — Macbeth names his own motive before the murder: he has "no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on th' other." The image is a rider who jumps too far and lands on the wrong side of the horse — ambition as a self-defeating leap. But the play does not make ambition the whole story: Macbeth also acts from fear, from his wife's pressure, and from a half-belief in fate, and once he has killed he kills again to protect what he has won. Ambition is the first cause, not the only one.

**The dagger and the theatre of the mind.** — The dagger soliloquy is the play's great experiment in staging a mind. Macbeth asks whether the weapon is "sensible / To feeling as to sight," concludes it is "a dagger of the mind, a false creation, / Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain," and yet still sees it: "I see thee still." The audience must decide what it sees. Is the dagger visible to the theatre as well as to Macbeth — a prop, a supernatural object, a hallucination made flesh by the actor — or is the stage bare and the vision only in the words? Different productions answer differently, and the answer changes the scene. The page cannot say whether the dagger is there; the performance must.

**"Tomorrow, and tomorrow."** — When Macbeth hears that his queen is dead, he answers with the most quoted speech in the play: "She should have died hereafter. / There would have been a time for such a word. / Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow..." Life becomes "a walking shadow," a "poor player / That struts and frets his hour upon the stage, / And then is heard no more," and finally "a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing." The theatre metaphor inside a play — an actor comparing life to an actor — is often read as Shakespeare's own meditation on performance. That reading needs care: the words belong to Macbeth at his lowest point and express his nihilism, not necessarily the playwright's, and a production can play the speech as grief, exhaustion, or defiance.

## Delay in *Hamlet*

**The source and the additions.** — *Hamlet* was written about 1600–1601. Its ultimate source is the legend of Amleth in the *Gesta Danorum* of the Danish historian Saxo Grammaticus (c. 1200), retold in French by François de Belleforest in 1570. An earlier English play on the subject, the lost *Ur-Hamlet*, was on the London stage by 1589 and is often attributed to Thomas Kyd, author of *The Spanish Tragedy*. In Saxo, Amleth feigns madness and takes his revenge; there is no ghost and little delay. Shakespeare's additions are the ones that matter: the ghost that commands revenge, the prince's long hesitation, the play-within-the-play, the graveyard, the soliloquies, and an ending in which nearly everyone dies.

**The ghost and the command.** — In Act I, scene 5, the ghost of Hamlet's father says he was murdered by Claudius, who poured poison into his ear, and commands: "Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder." Hamlet answers, "Haste me to know't, that I, with wings as swift / As meditation or the thoughts of love, / May sweep to my revenge." The haste is the point: he promises speed and does not deliver it. The ghost raises a question the play never settles. Is it his father's spirit, or a devil assuming "a pleasing shape" to damn him? The dead king says he is doomed for a time to walk the night, which implies a purgatory Protestant England had officially abolished. The audience must weigh testimony it cannot verify — a problem performance makes physical, since the ghost may be visible to the audience and invisible on stage.

**The delay.** — Why Hamlet delays is the oldest question in Shakespeare criticism, and there is no agreed answer. Bradley read it as the product of melancholy and a moral scruple that unfits him for a bloody act. Others argue that the delay is a convention of the revenge play, which needs five acts of postponement, or that the play is less about revenge than about uncertainty and conscience. Hamlet cannot explain it himself: he calls himself "a rogue and peasant slave," "pigeon-liver'd," and asks, "Am I a coward?" The play keeps the audience in the same uncertainty as the prince, and that shared uncertainty is what makes the soliloquies work.

**The play-within-the-play.** — Hamlet's scheme to test the ghost's word is the clearest piece of stagecraft in the play. He arranges for the visiting players to perform *The Murder of Gonzago*, with a speech of his own added, and tells the audience why: "I'll have these players / Play something like the murder of my father / Before mine uncle. I'll observe his looks ... The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King." In performance this is a scene of an audience watching an audience: the Globe's crowd watches Claudius watch a murder, and the dramatic irony is doubled, because the theatre knows the play is a trap while Claudius does not. The device also lets Shakespeare comment on his own art: a play is a machine for making people reveal themselves.

**Claudius at prayer.** — The most important delay is also the most theatrical. In Act III, scene 3, Claudius, alone, tries to pray and fails: "O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven ... Pray can I not." Hamlet comes upon him, draws his sword, and decides not to kill him, because to kill a man at prayer would send his soul to heaven: "Then trip him, that his heels may kick at heaven, / And that his soul may be as damn'd and black / As hell, whereto it goes." Hamlet leaves, and Claudius rises: "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below. / Words without thoughts never to heaven go." The audience has watched a perfect piece of dramatic irony: Hamlet spares Claudius on a false assumption, and knows it is false from Claudius's own soliloquy. Hamlet's delay is not merely a failure to act; it is a failure to understand, and the stage is what lets the audience see the gap.

**"The readiness is all."** — By Act V Hamlet has changed. In the graveyard he holds Yorick's skull and confronts mortality; before the fatal duel he tells Horatio, "There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow ... The readiness is all." The line is often read as a submission to providence. It is also the last delay: he stops trying to control the outcome and walks into the trap Claudius has set. The play that began with a ghost demanding haste ends with a prince who has stopped hurrying.

## The audience, and how a play means

**Dramatic irony.** — Dramatic irony is the audience's knowledge of what a character does not know, and the Elizabethan playhouse was built to produce it. The soliloquy and the aside let the audience into a mind; the visible stage lets it see two characters at once, each ignorant of the other's position. *Romeo and Juliet* gives the audience the news that Juliet is drugged and lets Romeo die believing she is dead. *Macbeth* lets the audience hear the witches' promises and watch Macbeth trust them. *Hamlet* lets the audience hear Claudius pray and watch Hamlet spare him for the wrong reason. In each case the meaning is not in the words alone but in the arrangement of knowledge across the stage.

**The audience's work.** — Shakespeare's theatre asks the audience to supply what it cannot show. The Chorus that opens *Henry V* apologises for the stage and begs the crowd to help: "Can this cockpit hold / The vasty fields of France? or may we cram / Within this wooden O the very casques / That did affright the air at Agincourt?" and then, "Piece out our imperfections with your thoughts." The "wooden O" is the Globe itself, and the speech describes how the theatre makes meaning: a few actors and a bare platform become a battlefield because the audience agrees to imagine it. The same principle governs the dagger that may or may not be there and the ghost that only some characters see. A play is a transaction between a script, a company, and a crowd, and the crowd does part of the work.

**Every performance is an interpretation.** — A printed play underdetermines its performance. It does not say how old the actors are or how the lines are spoken; those choices are made anew in every production, and they change the meaning. A young Hamlet makes the delay a coming-of-age; an older one makes it exhaustion. A Macbeth played as a soldier undone by ambition differs from one played as a man already hollowed out. The text is stable; the performance is not, and that instability is not a defect but the medium. Kott read the plays through the politics of postwar Europe, and Greenblatt through the religious conflicts of the Reformation. The play on the page is the same play; what it means depends on who is speaking it, and to whom.

## Chronology

- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle composes the *Poetics*; tragedy, hamartia, and catharsis get their defining terms.
- **c. 1200** — Saxo Grammaticus writes the legend of Amleth in the *Gesta Danorum*.
- **1498; 1508** — The *Poetics* is translated into Latin; it is printed in Greek.
- **1570** — François de Belleforest retells the Amleth story in the *Histoires tragiques*.
- **1576** — James Burbage builds the Theatre in Shoreditch, the first purpose-built playhouse in England.
- **1577; 1587** — Raphael Holinshed's *Chronicles* appear; they are Shakespeare's main source for *Macbeth*.
- **c. 1587** — Marlowe's *Tamburlaine* establishes blank verse on the public stage; Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy* founds the revenge play.
- **1589** — The lost *Ur-Hamlet* is on the London stage.
- **1594** — The Lord Chamberlain's Men are formed; Shakespeare is a sharer.
- **1599** — The Globe opens at Bankside, built with the Theatre's timber.
- **c. 1600–1601** — Shakespeare writes *Hamlet*.
- **1603** — James I succeeds Elizabeth; the company becomes the King's Men.
- **5 November 1605** — The Gunpowder Plot; the trial of Henry Garnet and the theme of equivocation follow.
- **c. 1606** — Shakespeare writes *Macbeth*, probably performed at court.
- **1608** — The King's Men acquire the indoor Blackfriars theatre.
- **29 June 1613** — The Globe burns down during *Henry VIII*; it is rebuilt in 1614.
- **1616** — Shakespeare dies at Stratford-upon-Avon.
- **1623** — The First Folio collects the plays as Comedies, Histories, and Tragedies.
- **1642; 1660** — Parliament closes the theatres; they reopen at the Restoration, after which women appear on the English public stage.
- **1904** — A. C. Bradley publishes *Shakespearean Tragedy*.
- **1933** — L. C. Knights publishes "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?"
- **1964** — Jan Kott publishes *Shakespeare Our Contemporary*.
- **1988** — Stephen Greenblatt publishes *Shakespearean Negotiations*.
- **1997** — The reconstructed Globe opens on Bankside.

## Questions to carry

- What does a stage let a playwright do that a page cannot, and what does the page let a reader see that a performance hides?
- If the *Poetics* was written centuries before Shakespeare and describes Greek plays, what is gained and what is lost by using its terms on him?
- Hamlet's delay has been explained as melancholy, as conscience, as a plot convention, and as the play's real subject. Which explanation best fits the text, and what evidence would settle it?
- Shakespeare's *Macbeth* alters Holinshed at almost every point. What do those changes reveal about what a play is for?
- The same script yields different plays in different productions. Does that make the text's meaning unstable, or does it show where the text's meaning actually lives?
