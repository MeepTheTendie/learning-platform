# How to write a history lesson

Derived from the assigned texts and lectures of seven Yale history courses:
HIST 210 (Freedman), 202 and 276 (Merriman), 116 (Freeman), 119 (Blight),
234 (Snowden), 251 (Wrightson). The point is to write history, not to preface it.

## The model: three tiers, kept distinct

Every serious course pairs three kinds of reading. Our lessons should too.

1. **A synthesis** that carries the narrative — Merriman's *A History of Modern
   Europe*, Wickham's *The Inheritance of Rome*, Foner's *A Short History of
   Reconstruction*. This is the spine.
2. **Primary documents** that carry the evidence — *The Federalist Papers*,
   Paine's *Common Sense*, Douglass's *Narrative*, the Burgundian Code. These are
   quoted, not paraphrased.
3. **A named interpretation** — a monograph or article that argues a case:
   Gross's *The Minutemen and Their World*, Browning's *Ordinary Men*, Wood's
   *Radicalism of the American Revolution*. This shows the reader that history is
   an argument, not a list.

The synthesis is interpretation; the documents are evidence. Never let the reader
confuse the two.

## Rules

1. **Open with the subject, not a preface.** Freedman begins with the crisis of
   the empire. No "by the end of this you will be able to."
2. **State the organizing question, then return to it.** "Why does an empire that
   endures for centuries collapse when it does?"
3. **Teach the debate; name the historians.** "Gibbon said X; we no longer accept
   that." Freeman sets Bailyn against Wood against Raphael. A lesson with no
   named disagreement is probably telling only one side.
4. **Mark interpretation as interpretation.** "Historians debate this. I have an
   opinion, but it is not settled."
5. **Explain an abstraction with one concrete case.** Merriman teaches the
   culture of imperialism through the Boy Scouts. The Danube official who misses
   his olives and wine explains the limits of Roman civilization.
6. **Keep contingency.** Outcomes were not inevitable. Freeman's fifth rule:
   anything could have happened. Do not write the past as if it led here on rails.
7. **Watch for anachronism; words change meaning.** "Democracy" did not mean in
   1776 what it means now. Give the period's own sense of a word before using it.
8. **People, not monuments; not only the famous.** The Founders were human, and
   they were not the only actors. Look for the ordinary people in the record.
9. **Density through structure.** Run-in topic headings, a clean chronology, and
   sentences that each carry a fact, a cause, or a consequence. Nothing padded.
10. **The sources do the talking.** Where a document exists, quote it and ask what
    it assumes, rather than summarizing it away.

## Anti-patterns (these fail the framing lint)

- Learning objectives, "you will be able to," course prefaces.
- Present-day moral verdicts, "global citizenship," "cultural competence."
- Modern buzzwords applied to the past.
- Asserting a contested interpretation as fact.
- Writing the outcome as inevitable.
