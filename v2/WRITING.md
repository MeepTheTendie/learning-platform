# How to write a history lesson

Derived from reading the assigned texts and a full lecture of Yale HIST 210,
*The Early Middle Ages* (Paul Freedman), and from the older textbooks (Myers).
The point is to write history, not to preface it.

## The model

A serious history course pairs two things and keeps them distinct:

1. **A modern synthesis** that carries the narrative — e.g. Chris Wickham's
   *The Inheritance of Rome* on Freedman's syllabus.
2. **Primary sources in translation** that carry the evidence — Einhard,
   Gregory of Tours, Procopius, the Burgundian Code, on the same syllabus.

The synthesis is *interpretation*; the sources are *evidence*. A lesson must
never let the reader confuse the two.

## Rules

1. **Open with the subject, not a preface.** Freedman begins with the crisis of
   the empire and its themes. No "by the end of this you will be able to."
2. **State the question, then return to it.** "Why does an empire that endures
   for centuries collapse when it does?" The question organizes the material.
3. **Name the historians and the debate.** "Gibbon said X; we no longer accept
   that, for reasons Y." Show the field as an argument, not a settled list.
4. **Mark interpretation as interpretation.** "Historians debate this. I have an
   opinion, but it is not settled."
5. **Density through structure.** Run-in topic headings, a clean chronology, and
   sentences that each carry a fact, a cause, or a consequence. Nothing padded.
6. **Signpost.** Tell the reader what comes next and why.
7. **Use concrete detail.** The Danube official who complains there are no olives
   and no wine; an army that discovers it can make and unmake emperors. Detail
   is what makes the past legible.
8. **Analogies explain, they do not moralize.** A comparison to something the
   reader knows is allowed when it clarifies a mechanism — never to deliver a
   present-day verdict on the past.
9. **Keep the period's own words and names.** Shumir, Accad, Chaldæa, *mare
   nostrum* — do not smooth the past into modern vocabulary.
10. **The sources do the talking.** Where a document exists, quote it and ask
    what it assumes, rather than paraphrasing it into a summary.

## Anti-patterns (these fail the framing lint)

- Learning objectives, "you will be able to," course prefaces.
- Present-day moral verdicts, "global citizenship," "cultural competence."
- Modern buzzwords applied to the past.
- Asserting a contested interpretation as fact.
